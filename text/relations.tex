\section{Relations}\label{sec:relations}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:cartesian_product_definition}
  In the literature, with slight variations, there is an established definition for finite Cartesian products, which are used to define functions, which in turn are used to define more general Cartesian products. Both definitions satisfy \fullref{thm:cartesian_product_universal_property}, so they are interchangeable in practice, however the formal development requires us to be careful with existence proofs and possible cyclic definitions.

  Another established practice discussed in \cref{rem:function_definition} is to formally identify functions with their graphs, which renders a lot of concepts like surjectivity meaningless.

  We approach both issues simultaneously:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item We start by introducing Kuratowski pairs in \cref{def:kuratowski_pair}.

    The authors we cite start similarly, but all continue in a different way.

    \item We introduce indexed families in \cref{def:indexed_family} as sets of Kuratowski pairs with a uniqueness constraint --- what most authors we cite call a function, and what \cite[def. I.3.1.1]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets} call a \enquote{graph} (\enquote{\textfr{graphe}} in French).

    After we prove \cref{thm:indexed_family_extensionality}, we will be free to interchange Kuratowski pairs with \hyperref[def:ordered_tuple]{ordered pairs} defined as \( 2 \)-indexed families.

    \item We use indexed families to introduce general Cartesian products in \cref{def:cartesian_product}.

    In \cref{def:ordered_tuple} and \cref{def:sequence}, we will introduce ordered tuples and sequences as special types of indexed families.

    \item We then use Cartesian products to introduce relations in \cref{def:relation}, and proceed to develop the theory of functions in \fullref{sec:functions} with a completely general notion of ordered tuple and Cartesian product.

    After we prove \cref{thm:cartesian_product_via_choice_functions}, we will be free to interchange ordered pairs with \hyperref[def:choice_function]{choice functions}.
  \end{itemize}

  As mentioned in the beginning, this approach differs from what is established in the literature. We list approaches used in different sources.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item All discussion of the aforementioned topics starts with a definition of Kuratowski pairs in
    \cite[def. I.4.11]{Kunen2013SetTheory},
    \cite[7]{Jech2006SetTheory},
    \cite[def. 6.2.1]{Hinman2005Logic},
    \cite[235]{Mendelson2015Logic},
    \cite[36]{Enderton1977SetTheory},
    \cite[23]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory},
    \cite[139]{КолмогоровДрагалин2006ЛогикаДополнительныеГлавы},
    \cite[182]{Герасимов2014Вычислимость},
    \cite[\S 1.2.3]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ}.

    \Textcite[72]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets} instead introduce an axiom that guarantees the existence of an ordered pair satisfying \cref{thm:kuratowski_pair_extensionality}.

    \item An ordered tuple is defined as an iterated pair, i.e. \( \braket{ a, b, c } = \braket{ \braket{ a, b }, c } \), in
    \cite[7]{Jech2006SetTheory},
    \cite[def. 6.2.1]{Hinman2005Logic},
    \cite[236]{Mendelson2015Logic},
    \cite[31]{Enderton1977SetTheory},
    \cite[139]{КолмогоровДрагалин2006ЛогикаДополнительныеГлавы},
    \cite[183]{Герасимов2014Вычислимость} and
    \cite[16]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ}.

    \Textcite[22]{Kunen2013SetTheory} defines a \enquote{sequence} as a function whose domain is an \hyperref[def:ordinal]{ordinal} (what we call a \hyperref[def:transfinite_sequence]{transfinite sequence}). He defines ordered tuples as special cases.

    \Textcite[36]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory} defines ordered \( n \)-tuples as elements of a Cartesian product whose index set is an unordered \( n \)-tuples (i.e. a set of \( n \) elements).

    \item A binary Cartesian product \( A \times B \) is defined as sets of all Kuratowski pairs \( \braket{ a, b } \), where \( a \) is in \( A \) and \( b \) is in \( B \).

    All aforementioned authors construct binary Cartesian products in this way ---
    \cite[def. I.6.5]{Kunen2013SetTheory},
    \cite[10]{Jech2006SetTheory},
    \cite[def. 6.2.3]{Hinman2005Logic},
    \cite[240]{Mendelson2015Logic},
    \cite[37]{Enderton1977SetTheory},
    \cite[24]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory},
    \cite[def. I.5.1]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets},
    \cite[141]{КолмогоровДрагалин2006ЛогикаДополнительныеГлавы},
    \cite[188]{Герасимов2014Вычислимость} and
    \cite[\S 1.2.3]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ}.

    \item A function from \( A \) to \( B \) is then defined as any subset of \( A \times B \) that contains exactly one pair \( (a, b) \) for every \( a \) in \( A \)\fnote{We discuss in \cref{rem:function_definition} that the definition of a function must also explicitly specify its domain and codomain, not only its graph.}.

    Out of the aforementioned authors, \textcite[def. II.3.1]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets} defines function graphs as subsets of the Cartesian product after discussing functions.

    Most other authors define functions as subsets of Cartesian products ---
    \cite[11]{Jech2006SetTheory},
    \cite[def. 6.2.8]{Hinman2005Logic},
    \cite[42]{Enderton1977SetTheory},
    \cite[30]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory},
    \cite[143]{КолмогоровДрагалин2006ЛогикаДополнительныеГлавы} and
    \cite[190]{Герасимов2014Вычислимость}.

    Without referencing Cartesian products, functions are defined as sets of tuples in
    \cite[def. I.6.3]{Kunen2013SetTheory},
    \cite[245]{Mendelson2015Logic} and
    \cite[\S 1.2.4]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ}.

    \item Finally, a \hyperref[def:choice_function]{choice function} \( f \) for a family of nonempty sets \( \mscrA \) is defined as a function from \( \mscrA \) to the union \( \bigcup \mscrA \) such that, for every \( A \) in \( \mscrA \), the value \( f(A) \) belongs to \( A \).

    The general Cartesian product \( \prod \mscrA \) of the collection \( \mscrA \) is defined as the set of all choice functions on \( \mscrA \).

    Both choice functions and general products are discussed in
    \cite[59; 60]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory},
    \cite[60; 151]{Enderton1977SetTheory},
    \cite[47; 53]{Jech2006SetTheory} and
    \cite[282; 285]{Mendelson2015Logic} (briefly), however only Halmos mentions the connection between them.

    Choice functions, but not general Cartesian products, are discussed in
    \cite[def. I.12.2]{Kunen2013SetTheory},
    \cite[def. 6.4.1]{Hinman2005Logic} and
    \cite[194]{Герасимов2014Вычислимость}.

    On the other hand, arbitrary products are defined without discussing choice functions in
    \cite[def. II.5.1]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets} and
    \cite[146]{КолмогоровДрагалин2006ЛогикаВведение}.
  \end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Kuratowski pairs}

\begin{definition}\label{def:kuratowski_pair}\mcite[thm. 9]{ШеньВерещагин2020НачалаТеорииМножеств}
  A \term[ru=упорядоченная пара по Куратовскому (\cite[thm. 9]{ШеньВерещагин2020НачалаТеорииМножеств}), en=Kuratowski pair (\cite[22]{Kunen2013SetTheory})]{Kuratowski pair} is a popular way to encode an ordered pair as a set.

  Formally, in accordance with \cref{def:set_theory_definition}, we can use nested roster notation to introduce a binary \hyperref[def:fol_signature/function]{function symbol}:
  \begin{equation*}
    \synK(\syna, \synb) \coloneqq \set{ \set{ \syna }, \set{ \syna, \synb } }.
  \end{equation*}

  In the metalanguage, we will denote the pair \( K(a, b) \) by \( \braket{ a, b } \).
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item We will use this definition only as a technical step for defining indexed families. What matters to us is the property \cref{thm:kuratowski_pair_extensionality}, which is also satisfied by ordered pairs as defined in \cref{def:ordered_tuple}. We will use the angle bracket notation only in the few places where we will explicitly rely on ordered pairs being defined as per this definition.

  \item For unordered pairs, we have the weaker statement \cref{thm:unordered_pair_extensionality}. For indexed families, we have the more general statement \cref{thm:indexed_family_extensionality}.
\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:kuratowski_pair_extensionality}
  For \hyperref[def:kuratowski_pair]{Kuratowski pairs} the following property holds:
  \begin{equation*}
    \braket{ a, b } = \braket{ c, d } \T{if and only if} a = c \T{and} b = d.
  \end{equation*}
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item \Textcite[72]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets} introduce an axiom for the existence of ordered pairs satisfying this property. See \cref{rem:cartesian_product_definition} for a broader discussion.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  \SufficiencySubProof Suppose that
  \begin{equation*}
    \set{ \set{ a }, \set{ a, b } }
    =
    \braket{ a, b }
    =
    \braket{ c, d }
    =
    \set{ \set{ c }, \set{ c, d } }.
  \end{equation*}

  \Cref{thm:unordered_pair_extensionality} implies that there are only two cases:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If \( \set{ a } = \set{ c, d } \) and \( \set{ a, b } = \set{ c } \), then, by the \hyperref[def:axiomatic_set_theory/extensionality]{axiom of extensionality}, the equalities imply \( a = c = d \) and \( a = b = c \), correspondingly. Hence, \( a = b = c = d \).

    \item If \( \set{ a } = \set{ c } \) and \( \set{ a, b } = \set{ c, d } \), then \( a = c \) from the first equality, and \cref{thm:unordered_pair_extensionality} applied to the second equality gives us two sub-cases:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item If \( a = c \) and \( b = d \), we are done.
      \item If \( a = d \) and \( b = c \), since \( a = c \), it follows that \( a = b = c = d \).
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}

  \NecessitySubProof If \( a = c \) and \( b = d \), extensionality implies that \( \set{ a } = \set{ c } \), and \cref{thm:unordered_pair_extensionality} implies that \( \set{ a, c } = \set{ b, d } \). We can again use \cref{thm:unordered_pair_extensionality} to conclude that
  \begin{equation*}
    \braket{ a, b }
    =
    \set{ \set{ a }, \set{ a, b } }
    =
    \set{ \set{ c }, \set{ c, d } }
    =
    \braket{ c, d }.
  \end{equation*}
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Indexed families}

\begin{definition}\label{def:indexed_family}\mimprovised
  An \term[ru=индексированное семейство (\cite[60]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика}), en=indexed family (\cite[2]{Engelking1989GeneralTopology})]{indexed family} is a simple \hyperref[con:variable_dependence]{correspondence} --- roughly, a \hyperref[def:function]{single-valued function} without a specified codomain. Different authors define indexed families, relations, set-valued and single-valued functions differently; see \cref{rem:cartesian_product_definition} and \cref{rem:function_definition}.

  Our approach is to define an indexed family as a set \( \mscrA \) whose members are \hyperref[def:kuratowski_pair]{Kuratowski pairs}. If \( \mscrA \) contains \( \braket{ k, a_k } \), we call \( k \) the \term[en=index (\cite[36]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory})]{index} of the pair, and \( a_k \) --- the \( k \)-th \term[ru=координата (кортежа чисел) (\cite[14]{Тыртышников2007ЛинейнаяАлгебра}), en=coordinate (\cite[36]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory})]{coordinate}, \term[ru=компонента (кортежа чисел) (\cite[61]{Курош1968КурсВысшейАлгебры}), en=component (\cite[35]{Enderton1977SetTheory})]{component} or \term[en=term (\cite[34]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory})]{term} of \( \mscrA \).

  We call the collection \( \mscrK \) of all indices of \( \mscrA \) the \term[en=index set (\cite[34]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory})]{index set} or \term[ru=domain (of relation) (\cite[27]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory})]{domain} of \( \mscrA \). By \hyperref[def:axiomatic_set_theory/extensionality]{extensionality}, the components of \( \mscrA \) completely describe it, so we prefer the simplified notation \( \seq{ a_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \).

  Formally, in accordance with \cref{def:set_theory_definition}, we can introduce the unary \hyperref[def:fol_signature/predicate]{predicate symbol}
  \begin{align*}
    \op{IsFamily}(\synf) &\coloneqq \qforall {\synx \synin \synf} \qexists \synk \qExists \syna (\synx \syneq \synK(\synk, \syna)) \\
  \intertext{and two unary \hyperref[def:fol_signature/function]{function symbols}}
    \syndom(\synf) &\coloneqq \set{ \synk \given \qexists \syna \synK(\synk, \syna) \synin \synf }. \\
    \syncod(\synf) &\coloneqq \set{ \syna \given \qexists \synk \synK(\synk, \syna) \synin \synf }.
  \end{align*}

  Here \( \cod(\mscrA) \) is the set of all components of \( \mscrA \), which we call its \term[en=codomain (of function) (\cite[11]{Savage2008ModelsOfComputation})]{codomain} of \( \mscrA \).
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item We define domains and codomains as auxiliary notions, based on the corresponding notions for functions. We will prefer using indexed families to functions when stating axioms as the formulas will be simpler.

  We will restate these definitions with broader discussion in \fullref{sec:functions}.

  \item In accordance with \cref{rem:cartesian_product_definition}, we define indexed families as sets of pairs, and then proceed to define Cartesian products in terms of indexed families. In \cref{def:function}, we will define functions via indexed families, unlike, for example, \textcite[ch. 9]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory} and \textcite[2]{Engelking1989GeneralTopology}, who define indexed families via functions.

  Nevertheless, after we prove \fullref{thm:cartesian_product_universal_property}, we will regard indexed families as functions whenever we will find it convenient.

  An extensive discussion of indexed families (which Halmos simply calls \enquote{families}) can be found in \cite[ch. 9]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory}.
\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:indexed_family_extensionality}
  For \hyperref[def:indexed_family]{indexed families} with the same index set \( \mscrK \) the following property holds:
  \begin{equation*}
    \seq{ a_k }_{k \in \mscrK} = \seq{ b_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \T{if and only if} a_k = b_k \T{for every} k \T{in} \mscrK.
  \end{equation*}
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item We have a similar statement for \hyperref[def:kuratowski_pair]{Kuratowski pairs} --- \cref{thm:kuratowski_pair_extensionality}.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  When justifying the notation \( \seq{ a_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \) in \cref{def:indexed_family}, we have already noted that the coordinates of an indexed family completely determine it. This statement is then a trivial corollary.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:ordered_tuple}\mimprovised
  Fix a \hyperref[def:natural_numbers]{natural number} \( n \), i.e. a member of the \hyperref[def:smallest_inductive_set]{smallest inductive set} \( \omega \).

  An \term[ru=кортеж (\cite[def. 1.2]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика}]{ordered tuple} of \term{length} \( n \) is a \hyperref[def:indexed_family]{family} indexed by \( n \). Other terms in use are \term[ru=упорядоченная \( n \)-торка (\cite[\S 1.2.3]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ}), en=\( n \)-tuple (\cite[51]{Enderton1977SetTheory})]{\( n \)-tuple}, \term[en=finite sequence (\cite[9]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory})]{finite sequence} and, in some contexts, \term[en=list (\cite{Savage2008ModelsOfComputation})]{list}.

  Rather than using the notation \( \seq{ a_k }_{k \in n} \), we will prefer
  \begin{equation*}
    (a_1, \ldots, a_n),
  \end{equation*}
  where, in accordance with \cref{rem:peano_arithmetic/zero} and \cref{rem:cardinal_colorings}, for convenience we start indexing at \( 1 \).

  When we are not interested in the tuple itself as a set, we will skip the parentheses and instead use the notation
  \begin{equation*}
    a_1, \ldots, a_n.
  \end{equation*}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Both ordered tuples and \hyperref[def:sequence]{sequence} are instances of transfinite sequences, which we will define in \cref{def:transfinite_sequence}.

  \item Ordered tuples are often defined via iterated Kuratowski pairs instead --- see \cref{rem:cartesian_product_definition}.
\end{comments}

\begin{definition}\label{def:ordered_tuple_length_terminology}
  We introduce following terms for \hyperref[def:ordered_tuple]{\( n \)-tuples} of certain lengths:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \term[en=empty tuple (\cite[example 3.5.10]{Tao2022AnalysisI})]{Empty tuple} when \( n = 0 \),
    \item \term{Singleton tuple} when \( n = 1 \).
    \item \term[bg=наредена двойка (\cite[367]{ГеновМиховскиМоллов1991Алгебра}), ru=(упорядоченная) пара (\cite[38]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика}), en=(ordered) pair (\cite[23]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory})]{Pair} when \( n = 2 \).
    \item \term[ru=(упорядоченная) тройка (\cite[39]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика}), en=(ordered) triple (\cite[42]{Enderton1977SetTheory})]{Triple} when \( n = 3 \).
    \item \term[ru=(упорядоченная) четвёрка (\cite[39]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика}), en=(ordered) quadruple (\cite[42]{Enderton1977SetTheory})]{Quadruple} when \( n = 4 \).
    \item \term[ru=(упорядоченная) пятёрка (\cite[39]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика}), en=(ordered) quintuple (\cite[42]{Enderton1977SetTheory})]{Quintuple} when \( n = 5 \).
  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:kuratowski_pairs_and_tuples}
  The \hyperref[def:ordered_tuple]{ordered pair} \( (a, b) \) with elements
  \begin{equation*}
    (a, b) = \set[\big]{ \set{ \set{ 1 }, \set{ 1, a } }, \set{ \set{ 2 }, \set{ 2, b } } }
  \end{equation*}
  is formally different from the \hyperref[def:kuratowski_pair]{Kuratowski pair}
  \begin{equation*}
    \braket{ a, b } = \set[\big]{ \set{ a }, \set{ a, b } }.
  \end{equation*}

  We could define an ordered triple as \( \braket{ \braket{ a, b }, c } \), like many of the authors discussed in \cref{rem:cartesian_product_definition}.

  For the sake of consistency in our definitions, we wanted to avoid working with tuples defined in terms of iterated Kuratowski pairs. We even introduced a special notation for Kuratowski pairs, just so we can avoid any confusion.

  Nevertheless, it is occasionally conventional to conflate \( (a, b) \) with \( \braket{ a, b } \), as well as the ordered pairs \( ((a, b), c) \) and \( (a, (b, c)) \)\fnote{The triples \( ((a, b), c) \) and \( (a, (b, c)) \) are subject to the defining equivalences of a \hyperref[def:cartesian_monoidal_category]{Cartesian monoidal category}; see \cref{ex:category_of_small_sets_is_monoidal}.} and the triple \( (a, b, c) \). \Fullref{thm:cartesian_product_universal_property} will justify using any definition of ordered tuple, as long as its usage is consistent.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}\label{def:sequence}\mcite[45]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory}
  An \term{infinite sequence} or simply \term[ru=последовательность (\cite[193]{Герасимов2014Вычислимость})]{sequence} is a \hyperref[def:indexed_family]{family} indexed by the \hyperref[def:smallest_inductive_set]{smallest inductive set} \( \omega \).

  Rather than using the notation \( \seq{ a_k }_{k \in \omega} \), we will prefer
  \begin{equation*}
    \seq{ a_k }_{k=1}^\infty,
  \end{equation*}
  where, in accordance with \cref{rem:peano_arithmetic/zero}, for convenience we start indexing at \( 1 \).
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Both sequences and \hyperref[def:ordered_tuple]{ordered tuples} are instances of transfinite sequences, which we will define in \cref{def:transfinite_sequence}.
\end{comments}

\begin{definition}\label{def:set_enumeration}\mimprovised
  An \term[en=enumeration (\cite[190]{Kleene2002Logic})]{enumeration} of the set \( A \) is an \hyperref[def:ordered_tuple]{ordered tuple} or \hyperref[def:sequence]{infinite sequence} (possibly \hyperref[def:transfinite_sequence]{transfinite}) over \( A \) that is bijective as a function.
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item We generalize enumerations to multisets in \cref{def:multiset_enumeration}.
\end{comments}

\paragraph{Cartesian products}

\begin{definition}\label{def:cartesian_product}\mcite[36]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory}
  For an \hyperref[def:indexed_family]{indexed family} \( \seq{ A_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \), we define the \term[bg=Декартово произведение (\cite[367]{ГеновМиховскиМоллов1991Алгебра}), ru=Декартово произведение (\cite[146]{КолмогоровДрагалин2006ЛогикаДополнительныеГлавы})]{Cartesian product}
  \begin{equation*}
    \prod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \coloneqq \set[\big]{ \seq{ a_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \given \qforall* {k \in \mscrK} a_k \in A_k }.
  \end{equation*}

  For a \hyperref[def:ordered_tuple]{list} \( A_1, \ldots, A_n \) of sets, we often prefer the notation
  \begin{equation*}
    A_1 \times \cdots \times A_n.
  \end{equation*}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item We base this definition on \cite[36]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory}, but we avoid defining binary Cartesian products and functions beforehand. See \cref{rem:cartesian_product_definition} for a discussion of how Cartesian products are defined across the literature and why we chose this particular definition.

  \item Finite Cartesian products can be constructed straightforwardly, however more general products may require the axiom of choice --- see \cref{rem:cartesian_product_existence}.

  \item After we prove \fullref{thm:cartesian_product_universal_property}, we will be able to regard the Cartesian product as a set of \hyperref[def:choice_function]{choice functions}.

  \item Taking the \( k \)-th coordinate of every indexed family in the product \( \prod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \), we obtain a function from \( \prod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \) to \( A_k \). This is defined as discussed in \cref{def:cartesian_product_projection}.

  \item As shown in \cref{thm:set_of_all_functions_via_cartesian_product}, in the degenerate case where \( A_k = A_m \) for \( k \neq m \), the Cartesian product is \hyperref[def:equinumerosity]{equinumerous} with the \hyperref[def:set_of_all_functions]{set of all functions} from \( A \) to \( B \), which we denote by either \( B^A \) or \( \fun(A, B) \) depending on the situation. After we prove the aforementioned proposition, we will conflate \( B^A \) with \( \prod_{a \in A} B \).

  An analogous equivalence is also present in other systems like \hyperref[def:martin_lof_type_theory]{Martin-L\"of type theory} --- see \cref{rem:arrow_type_via_dependent_product}.
\end{comments}

\paragraph{Relations}

\begin{definition}\label{def:relation}\mcite[42]{Enderton1977SetTheory}
  Let \( A_1, \ldots, A_n \) be a \hyperref[def:ordered_tuple]{list} of sets. We call any subset
  \begin{equation*}
    R \subseteq A_1 \times \cdots \times A_n
  \end{equation*}
  of their \hyperref[def:cartesian_product]{Cartesian product} an \( n \)-ary \term[ru=\( n \)-местное отношение (\cite[90]{Герасимов2014Вычислимость})]{relation}.

  We say that the values
  \begin{equation*}
    x_1 \in A_1, \ldots, x_n \in A_n
  \end{equation*}
  are \term[en=related by \( R \) (\cite[6]{Aluffi2009Algebra})]{related} by \( R \) if \( (x_1, \ldots, x_n) \in R \).

  \begin{thmenum}[series=def:relation]
    \thmitem{def:relation/arity}\mimprovised We call \( n \) the \term{arity} of \( R \).

    For some small values of \( n \), we use the adjectives from \cref{def:operation_arity_terminology} like \enquote{unary} or \enquote{binary} for describing the arity.

    \thmitem{def:relation/single_set}\mimprovised If all \( A = A_1 = \cdots = A_n \), we say that \( R \subseteq A^n \) is a relation \emph{on} \( A \).

    \thmitem{def:relation/empty}\mcite[def. 1.1.1]{Harzheim2005OrderedSets} We call the relation \term{empty} if it is the empty set, i.e. if no tuple of elements is related.

    \medskip

    \thmitem{def:relation/restriction}\mcite[\S 3.3]{Schechter1997AnalysisHandbook} If \( B_k \subseteq A_k \) for \( k = 1, \ldots, n \), we can define the \term{restriction} of \( R \subseteq A_1 \times \cdots \times A_n \) to \( B_1 \times \cdots \times B_n \) by simply intersecting \( R \) with the latter.

    In case \( A = A_1 = \cdots = A_n \) and \( B = B_1 = \cdots = B_n \), we say that we restrict \( R \) to \( B \) rather than \( B^n \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}\label{def:binary_relation}\mcite[def. 1.1.1]{Harzheim2005OrderedSets}
  An important special case of \hyperref[def:relation]{relations} are \term{binary relations}. Given two sets, a binary relation between them is a subset of their \hyperref[def:cartesian_product]{Cartesian product}.

  In addition to the terminology for \cref{def:relation}, we also introduce the following:
  \begin{thmenum}[series=def:binary_relation]
    \thmitem{def:binary_relation/inverse} We defined the \term{inverse relation} of \( R \) as
    \begin{equation*}
      R^{-1} \coloneqq \set{ (y, x) \given (x, y) \in R }.
    \end{equation*}

    \thmitem{def:binary_relation/diagonal} For every set \( A \), we define a special relation,
    \begin{equation*}
      \Delta_A \coloneqq \set{ (x, x) \given x \in A },
    \end{equation*}
    which we call the \term[ru=диагональное (отношение) (\cite[def. 2.7]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{diagonal relation} or \term{identity relation}.

    \thmitem{def:binary_relation/composition}\mcite[7]{Kelley1975GeneralTopology} Given two binary relations \( R \subseteq A \times B \) and \( S \subseteq B \times C \), we define their composition as
    \begin{equation*}
      S \bincirc R \coloneqq \set{ (x, z) \in A \times C \given \qexists* ({y \in B} (x, y) \in R \T{and} (y, z) \in S) }.
    \end{equation*}
  \end{thmenum}

  Whenever \( A = B \) and \( R \) is simply a binary relation over \( A \), the following are commonly used properties. They are presented as first-order formulas that can be used as axioms for some \hyperref[def:logical_theory]{logical theory} (with \hyperref[def:function_application_notation/infix]{infix notation} for \( R \)):
  \begin{thmenum}[resume=def:binary_relation]
    \thmitem{def:binary_relation/reflexive}\mcite[def. 6.2.14(i)]{Hinman2005Logic} We call \( R \) \term[bg=рефлексивна (релация) (\cite[369]{ГеновМиховскиМоллов1991Алгебра}), ru=рефлексивное (отношение) (\cite[def. 2.7]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{reflexive} if \( \Delta_A \subseteq R \), i.e. if every element of \( A \) is related with itself.

    The following formula is used as an axiom for \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set/strict]{nonstrict partial orders} and \hyperref[def:entourage]{entourages}:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:def:binary_relation/reflexive}
      \qforall \synx (\synx \mathbin{R} \synx).
    \end{equation}

    \thmitem{def:binary_relation/irreflexive}\mcite[def. 6.2.14(ii)]{Hinman2005Logic} We call \( R \) \term[ru=антирефлексивное (отношение) (\cite[def. 2.7]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{irreflexive} if \( \Delta_A \cap R = \varnothing \), i.e. if no element of \( A \) is related with itself.

    The following formula is used as an axiom for \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set/strict]{strict partial orders}:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:def:binary_relation/irreflexive}
      \synneg \qexists \synx (\synx \mathbin{R} \synx).
    \end{equation}

    \thmitem{def:binary_relation/symmetric}\mcite[def. 6.2.14(ix)]{Hinman2005Logic} We call \( R \) \term[bg=симетрична (релация) (\cite[369]{ГеновМиховскиМоллов1991Алгебра}), ru=симметрическое (отношение) (\cite[def. 2.7]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{symmetric} if \( R = R^{-1} \).

    The following formula is used as an axiom for \hyperref[def:equivalence_relation]{equivalence relations}, \hyperref[def:hypergraph/multigraph]{undirected graphs} and \hyperref[def:entourage]{entourages}:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:def:binary_relation/symmetric}
      \synx \mathbin{R} \syny \synimplies \syny \mathbin{R} \synx.
    \end{equation}

    \thmitem{def:binary_relation/antisymmetric}\mcite[example 2.2.1(ii)(c)]{Hinman2005Logic} We call \( R \) \term[bg=антисиметрична (релация) (\cite[369]{ГеновМиховскиМоллов1991Алгебра}), ru=антисимметрическое (отношение) (\cite[def. 2.7]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{antisymmetric} if \( R \cap R^{-1} = \Delta_X \).

    The following formula is used as an axiom for \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set]{partial orders}:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:def:binary_relation/antisymmetric}
      (\synx \mathbin{R} \syny \synwedge \syny \mathbin{R} \synx) \synimplies \synx \syneq \syny.
    \end{equation}

    \thmitem{def:binary_relation/transitive}\mcite[def. 6.2.14(iii)]{Hinman2005Logic} We call \( R \) \term[bg=транзитивна (релация) (\cite[369]{ГеновМиховскиМоллов1991Алгебра}), ru=транзитивное (отношение) (\cite[def. 2.7]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{transitive} if \( R = R \bincirc R \).

    The following formula is used as an axiom for \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set]{preorders}:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:def:binary_relation/transitive}
      (\synx \mathbin{R} \syny \synwedge \syny \mathbin{R} \synz) \synimplies \synx \mathbin{R} \synz.
    \end{equation}

    \thmitem{def:binary_relation/connected}\mcite[63]{Enderton1977SetTheory} We call \( R \) \term[ru=полное (отношение) (\cite[def. 2.7]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{connected} if any two member of \( A \) are related.

    The following formula is used as an axiom for \hyperref[def:totally_ordered_set]{nonstrict total orders}:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:def:binary_relation/connected}
      \qforall \synx \qforall \syny (\synx \mathbin{R} \syny \synvee \syny \mathbin{R} \synx).
    \end{equation}

    \thmitem{def:binary_relation/trichotomy}\mcite[62]{Enderton1977SetTheory} We say that \( R \) satisfies \term[bg=трихотомия (\cite[11]{Тагамлицки1971ДиференциалноСмятане})]{trichotomy} if every two elements of \( A \) are either related or equal.

    The following formula is used as an axiom for \hyperref[def:totally_ordered_set]{strict total orders}:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:def:binary_relation/trichotomy}
      \qforall \synx \qforall \syny (\synx \mathbin{R} \syny \synvee \syny \mathbin{R} \synx \synvee \syny = \synx).
    \end{equation}
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Relations are the semantic counterpart to \hyperref[def:fol_signature/predicate]{first-order predicates} and, as discussed in \cref{rem:boolean_valued_functions_and_relations}, are interchangeable with Boolean-valued functions.
\end{comments}

\begin{example}\label{ex:def:binary_relation}
  We list examples of \hyperref[def:binary_relation]{binary relations}:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{ex:def:binary_relation/functions} \hyperref[def:function]{Functions} are special binary relations.

    \thmitem{ex:def:binary_relation/orders} \hyperref[ch:order_theory]{Orders} are also special binary relations.

    \thmitem{ex:def:binary_relation/graphs} \hyperref[def:directed_graph]{Directed graphs} can be defined as binary relations.

    \thmitem{ex:def:binary_relation/entourages} \hyperref[def:entourage]{Entourages} are binary relations in \hyperref[def:uniform_space]{uniform spaces}.

    \thmitem{ex:def:binary_relation/boolean_valued_function} Relations are equivalent to Boolean-valued functions as shown in \cref{rem:boolean_valued_functions_and_relations}, and are often used for defining semantics of predicate symbols in \hyperref[sec:first_order_logic]{first-order logic}.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{example}

\paragraph{Closures of binary relations}

\begin{definition}\label{def:relation_closures}
  Let \( R \subseteq A^2 \) be a binary relation on the set \( A \). We introduce several \hyperref[def:abstract_closure_operator]{closure operators}:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:relation_closures/reflexive}\mcite[example 9.4.1]{Rosen2019DiscreteMathematics} We define the \term{reflexive closure} of \( R \) as
    \begin{equation*}
      \cl^R(R) \coloneqq R \cup \Delta_X.
    \end{equation*}

    \thmitem{def:relation_closures/symmetric}\mcite[example 9.4.2]{Rosen2019DiscreteMathematics} We define the \term{symmetric closure} of \( R \) as
    \begin{equation*}
      \cl^S(R) \coloneqq R \cup R^{-1}.
    \end{equation*}

    \thmitem{def:relation_closures/transitive}\mcite[\S 9.4.4]{Rosen2019DiscreteMathematics} We define the \term{transitive closure} of \( R \) as
    \begin{equation*}
      \cl^T(R) \coloneqq \bigcup \set{ R^k \given k = 1, 2, \ldots },
    \end{equation*}
    where \( R^k \) is iterated \hyperref[def:binary_relation/composition]{composition} of \( R \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Any binary relation can be regarded as a \hyperref[def:directed_graph]{directed graph} in accordance with \cref{def:directed_graph_induced_by_relation}, which allows conflating this definition with the transitive closure of a (directed) graph defined in \cref{def:relation_closures/transitive}.

  On the other hand, the concept is fundamentally different from that of the transitive closure of a set defined in \cref{def:transitive_closure_of_a_set}.
\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:def:relation_closures}
  \hyperref[def:relation_closures]{Binary relation closures} have the following basic properties:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:def:relation_closures/reflexive_relation} The symmetric and transitive closures of a reflexive relation are also reflexive.

    \thmitem{thm:def:relation_closures/symmetric_relation} The reflexive and transitive closures of a symmetric relation are also symmetric.

    \thmitem{thm:def:relation_closures/transitive_relation} The reflexive closure of a transitive relation is also transitive. The symmetric closure of a transitive relation may not be transitive --- see \cref{ex:thm:def:relation_closures/symmetric_and_transitive}.

    \thmitem{thm:def:relation_closures/reflexive_and_symmetric} The reflexive and symmetric closures commute:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:def:relation_closures/reflexive_and_symmetric}
      \cl^S \cl^R(R) = \cl^R \cl^S(R).
    \end{equation}

    \thmitem{thm:def:relation_closures/reflexive_and_transitive} The transitive and reflexive closures commute:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:def:relation_closures/reflexive_and_transitive}
      \cl^R \cl^T(R) = \cl^T \cl^R(R).
    \end{equation}

    \thmitem{thm:def:relation_closures/symmetric_and_transitive} For the transitive and symmetric closures of \( R \) we have
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:def:relation_closures/symmetric_and_transitive}
      \cl^S \cl^T(R) \subseteq \cl^T \cl^S(R).
    \end{equation}

    The converse holds if \( R \) is symmetric but not in general --- see \cref{ex:thm:def:relation_closures/symmetric_and_transitive}.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:relation_closures/reflexive_relation} Trivial.
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:relation_closures/symmetric_relation} Trivial.
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:relation_closures/transitive_relation} Trivial.
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:relation_closures/reflexive_and_symmetric} Trivial.
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:relation_closures/reflexive_and_transitive} The reflexive closure only adds pairs of the form \( (x, x) \). Thus, if \( (x, y) \in \cl^T(\cl^R(R)) \) for \( x \neq y \), then \( (x, y) \in \cl^T(R) \subseteq \cl^R(\cl^T(R)) \).

  Conversely, if \( (x, y) \in \cl^R(\cl^T(R)) \) for \( x \neq y \), then \( (x, y) \in \cl^T(R) \subseteq \cl^R(\cl^T(R)) \).

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:relation_closures/symmetric_and_transitive} If \( (x, y) \in \cl^S(\cl^T(R)) \), then we have the following possibilities:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If \( (x, y) \in R \), obviously \( (x, y) \in \cl^T(\cl^S(R)) \).
    \item If \( (x, y) \in \cl^T(R) \setminus R \), then there exists some natural number \( k > 1 \) such that \( (x, y) \in R^k \).

    Since \( R^k \subseteq [\cl^S(R)]^k \), as can be shown via induction, we have \( (x, y) \in [\cl^S(R)]^k \). We thus conclude that \( (x, y) \in \cl^T(\cl^S(R)) \).

    \item Finally, if \( (x, y) \notin \cl^T(R) \), then \( (y, x) \in \cl^T(R) \). As in the previous step, we can show that \( (y, x) \in \cl^T(\cl^S(R))) \). The latter set is symmetric, hence \( (x, y) \in \cl^T(\cl^S(R)) \).
  \end{itemize}

  Since \( (x, y) \) was arbitrary, we conclude that \eqref{eq:thm:def:relation_closures/symmetric_and_transitive} holds.

  Furthermore, if \( R \) is symmetric, then
  \begin{equation*}
    \cl^T \cl^S(R)
    =
    \cl^T(R)
    \reloset {\ref{thm:def:relation_closures/reflexive_relation}} =
    \cl^S \cl^T(R).
  \end{equation*}
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:thm:def:relation_closures/symmetric_and_transitive}
  \hyperref[def:relation_closures/symmetric]{Symmetric} and \hyperref[def:relation_closures/transitive]{transitive} closures of relations do not commute in general. This is also a consequence of the difference between \hyperref[def:graph_connectedness/weak]{weak} and \hyperref[def:graph_connectedness/strong]{strong} connectedness of directed (multi)graphs.

  Consider the set \( A = \set{ a, b, c } \) and the relation \( R = \set{ (a, b), (c, b) } \).

  \begin{figure}
    \begin{equation*}
      \includegraphics[page=1]{output/ex__thm__def__relation_closures__symmetric_and_transitive}
    \end{equation*}
    \caption{The relation \( R \) from \cref{ex:thm:def:relation_closures/symmetric_and_transitive} regarded as \hyperref[def:directed_graph]{directed graphs} with the additional arcs from \( \cl^S(R) \) dashed.}\label{fig:ex:thm:def:relation_closures/symmetric_and_transitive}
  \end{figure}

  It should be noted that \( R \) is \hyperref[def:binary_relation/transitive]{transitive}. Thus,
  \begin{equation*}
    \cl^S(\cl^T(R)) = \cl^S(R) = R \cup \set{ (b, a), (b, c) }.
  \end{equation*}

  The latter set is not transitive because \( (a, b) \) and \( (b, c) \) both belong to \( \cl^S(R) \), but \( (a, c) \) does not.

  This shows that the converse of \eqref{eq:thm:def:relation_closures/symmetric_and_transitive} does not hold in general.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Equivalence relations}

\begin{definition}\label{def:equivalence_relation}\mcite[56]{Enderton1977SetTheory}
  If a binary relation on the set \( A \) is \hyperref[def:binary_relation/reflexive]{reflexive}, \hyperref[def:binary_relation/symmetric]{symmetric} and \hyperref[def:binary_relation/transitive]{transitive}, we call it an \term[ru=отношение эквивалентности (\cite[66]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика})]{equivalence relation}. In other words, an equivalence relation is a symmetric \hyperref[def:preordered_set]{preorder}.

  We often denote equivalence relations via the symbol \( \sim \).

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:equivalence_relation/coset}\mcite[57]{Enderton1977SetTheory} We define the \term[ru=класс эквивалентности (\cite[70]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика})]{equivalence class} of \( x \in A \) as the set
    \begin{equation*}
      [x] \coloneqq \set{ y \in A \given x \sim y }
    \end{equation*}
    of all elements of \( A \) that are related to \( x \).

    \thmitem{def:equivalence_relation/quotient}\mcite[58]{Enderton1977SetTheory} We define the \term{quotient set} of \( A \) by \( \sim \) as the set
    \begin{equation*}
      A / {\sim} \ \coloneqq \set{ [x] \given x \in A }.
    \end{equation*}

    If we have a canonical way to choose a representative from each coset, then \( A / {\sim} \) may be regarded as a subset of \( A \).

    \thmitem{def:equivalence_relation/projection}\mimprovised Using forward references to \fullref{sec:functions}, we define the \term[en=canonical projection (\cite[example 2.6]{Aluffi2009Algebra})]{canonical projection} as the function
    \begin{equation*}
      \begin{aligned}
        &\pi: A \to A / {\sim}  \\
        &\pi(x) \coloneqq [x].
      \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}

    If we have a fixed \hyperref[def:choice_function]{choice function} \( c: A / {\sim} \to A \), we can also define the canonical embedding
    \begin{equation*}
      \begin{aligned}
        &\iota: A / {\sim} \to A \\
        &\iota([x]) \coloneqq c(x).
      \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}

    We sometimes have an obvious choice function. For example, as shown in \cref{thm:truncated_polynomial_algebra/isomorphism}, in a suitable setting the polynomial remainder \( \rem(f, g) \) is a \hyperref[def:quotient_polynomial_algebra_as_truncated]{principal representative} in every coset \( [f] \) of the quotient algebra \( R[X] / \braket{ g(X) } \). In this case, the canonical projection may be regarded as a function from \( A \) to the subset \( c(A / {\sim}) \) of \( A \). Otherwise, \( \pi \) can be regarded as a \hyperref[def:function]{set-valued map}.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}\label{ex:def:equivalence_relation}
  We list examples of \hyperref[def:equivalence_relation]{equivalence relations}:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{ex:def:equivalence_relation/tuple} Given a \hyperref[def:cartesian_product]{Cartesian product} \( \prod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \), for every index \( m \) in \( \mscrK \) we can define the equivalence relation
    \begin{equation*}
       \seq{ x_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \sim_m \seq{ y_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \T{if} x_m = y_m.
    \end{equation*}

    Then the quotient \(\prod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k / {\sim_m} \) has exactly one coset for every member of \( A_m \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:equality_is_smallest_equivalence_relation}
  The equality \hyperref[def:binary_relation]{relation} \( = \) is the intersection of all equivalence relations.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  It coincide with the \hyperref[def:binary_relation/diagonal]{diagonal relation} \( \Delta_X \). It is the smallest reflexive (resp. symmetric and transitive) relation on \( A \), that is, the intersection of all reflexive (resp. symmetric and transitive) relations.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:set_cover}\mcite[44]{Engelking1989GeneralTopology}
  A \term[ru=покрытие (\cite[32]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{cover} of a \hyperref[def:set]{set} \( A \) is a collection \( \mscrA \subseteq \pow(A) \) of subsets of \( A \) such that \( A = \bigcup \mscrA \).
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item We sometimes use the term more loosely and say that an arbitrary family of sets \( \mscrA \) is a cover of \( A \) if \( A \subseteq \bigcup \mscrA \). The two definitions are identical if we intersect each set in \( \mscrA \) with \( A \).

  \item Some authors like \cite[32]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток} additionally disallow empty sets in \( \mscrA \).
\end{comments}

\begin{definition}\label{def:set_partition}\mcite[57]{Enderton1977SetTheory}
  A \term[ru=разбиение (\cite[32]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{partition} of a \hyperref[def:set]{set} \( A \) is a pairwise \hyperref[def:disjoint_sets]{disjoint} \hyperref[def:set_cover]{cover} with nonempty sets. In other words, the cover \( \mscrA \) is a partition if and only if each element of \( A \) belong to exactly one set in \( \mscrA \).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:equivalence_partition}
  Fix a set \( A \). Let \( {\sim} \) be a binary relation of \( A \). The following are equivalent:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:equivalence_partition/equivalence} \( {\sim} \) is an \hyperref[def:equivalence_relation]{equivalence relation}.

    \thmitem{thm:equivalence_partition/partition} There exists a \hyperref[def:set_partition]{partition} \( \mscrA \) of \( A \) such that \( x \sim y \) if and only if they belong to the same set in the partition \( \mscrA \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \ImplicationSubProof{thm:equivalence_partition/equivalence}{thm:equivalence_partition/partition} Let \( {\sim} \) be an equivalence relation on \( A \). The quotient set \( A / {\sim} \) is a partition. Indeed:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Every element \( x \in A \) belongs exactly one equivalence class \( [x] \) by definition.

    \item The equivalence classes are disjoint. Indeed, assume the contrary. Then there exist \( x \) and \( y \) such that \( [x] \cap [y] \neq \varnothing \) and yet \( x \nsim y \).

    Let \( z \in [x] \cap [y] \). Then \( z \sim x \) and \( z \sim y \), thus from transitivity of \( {\sim} \) we have \( x \sim z \sim y \) and hence \( x \sim y \), which contradicts our assumption that \( x \nsim y \).

    Hence, either \( [x] = [x] \) or \( [x] \cap [y] = \varnothing \). That is, different equivalence classes are disjoint.
  \end{itemize}

  \ImplicationSubProof{thm:equivalence_partition/partition}{thm:equivalence_partition/equivalence} Let \( \mscrA \) be a partition of \( A \) such that \( x \sim y \) if and only if they both belong to the same set in \( \mscrA \).

  Given \( x \in A \), denote by \( A_x \) the set in \( \mscrA \) which contains \( x \). The family \( \seq{ A_x }_{x \in A} \) is well-defined since \( \mscrA \) is a partition, which means that \( x \) belongs to exactly one set in \( \mscrA \).

  \SubProofOf*[def:binary_relation/reflexive]{reflexivity} Clearly \( A_x = A_x \), hence \( x \sim x \).

  \SubProofOf*[def:binary_relation/symmetric]{symmetry} If \( x \sim y \), then \( A_x = A_y \), which implies \( A_y = A_x \) and thus \( x \sim y \).

  \SubProofOf*[def:binary_relation/transitive]{transitivity} If \( x \sim y \) and \( y \in z \), then \( A_x = A_y = A_z \) and thus \( x \sim z \).
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Equivalence closures}

\begin{definition}\label{def:equivalence_closure}\mcite[89]{GirardEtAl1989ProofsAndTypes}
  Consider some \hyperref[def:binary_relation]{binary relation} \( R \) on \( A \). The intersection of all \hyperref[def:equivalence_relation]{equivalence relations} on \( A \) containing \( R \) is again an equivalence relation, which we call the \term{equivalence closure} of \( R \).
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item \Cref{thm:moore_family_closure_operator} implies that the equivalence closure is a \hyperref[def:abstract_closure_operator]{closure operator} on \( A^2 \).

  In particular, as a consequence of \cref{thm:closure_operator_minimality}, it is the least among all equivalence relations containing \( R \).

  \item See \cref{thm:equivalence_closure_characteriation} for a useful characterization. A more flexible construction utilizing \fullref{thm:recursively_defined_relations} is given in \cref{thm:generated_equivalence_relation}.
\end{comments}
\begin{defproof}
  Clearly \( A^2 \) is an equivalence relation, so the intersection is nonempty. It is then straightforward to show that this intersection is itself an equivalence relation.
\end{defproof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:equivalence_closure_characteriation}
  Consider some \hyperref[def:binary_relation]{binary relation} \( R \) on a set \( A \). The \hyperref[def:equivalence_closure]{equivalence closure} of \( R \) is the \hyperref[def:relation_closures/reflexive]{reflexive}, \hyperref[def:relation_closures/symmetric]{symmetric} and \hyperref[def:relation_closures/transitive]{transitive} closure \( \cl^T \cl^S \cl^R (R) \) of \( R \).
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item This holds for any permutation of the closures as long as \( \cl^T \) is applied \emph{after} \( \cl^S \). This latter restriction is due to \cref{thm:def:relation_closures/symmetric_and_transitive}.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  By \cref{thm:def:relation_closures/reflexive_relation}, \( \cl^S \cl^R (R) \) is reflexive. It is also symmetric as the symmetric closure of \( \cl^R(R) \).

  Then the transitive closure \( E \coloneqq \cl^T \cl^S \cl^R (R) \) is also symmetric and reflexive by \cref{thm:def:relation_closures/reflexive_relation} and \cref{thm:def:relation_closures/symmetric_relation}.

  Therefore, \( E \) is an equivalence relation containing \( R \). It remains to show that any equivalence relation \( E' \) containing \( R \) is a superset of \( E \).

  Fix a pair \( (x, y) \) from \( E \). Then there exists some positive integer \( k \) such that \( (x, y) \) belongs to
  \begin{equation*}
    \parens[\big]{ \cl^S \cl^R (R) }^k.
  \end{equation*}

  We can use induction on \( k \) to show that \( (x, y) \) belongs to \( E' \).
  \begin{itemize}
    \item When \( k = 1 \), either \( (x, y) \) or \( (y, x) \) belong to \( \cl^R (R) \), and hence to \( E' \).
    \item When \( k > 1 \), there exists some element \( z \) of \( A \) such that \( (x, z) \) belongs to \( \parens[\big]{ \cl^S \cl^R (R) }^{k-1} \) and \( (z, y) \) belong to \( \cl^S \cl^R (R) \).

    By the inductive hypothesis \( (x, z) \) belongs to \( E' \). By the same reasoning as in the base case, \( (z, y) \) belongs to \( E' \). Then, since \( E' \) is transitive, \( (x, y) \) also belongs to \( E' \).
  \end{itemize}

  Therefore, \( E \subseteq E' \), making \( E \) the smallest equivalence relation containing \( E \).
\end{proof}
