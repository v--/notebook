\section{Lattices}\label{sec:lattices}

\paragraph{Lattices}

\begin{definition}\label{def:lattice}\mcite[def. 4.2]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток}
  A \term[ru=решётка, en=lattice (\cite[12]{Grätzer2011LatticeTheory})]{lattice} is a nonempty set \( L \) equipped with two \hyperref[def:operation_arity_terminology]{binary} \hyperref[def:operation_on_set]{operations}\fnote{The terms \enquote{join} for \( \vee \) and \enquote{meet} for \( \wedge \) may be difficult to remember. A helpful accident is the ability to write \enquote{meet} as \enquote{\( \wedge \wedge \)eet}.} --- \term[ru=объединение, en=join (\cite[12]{Grätzer2011LatticeTheory})]{join}, denoted by \( \vee \), and \term[ru=пересечение, en=meet (\cite[12]{Grätzer2011LatticeTheory})]{meet}, denoted by \( \wedge \) --- satisfying the axioms we list in \cref{def:lattice/theory}.

  We use this algebraic definition because it allows us to formulate an \hyperref[def:fol_equational_theory]{equational theory} for lattices. Another definition is possible --- every lattice has a natural \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set]{partial order}, which can be used as a \hyperref[con:primitive_notion]{primitive notion} instead of joins and meets --- see \cref{thm:lattice_via_order_relation}.

  Lattices have the following metamathematical properties:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:lattice/theory}\mimprovised We can state an \hyperref[def:fol_equational_theory]{equational theory} for lattice over the signature with two symbols --- \( \latvee \) and \( \latwedge \) --- with a check on top used to distinguish them from the \hyperref[con:metalanguage]{metalingual} symbols \( \vee \) and \( \wedge \) and from the \hyperref[con:improper_symbol]{improper symbols} \( \synvee \) and \( \synwedge \).

    The theory itself can be axiomatized as follows:\fnote{Technically, we must take the \hyperref[def:fol_quantifier_closure]{universal closures} of the formulas here.}
    \begin{thmenum}[series=def:lattice]
      \thmitem{def:lattice/theory/individual} For both symbols, we need the associativity axiom \eqref{eq:def:binary_operation/associative} and the commutativity axiom \eqref{eq:def:binary_operation/commutative}.

      \thmitem{def:lattice/theory/absorption} Additionally, we need the following \term[ru=(законы) поглощения (\cite[6]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток}), en=absorption (\cite[8]{Birkhoff1967LatticeTheory})]{absorption} laws
      \begin{align}
        \synx \latvee (\synx \latwedge \syny) &\syneq \synx, \label{eq:def:lattice/theory/absorption/join} \\
        \synx \latwedge (\synx \latvee \syny) &\syneq \synx. \label{eq:def:lattice/theory/absorption/meet}
      \end{align}
    \end{thmenum}

    The idempotence axiom \eqref{eq:def:binary_operation/idempotent} for \( \synvee \) and \( \synwedge \) is sometimes taken as an axiom, for example in \cite[def. 4.2]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток} and \cite[12]{Grätzer2011LatticeTheory}, however it can be easily derived from absorption, as we shall see.

    This theory can be extended:
    \begin{thmenum}[resume=def:lattice]
      \thmitem{def:lattice/theory/leq} We can use any of the following two equivalent \hyperref[def:fol_definition/predicate]{first-order predicate definition} of \( {\synleq} \):
      \begin{align}
        \synx \synleq \syny &\syniff \synx \latvee \syny \syneq \syny, \label{eq:def:lattice/theory/leq/join} \\
        \synx \synleq \syny &\syniff \synx \latwedge \syny \syneq \synx. \label{eq:def:lattice/theory/leq/meet}
      \end{align}

      This relation is a \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set]{partial order}, as we will show in the proof of correctness.
    \end{thmenum}

    \thmitem{def:lattice/morphism}\mcite[def. 4.3]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток} We say that a function between lattices is a \term[ru=решёточный гомоморфизм, en=homomorphism (of lattices) (\cite[30]{Grätzer2011LatticeTheory})]{lattice homomorphism} if it respects joins and meets.

    These are precisely the \hyperref[def:fol_homomorphism]{first-order homomorphisms}, which makes the concepts from \fullref{sec:first_order_structures} applicable.

    \Cref{thm:def:lattice/morphism_respects_order} implies that a lattice homomorphism is \hyperref[def:order_function/preserving]{order-preserving}, and \cref{thm:def:lattice/embedding} implies that an order embedding is a lattice embedding.

    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[page=1]{output/def__lattice}
      \caption{An order homomorphism between lattices, which is not a lattice homomorphism.}
      \label{fig:def:lattice/morphism/order_homomorphism_not_lattice_homomorrphism}
    \end{figure}

    \thmitem{def:lattice/category}\mcite[\S I.3.2]{Johnstone1982StoneSpaces} The first-order theory of lattices naturally gives rise to a \hyperref[def:fol_theory_model_functor/obj]{category of small models}. We denote this category by \( \cat{Lat} \).

    We list several properties:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item The logical theory presented here is equational and thus has \hyperref[def:fol_free_term_model]{free term models}; \Fullref{thm:fol_free_term_model_universal_property} provides a \hyperref[def:category_adjunction]{left adjoint} to the \hyperref[def:fol_theory_forgetful_functor/set]{forgetful functor} \( U: \cat{Lat} \to \cat{Set} \). We discuss these free lattices in \cref{def:free_lattice}.

      In particular, \cref{thm:concrete_category_function_invertibility/injective} implies that a lattice homomorphism is a \hyperref[def:morphism_cancellation/left]{monomorphism} if and only if it is \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/injective]{injective}.

      \item A lattice can itself be regarded as a category, where \hyperref[def:category_of_cones/limit]{categorical limits} correspond to meets and \hyperref[def:category_of_cones/colimit]{categorical colimits} correspond to joins. See \cref{ex:limits_of_partially_ordered_set}.
    \end{itemize}

    \thmitem{def:lattice/subobject}\mcite[def. 4.4]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток} A \term[ru=подрешётка, en=sublattice (\cite[31]{Grätzer2011LatticeTheory})]{sublattice} of \( L \) is a subset of \( L \) closed under joins and meets.

    It follows from \cref{thm:fol_equational_theory_models/substructure} that the sublattices of \( L \) are precisely its \hyperref[def:fol_substructure/submodel]{first-order submodels}, and from \cref{thm:fol_categorical_subobjects} that they are its \hyperref[def:categorical_subobject]{categorical subobjects}.

    Furthermore, as a consequence of \cref{thm:fol_equational_theory_models/image}, the image of a lattice homomorphism is a sublattice of its codomain.

    \thmitem{def:lattice/opposite}\mimprovised We define the \term{dual lattice} of \( (L, \vee, \wedge) \) as
    \begin{equation*}
      (L, \vee, \wedge)^\oppos \coloneqq (L, \wedge, \vee).
    \end{equation*}

    The dual lattice coincides with the \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set/opposite]{dual ordered set}, as shown in \cref{thm:def:lattice/dual_order}.

    For the function symbols themselves, we define \( {\vee}^\oppos \) as \( {\wedge} \) and vice versa.

    For a fixed Grothendieck universe, this induces an \hyperref[con:opposite_object]{opposite object functor} in the corresponding category of small lattices. Like for partially ordered sets, here we prefer the term \enquote{dual} to \enquote{opposite} because of \fullref{thm:lattice_duality}.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item There is an unrelated concept also called a \enquote{lattice}, which we define in \cref{def:point_lattice} as \enquote{point lattice}. See \cref{rem:lattice_terminology} for a discussion of terminology.
\end{comments}
\begin{defproof}
  \SubProof{Proof that \( \vee \) and \( \wedge \) are idempotent} For \( \latvee \), we have
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{ [\eqref{eq:def:lattice/theory/leq/join}]^{a_1} }
      \infer1[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/forall/elim}]{ \qforall \syny \synx \syneq \synx \latvee (\synx \latwedge \syny) }
      \infer1[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/forall/elim}]{ \synx \syneq \synx \latvee (\synx \latwedge (\synx \latvee \synx)) }

      \hypo{ [\eqref{eq:def:lattice/theory/leq/meet}]^{a_2} }
      \infer1[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/forall/elim}]{ \qforall \syny \synx \syneq \synx \latwedge (\synx \latvee \syny) }
      \infer1[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/forall/elim}]{ \synx \syneq \synx \latwedge (\synx \latvee \synx) }

      \infer2[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/equality/elim}]{ \synx \syneq \synx \latvee \synx }
      \infer1[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/forall/intro}]{ \eqref{eq:def:binary_operation/idempotent} }
    \end{prooftree}
  \end{equation*}

  For \( \latwedge \), the proof is dual.

  \SubProof{Proof that \( \leq \) is a partial order} We will show that \eqref{eq:def:lattice/theory/leq/join} satisfies the axioms for partially ordered sets from \cref{def:partially_ordered_set/theory}.

  To avoid excessive rule applications, we will use \( \synx \latvee \syny \syneq \syny \) directly instead of \( \synx \synless \syny \).

  \SubProofOf*[def:binary_relation/reflexive]{reflexivity}
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{prooftree}
      \infer0[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/forall/intro}]{ [\eqref{eq:def:binary_operation/idempotent}]^{a_1} }
      \infer1[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/forall/elim}]{ \synx \syneq \synx \latvee \synx }
      \infer1[\ref{inf:thm:fol_natural_deduction_equality/symm}]{ \synx \latvee \synx \syneq \synx }
    \end{prooftree}
  \end{equation*}

  \SubProofOf*[def:binary_relation/transitive]{transitivity}
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{ [\syny \latvee \synz \syneq \synz]^v }
      \hypo{ [\synx \latvee \syny \syneq \syny]^u }
      \infer2[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/equality/elim}]{ (\synx \latvee \syny) \latvee \synz \syneq \synz }

      \hypo{ [\eqref{eq:def:binary_operation/associative}]^{a_1} }
      \infer1[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/forall/elim}*]{ (\synx \latvee \syny) \latvee \synz \syneq \synx \latvee (\syny \latvee \synz) }

      \infer2[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/equality/elim}]{ \synx \latvee (\syny \latvee \synz) \syneq \synz }

      \hypo{ [\syny \latvee \synz \syneq \synz]^v }
      \infer2[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/equality/elim}]{ \synx \latvee \synz \syneq \synz }
    \end{prooftree}
  \end{equation*}

  \SubProofOf*[def:binary_relation/antisymmetric]{antisymmetry}
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{ [\synx \latvee \syny \syneq \syny]^u }
      \hypo{ [\eqref{eq:def:binary_operation/commutative}]^{a_1} }
      \infer1[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/forall/elim}*]{ \synx \latvee \syny \syneq \syny \latvee \synx }
      \infer2[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/equality/elim}]{ \syny \latvee \synx \syneq \syny }

      \hypo{ [\syny \latvee \synx \syneq \synx]^v }

      \infer2[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/equality/elim}]{ \synx \syneq \syny }
    \end{prooftree}
  \end{equation*}

  \SubProof{Proof that \eqref{eq:def:lattice/theory/leq/join} and \eqref{eq:def:lattice/theory/leq/meet} are equivalent} We have
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{prooftree}
      \hypo{ [\eqref{eq:def:lattice/theory/leq/meet}]^{a_1} }
      \infer1[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/forall/elim}*]{ \synx \syneq \synx \latwedge (\synx \latvee \syny) }

      \hypo{ [\synx \latvee \syny \syneq \syny]^u }

      \infer2[\ref{inf:def:fol_natural_deduction/equality/elim}]{ \synx \latwedge \syny \syneq \synx }
    \end{prooftree}
  \end{equation*}
  and similarly in the other direction.
\end{defproof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:lattice_via_order_relation}
  Consider a \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set]{partially ordered set} \( (P, \leq) \) in which every pair of elements has a supremum and an infimum.

  Then \( (P, \sup, \inf) \) is a \hyperref[def:lattice]{lattice} whose \hyperref[def:lattice/theory/leq]{order} coincides with \( {\leq} \).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Suprema and infima are obviously associative and commutative as binary operations because ordering is immaterial for pure sets and \( x \vee y \) is defined as \( \sup\set{ x, y } \).

  We will demonstrate the absorption law \eqref{eq:def:lattice/theory/absorption/join}. Let \( l \) be a lower bound of \( x \) and \( y \). Then \( l \leq x \), hence \( \sup\set{ x, l } = x \). Since \( \inf\set{ x, y } \) is itself a lower bound of \( x \) and \( y \), we can conclude that
  \begin{equation*}
    \sup\set{ x, \inf\set{ x, y } } = x.
  \end{equation*}

  The dual absorption law \eqref{eq:def:lattice/theory/absorption/meet} follows by \fullref{thm:order_duality}.

  It remains to validate \cref{def:lattice/theory/leq}, i.e. to show that
  \begin{equation*}
    x \leq y \T{if and only if} \sup\set{ x, y } = y
  \end{equation*}
  and
  \begin{equation*}
    x \leq y \T{if and only if} \inf\set{ x, y } = x,
  \end{equation*}
  both of which are obvious.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Principle of duality for lattices]\label{thm:lattice_duality}
  We can extend \fullref{thm:order_duality} to \hyperref[def:lattice]{lattices} by translating dual function symbols.

  Namely, let \( \tau^\oppos \) be the \hyperref[def:fol_term]{first-order term} obtained via \fullref{alg:fol_term_signature_translation} from \( \tau \) by swapping \( \latwedge \) and \( \latvee \) and, in \hyperref[def:bounded_lattice]{bounded lattices}, by also swapping \( \lattop \) and \( \latbot \). Similarly, let \( \varphi^\oppos \) be the \hyperref[def:fol_formula]{first-order formula} in which we do the same exchanges via \fullref{alg:fol_formula_signature_translation}. If we extend the theory with inequality relation symbols, we must swap them also.

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:lattice_duality/term_assignment} For every lattice \( (L, \vee, \wedge) \), every \hyperref[def:fol_variable_assignment]{variable assignment} \( v \) into \( L \) and every term \( \tau \), we have
    \begin{equation*}
      \Bracks{\tau}_{(L, \vee, \wedge)}^v = \Bracks{\tau^\oppos}_{(L, \wedge, \vee)}^v.
    \end{equation*}

    \thmitem{thm:lattice_duality/formula_assignment} For every lattice \( (L, \vee, \wedge) \), every \hyperref[def:fol_variable_assignment]{variable assignment} \( v \) into \( L \) and every formula \( \varphi \), we have
    \begin{equation*}
      \Bracks{\varphi}_{(L, \vee, \wedge)}^v = \Bracks{\varphi^\oppos}_{(L, \wedge, \vee)}^v.
    \end{equation*}

    \thmitem{thm:lattice_duality/equisatisfiable} A set \( \Gamma \) of formulas is \hyperref[def:equisatisfiability]{equisatisfiable} with \( \Gamma^\oppos \).

    \thmitem{thm:lattice_duality/tautology} The formula \( \varphi \) is a \hyperref[def:fol_semantics/tautology]{tautology} if and only if \( \varphi^\oppos \) is.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{theorem}
\begin{comments}
  \item See also \fullref{thm:boolean_algebra_duality}.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  Straightforward induction.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:def:lattice}
  \hyperref[def:lattice]{Lattices} have the following basic properties:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:def:lattice/dual_order} The induced order in the \hyperref[def:lattice/opposite]{dual lattice} \( L^\oppos \) of \( L \) is the \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set/opposite]{dual order} of that in \( L \).

    \thmitem{thm:def:lattice/operations_preserve_order} If \( x_1 \leq y_1 \) and \( x_2 \leq y_2 \), then \( x_1 \vee x_2 \leq y_1 \vee y_2 \) and \( x_1 \wedge x_2 \leq y_1 \wedge y_2 \).

    \thmitem{thm:def:lattice/distributive_inequality}\mcite[lemma II.5.4]{Birkhoff1967LatticeTheory} For any elements \( x \), \( y \) and \( z \), we have
    \begin{align}
      x \vee (y \wedge z) &\leq (x \vee y) \wedge (x \vee z), \label{eq:thm:def:lattice/distributive_inequality/join_over_meet} \\
      x \wedge (y \vee z) &\geq (x \wedge y) \vee (x \wedge z). \label{eq:thm:def:lattice/distributive_inequality/meet_over_join}
    \end{align}

    \thmitem{thm:def:lattice/modular_inequality}\mcite[lemma II.5.5]{Birkhoff1967LatticeTheory} For any elements \( x \), \( y \) and \( z \), we have
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:def:lattice/modular_inequality}
      x \leq z \T{implies} x \vee (y \wedge z) \leq (x \vee y) \wedge z.
    \end{equation}

    \thmitem{thm:def:lattice/totally_ordered} Every \hyperref[def:totally_ordered_set]{totally ordered set} is a \hyperref[def:lattice]{lattice}.

    \thmitem{thm:def:lattice/morphism_respects_order} If a function between lattices preserves either joins or meets, it is an order homomorphism.

    \thmitem{thm:def:lattice/embedding} Every \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set/morphism]{order embedding} between lattices is a \hyperref[def:lattice/morphism]{lattice embedding}.

    \thmitem{thm:def:lattice/closed_interval} A \hyperref[def:order_interval/closed]{closed interval} in a lattice is a sublattice.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:lattice/dual_order} Straightforward.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:lattice/operations_preserve_order} We will first prove an auxiliary result: if \( x \leq y \), then \( x \wedge z \leq y \wedge z \) and \( z \wedge x \leq z \wedge y \) for any \( z \). This holds because every lower bound of \( x \) and \( z \) is also a lower bound of \( y \) and \( z \).

  Then, if \( x_1 \leq y_1 \) and \( x_2 \leq y_2 \), we have
  \begin{equation*}
    x_1 \wedge x_2 \leq y_1 \wedge x_2 \leq y_1 \wedge y_2.
  \end{equation*}

  Similarly, \( x \leq y \) implies \( x \vee z \leq y \vee z \) and \( z \vee x \leq z \vee y \) for any \( z \) because every upper bound of \( y \) and \( z \) is an upper bound of \( x \) and \( z \).

  Then, if \( x_1 \leq y_1 \) and \( x_2 \leq y_2 \), we have
  \begin{equation*}
    x_1 \vee x_2 \leq y_1 \vee x_2 \leq y_1 \vee y_2.
  \end{equation*}

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:lattice/distributive_inequality} Obviously \( y \wedge z \) is a lower bound of \( y \) and \( z \). \Cref{thm:def:lattice/operations_preserve_order} implies that \( x \vee (y \wedge z) \) cannot exceed \( x \vee y \) nor \( x \vee z \). It is thus a lower bound and cannot exceed the greatest lower bound, so
  \begin{equation*}
    x \vee (y \wedge z) \leq (x \vee y) \wedge (x \vee z).
  \end{equation*}

  This demonstrates \eqref{eq:thm:def:lattice/distributive_inequality/join_over_meet}. \Fullref{thm:lattice_duality/tautology} then implies \eqref{eq:thm:def:lattice/distributive_inequality/meet_over_join}.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:lattice/modular_inequality} Let \( x \leq z \). Then \( z \wedge x = x \). Furthermore, for any \( y \), \eqref{eq:thm:def:lattice/distributive_inequality/meet_over_join} implies that
  \begin{equation*}
    z \wedge (y \vee x) \geq (z \wedge y) \vee \underbrace{(z \wedge x)}_{x}
  \end{equation*}

  Rearranging the terms (as justified by commutativity), we obtain the desired inequality \eqref{eq:thm:def:lattice/modular_inequality}.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:lattice/totally_ordered} Trivial.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:lattice/morphism_respects_order} Suppose that the function \( f: L \to M \) preserves joins. If \( x \leq y \), i.e. if \( x \vee y = y \), then
  \begin{equation*}
    f(x) \vee f(y) = f(x \vee y) = f(y),
  \end{equation*}
  which is equivalent to \( f(x) \leq f(y) \).

  The case where \( f \) preserves meets is analogous.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:lattice/embedding} Let \( f: L \to M \) be an order embedding between lattices. By \cref{thm:def:order_function/embedding_iff_reflecting}, \( f \) is an injective function that is both order-preserving and order-reflecting.

  \SubProof*{Proof that \( f \) preserves joins} Since \( x \vee y \) is an upper bound of \( x \) and \( y \), \( f(x \vee y) \) is an upper bound of \( f(x) \) and \( f(y) \). We will show that it is the least upper bound.

  Let \( u \) be an upper bound of \( f(x) \) and \( f(y) \). Then, since \( f \) is order-reflecting, we have
  \begin{equation*}
    x = f^{-1}(f(x)) \leq f^{-1}(u).
  \end{equation*}

  Thus, \( f^{-1}(u) \) is an upper bound of \( x \) and \( y \), and
  \begin{equation*}
    x \vee y \leq f^{-1}(u).
  \end{equation*}

  Then
  \begin{equation*}
    f(x \vee y) \leq u.
  \end{equation*}

  Since \( u \) was chosen arbitrarily, we conclude that \( f(x \vee y) \) is a least upper bound of \( f(x) \) and \( f(y) \).

  \SubProof*{Proof that \( f \) preserves meets} Analogous.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:lattice/closed_interval} Trivial.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Semilattices}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:semilattices}
  Within this monograph, we often go out of our way in order to handle more general cases. For example, we sometimes prefer \hyperref[def:monoid]{monoids} to \hyperref[def:group]{groups}, \hyperref[def:semiring]{semirings} (with \hyperref[def:semimodule]{semimodules} and \hyperref[def:algebra_over_semiring]{algebras over semirings}) to \hyperref[def:ring]{rings} (with \hyperref[def:module]{modules} and \hyperref[def:algebra_over_ring]{algebras over rings}), as well as abstract \hyperref[def:topological_vector_space]{topological vector spaces} instead of \hyperref[def:euclidean_space]{Euclidean spaces}.

  However, because we lack utility for \hyperref[def:semilattice]{semilattices}, we generally prefer \hyperref[def:lattice]{lattices}. Furthermore, \cref{thm:complete_semilattice} implies that the concept of semilattices only makes sense for lattices that are not \hyperref[def:complete_lattice]{complete}, and most lattices we will encounter will be complete.

  Nevertheless, for the sake of completeness, we state a definition and a few examples.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}\label{def:semilattice}\mcite[22]{Birkhoff1967LatticeTheory}
  We say that a \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set]{partially ordered set} is a \term{join-semilattice} if every pair of elements has a \hyperref[def:extremal_points/supremum_and_infimum]{supremum} and a \term{meet-semilattice} if every two elements have an \hyperref[def:extremal_points/supremum_and_infimum]{infimum}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}\label{ex:def:semilattice}
  We list some examples of \hyperref[def:semilattice]{semilattices}:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{ex:def:semilattice/lattice} Every \hyperref[def:lattice]{lattice} is both a join-semilattice and a meet-semilattice.

    \thmitem{ex:def:semilattice/tree} Consider the \hyperref[def:rooted_tree]{rooted tree} \( T = (V, E, r) \) and the \hyperref[def:rooted_tree/order]{tree order}, with respect to which \( u \leq v \) if \( u \) is an \hyperref[def:rooted_tree/ancestor_descendant]{ancestor} of \( v \).

    Given two vertices \( u \) and \( v \), we are interested in their highest common ancestor. Consider the paths
    \begin{equation*}
      r = u_0 \to u_1 \to \cdots \to u_{n-1} \to u_n = u
    \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*}
      r = v_0 \to v_1 \to \cdots \to v_{m-1} \to v_m = v.
    \end{equation*}

    Let \( k \) be the largest index such that \( u_k = v_k \). This is the desired ancestor --- it is the infimum of \( u \) and \( v \) with respect to the tree order.

    This demonstrates that \( (T, \leq) \) is a meet semilattice. It is generally not a join semilattice.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{example}

\paragraph{Bounded lattices}

\begin{definition}\label{def:bounded_lattice}\mcite[def. 2.12]{DaveyPriestley2002LatticeTheory}
  A \term{bounded lattice} is a \hyperref[def:lattice]{lattice} whose partial order is \hyperref[def:extremal_points/bounds]{bounded}.

  Compared to plain lattices, bounded lattices have the following metamathematical properties:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:bounded_lattice/theory}\mimprovised We can formulate an \hyperref[def:fol_equational_theory]{equational theory} for bounded lattices by extending the \hyperref[def:lattice/theory]{theory of lattices} with two additional axioms for the new function symbols \( \lattop \) and \( \latbot \). In the following table, each column constitutes a choice of axioms:
    \begin{align}
      &\qforall \synx (\lattop \synvee \synx \syneq \lattop),  && \T{or} &&\qforall \synx (\lattop \synwedge \synx \syneq \synx), && \T{or} &&\qforall \synx (\synx \synleq \lattop), \label{eq:def:bounded_lattice/theory/top} \\
      &\qforall \synx (\latbot \synwedge \synx \syneq \latbot) && \T{or} &&\qforall \synx (\latbot \synvee \synx \syneq \synx),   && \T{or} &&\qforall \synx (\latbot \synleq \synx). \label{eq:def:bounded_lattice/theory/bot}
    \end{align}

    All formulas in a single row are \hyperref[def:interderivability]{interderivable} because of the compatibility conditions from \cref{def:lattice/theory/leq}.

    \thmitem{def:bounded_lattice/morphism}\mcite[\S 2.17]{DaveyPriestley2002LatticeTheory} We say that a function between bounded lattices is a \term[en=\( \set{0, 1} \)-homomorphism (\cite[\S 2.17]{DaveyPriestley2002LatticeTheory})]{homomorphism of bounded lattices} if it is a \hyperref[def:lattice/morphism]{lattice homomorphism} that additionally preserves \( \top \) and \( \bot \).

    These are precisely the \hyperref[def:fol_homomorphism]{first-order homomorphisms}, which makes the concepts from \fullref{sec:first_order_structures} applicable.

    \thmitem{def:bounded_lattice/category}\mimprovised As with ordinary lattices, the first-order theory of bounded lattices give rise to a \hyperref[def:fol_theory_model_functor/obj]{category of small models}.

    \thmitem{def:bounded_lattice/subobject}\mimprovised A \term{bounded sublattice} of \( L \) is a \hyperref[def:lattice/subobject]{sublattice} that contains \( \top \) and \( \bot \).

    It follows from \cref{thm:fol_equational_theory_models/substructure} that the bounded sublattices of \( L \) are precisely its \hyperref[def:fol_substructure/submodel]{first-order submodels}, and from \cref{thm:fol_categorical_subobjects} that they are its \hyperref[def:categorical_subobject]{categorical subobjects}.

    Furthermore, as a consequence of \cref{thm:fol_equational_theory_models/image}, the image of a bounded lattice homomorphism is a bounded sublattice of its codomain.

    \thmitem{def:bounded_lattice/trivial}\mimprovised If a bounded lattice has only two elements, these must be \( \top \) and \( \bot \). Any two such lattices are isomorphic as \hyperref[def:universal_objects/initial]{initial objects} in the corresponding category of bounded lattices.

    If the lattice has only one element, then additionally \( \top = \bot \). These are instead \hyperref[def:universal_objects/terminal]{terminal objects}.

    Since initial and terminal objects differ, bounded lattices have no \hyperref[def:universal_objects/zero]{zero object}, and thus there is not \enquote{trivial bounded lattice} in the sense of \cref{def:trivial_object}.

    Instead, to discriminate the two cases, we call them \term{two-element} or \term{one-element} bounded lattices.

    A particularly important two-element bounded lattice is the \hyperref[def:finite_field]{finite field} \( \BbbF_2 = \set{ 0, 1 } \).

    \thmitem{def:bounded_lattice/opposite}\mimprovised We define the \term{dual bounded lattice} of \( (L, \vee, \wedge, \top, \bot) \) by exchanging both the binary operations and the constants, i.e.
    \begin{equation*}
      (L, \vee, \wedge, \top, \bot)^\oppos \coloneqq (L, \wedge, \vee, \bot, \top).
    \end{equation*}

    We say that the two constants are \term[en=dual (constants) (\cite[193]{Gottschalk1953Quaternality})]{dual} and define \( \top^\oppos \) as \( \bot \) and vice versa.

    For a fixed Grothendieck universe, this induces an \hyperref[con:opposite_object]{opposite object functor} in the corresponding category of bounded lattices.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:def:bounded_lattice}
  \hyperref[def:bounded_lattice]{Bounded lattices} have the following basic properties:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:def:bounded_lattice/finite} Every finite lattice is bounded.

    \thmitem{thm:def:bounded_lattice/neutral} \( \top \) (resp. \( \bot \)) acts as a \hyperref[def:semigroup_neutral_element]{neutral element} for \( \wedge \) (resp. \( \vee \)).

    \thmitem{thm:def:bounded_lattice/opposite_order} The induced order in the \hyperref[def:bounded_lattice/opposite]{opposite bounded lattice} \( L^\oppos \) of \( L \) is the \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set/opposite]{opposite} of the induced order of \( L \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:bounded_lattice/finite} Trivial.
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:bounded_lattice/neutral} Restatement of the compatibility axioms in \cref{def:lattice/theory/leq}.
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:bounded_lattice/opposite_order} Follows from \cref{thm:def:lattice/dual_order} by also considering that \( \top \) (resp. \( \bot \)) is the bottom (resp. top) element in the opposite order.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Modular lattices}

\begin{definition}\label{def:modular_lattice}\mcite[13]{Birkhoff1967LatticeTheory}
  We say that a \hyperref[def:lattice]{lattice} is \term[ru=модулярная (решётка) (\cite[def. 4.6]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{modular} if the (\hyperref[def:fol_quantifier_closure]{universal closure} of the) following axiom holds:
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:def:modular_lattice}
    \synx \synleq \synz \synimplies \synx \latvee (\syny \latwedge \synz) \syneq (\synx \latvee \syny) \latwedge \synz.
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item \Cref{thm:def:lattice/modular_inequality} demonstrates that an inequality in \eqref{eq:def:modular_lattice} always holds.
\end{comments}

\begin{example}\label{ex:def:modular_lattice}
  We list examples of \hyperref[def:modular_lattice]{modular lattices}:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{ex:def:modular_lattice/absorption} Whenever the \hyperref[def:fol_variable_assignment]{variable assignment} has the same value for \( \synx \) and \( \synz \), the modular identity \eqref{eq:def:modular_lattice} holds due to absorption. Indeed, with \( \synx \mapsto x \), \( \synz \mapsto x \) and \( \syny \mapsto y \), we obtain
    \begin{equation*}
      x \vee (y \wedge x)
      \reloset {\eqref{eq:def:binary_operation/commutative}} =
      x \vee (x \wedge y)
      \reloset {\eqref{eq:def:lattice/theory/absorption/join}} =
      x
      \reloset {\eqref{eq:def:lattice/theory/absorption/meet}} =
      x \wedge (x \vee y)
      \reloset {\eqref{eq:def:binary_operation/commutative}} =
      x \wedge (y \vee x).
    \end{equation*}

    \thmitem{ex:def:modular_lattice/two_element} The \hyperref[def:bounded_lattice/trivial]{two-element bounded lattice} \( \set{ \bot, \top } \) is clearly modular. Indeed, with the assignment \( \synx \mapsto \bot \), \( \synz \mapsto \top \) and \( \syny \mapsto y \), we have
    \begin{align*}
      \bot \vee (y \wedge \top)
      =
      \bot \vee y
      =
      (\bot \vee y) \wedge \top.
    \end{align*}

    More generally, \cref{thm:def:distributive_lattice/modular} implies that any \hyperref[def:distributive_lattice]{distributive lattice} is modular, and most lattices we will encounter will be distributive.

    \thmitem{ex:def:modular_lattice/pentagon}\mcite[109]{Grätzer2011LatticeTheory} An example of a non-modular lattice is the \term{pentagon}
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:ex:def:modular_lattice/pentagon}\tag{\( N_5 \)}
      \begin{aligned}
        \includegraphics[page=1]{output/ex__def__modular_lattice}
      \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}

    It is not modular because \( a \leq b \), yet
    \begin{equation*}
      a \vee \underbrace{(c \wedge b)}_\bot
      =
      a
      \neq
      b
      =
      \underbrace{(a \vee c)}_\top \wedge b.
    \end{equation*}
  \end{thmenum}
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:modular_lattice_characterization}\mcite[thm. 102(i)]{Grätzer2011LatticeTheory}
  A \hyperref[def:lattice]{lattice} is \hyperref[def:modular_lattice]{modular} if and only if no \hyperref[def:lattice/subobject]{sublattice} is isomorphic to the pentagon lattice \eqref{eq:ex:def:modular_lattice/pentagon}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SufficiencySubProof If \( M \) is a sublattice of \( L \) isomorphic to \( N_5 \), then the modular law does not hold for \( L \) with the same counterexample \cref{ex:def:modular_lattice/pentagon} as for \( N_5 \). Therefore, if \( L \) is modular, \( N_5 \) cannot be isomorphic to a sublattice of \( L \).

  \NecessitySubProof Suppose that \( N_5 \) is not a sublattice of \( L \).

  Aiming at a contradiction, suppose that \( x \leq z \) and we have the following counterexample to the modular law:
  \begin{equation*}
    x \vee (y \wedge z) \neq (x \vee y) \wedge z.
  \end{equation*}

  Then \( y \) is not comparable to neither \( x \) nor \( z \):
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If \( y \leq x \leq z \), then
    \begin{equation*}
      x \vee (\underbrace{y \wedge z}_{y}) = x = (\underbrace{x \vee y}_{x}) \wedge z,
    \end{equation*}
    and we have assumed that the modular law does not hold.

    \item If \( x \leq y \leq z \), then
    \begin{equation*}
      x \vee (\underbrace{y \wedge z}_{y}) = y = (\underbrace{x \vee y}_{y}) \wedge z,
    \end{equation*}
    which is again a contradiction.

    \item If \( x \leq z \leq y \), then
    \begin{equation*}
      x \vee (\underbrace{y \wedge z}_{z}) = z = (\underbrace{x \vee y}_{y}) \wedge z,
    \end{equation*}
    which is again a contradiction.
  \end{itemize}

  Not only is \( y \) not comparable to both \( x \) and \( z \), but, as shown in \cref{ex:def:modular_lattice/absorption}, \( x \) and \( z \) cannot be equal. Therefore, \( L \) has the following pentagonal sublattice:
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:modular_lattice_characterization/pentagonal}
    \begin{aligned}
      \includegraphics[page=1]{output/thm__modular_lattice_characterization}
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}

  But we have assumed that no sublattice of \( L \) is isomorphic to \( N_5 \). The obtained contradiction shows that the modular axiom \eqref{eq:def:modular_lattice} holds for any triple of elements in \( L \). Then \( L \) is modular.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Distributive lattices}

\begin{definition}\label{def:distributive_lattice}\mcite[12]{Birkhoff1967LatticeTheory}
  We say that a \hyperref[def:lattice]{lattice} is \term[ru=дистрибутивная (решётка) (\cite[def. 4.7]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{distributive} if the following axioms hold:
  \begin{align}
    \synx \latvee (\syny \latwedge \synz) &\syneq (\synx \latvee \syny) \latwedge (\synx \latvee \synz), \label{eq:def:distributive_lattice/join_over_meet} \\
    \synx \latwedge (\syny \latvee \synz) &\syneq (\synx \latwedge \syny) \latvee (\synx \latwedge \synz). \label{eq:def:distributive_lattice/meet_over_join}
  \end{align}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item \Cref{thm:def:lattice/distributive_inequality} demonstrates that an inequality in \eqref{eq:def:distributive_lattice/join_over_meet} and \eqref{eq:def:distributive_lattice/meet_over_join} always holds.
\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:def:distributive_lattice}
  \hyperref[def:distributive_lattice]{Distributive lattices} have the following basic properties:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:def:distributive_lattice/modular} Distributive lattices are \hyperref[def:modular_lattice]{modular}.
    \thmitem{thm:def:distributive_lattice/totally_ordered} Every \hyperref[def:totally_ordered_set]{totally ordered set} is a distributive lattice.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:distributive_lattice/modular} Modularity follows from distributivity similarly to how its weaker form \cref{thm:def:lattice/modular_inequality} follows from the distributivity inequalities \cref{thm:def:lattice/distributive_inequality}.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:distributive_lattice/totally_ordered} Let \( (P, \leq) \) be totally ordered. \Cref{thm:def:lattice/totally_ordered} implies that it is a lattice. For any three elements \( x \), \( y \) and \( z \), we have the following possibilities:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If \( x \leq \min\set{ y, z } = y \wedge z \), then
    \begin{equation*}
      x \vee (y \wedge z) = y \wedge z = (x \vee y) \wedge (x \vee z).
    \end{equation*}

    \item If \( x \geq \min\set{ y, z } = y \wedge z \), then
    \begin{equation*}
      x \vee (y \wedge z) = x = \underbrace{(x \vee y) \wedge (x \vee z)}_{\T*{one of the two is} x}
    \end{equation*}
  \end{itemize}

  This shows \eqref{eq:def:distributive_lattice/meet_over_join}. The other law \eqref{eq:def:distributive_lattice/join_over_meet} is shown analogously. Therefore, \( P \) is distributive.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:def:distributive_lattice}
  We list examples of \hyperref[def:distributive_lattice]{distributive lattices}:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{ex:def:distributive_lattice/two_element} The \hyperref[def:bounded_lattice/trivial]{two-element bounded lattice} \( \set{ \top, \bot } \) is distributive because of the bounded lattice laws from \cref{def:bounded_lattice/theory}.

    More generally, every \hyperref[def:heyting_algebra]{Heyting algebra} is distributive, as well as every \hyperref[def:totally_ordered_set]{totally ordered set}.

    \thmitem{ex:def:distributive_lattice/diamond}\mcite[109]{Grätzer2011LatticeTheory} An example of a \hyperref[def:modular_lattice]{modular} but non-distributive lattice is the \term{diamond}\fnote{\textcite{Birkhoff1967LatticeTheory} uses the notation \enquote{\( M_5 \)}, but \textcite{Grätzer2011LatticeTheory} and \textcite{DaveyPriestley2002LatticeTheory} prefer \enquote{\( M_3 \)}}
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:ex:def:distributive_lattice/diamond}\tag{\( M_3 \)}
      \begin{aligned}
        \includegraphics[page=1]{output/ex__def__distributive_lattice}
      \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}

    It is modular as a consequence of \cref{thm:modular_lattice_characterization}. Yet, it is not distributive because
    \begin{equation*}
      a \vee (b \wedge c) = a \vee \bot = a
      \neq
      \top = \top \vee \top = (a \vee c) \wedge (b \vee c).
    \end{equation*}
  \end{thmenum}
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:distributive_modular_lattice_characterization}\mcite[thm. 102(ii)]{Grätzer2011LatticeTheory}
  A \hyperref[def:modular_lattice]{modular lattice} is \hyperref[def:modular_lattice]{distributive} if and only if no \hyperref[def:lattice/subobject]{sublattice} is isomorphic to the diamond lattice \eqref{eq:ex:def:distributive_lattice/diamond}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SufficiencySubProof If \( M \) is a sublattice of \( L \) isomorphic to \( M_3 \), distributivity does not hold for \( L \) with the same counterexample \cref{ex:def:distributive_lattice/diamond} as for \( M_3 \). Therefore, if \( L \) is distributive, \( M_3 \) cannot be isomorphic to a sublattice of \( L \).

  \NecessitySubProof Suppose that \( M_3 \) is not a sublattice of the modular lattice \( L \).

  Aiming at a contradiction, suppose that we have the following counterexample to distributivity:
  \begin{equation*}
    x \vee (y \wedge z) \neq (x \wedge y) \vee (x \wedge z).
  \end{equation*}

  Then \( x \), \( y \) and \( z \) are not pairwise comparable. Indeed, we have the following possibilities:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If all three are comparable, then \( \set{ x, y, z } \) is a chain, which is distributive by \cref{thm:def:distributive_lattice/totally_ordered}. This contradicts our assumption that the distributive laws fail for \( x \), \( y \) and \( z \).

    \item If only two are comparable, say \( x \leq z \), then we obtain the \hyperref[ex:def:modular_lattice/pentagon]{pentagonal lattice} \eqref{eq:thm:modular_lattice_characterization/pentagonal}, which contradicts the modularity of \( L \).

    \item It remains for \( x \), \( y \) and \( z \) not to be comparable. We thus obtain the following diamond:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:distributive_lattice_characterization/diamond}
      \begin{aligned}
        \includegraphics[page=1]{output/thm__distributive_lattice_characterization}
      \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}

    But we have assumed that no sublattice of \( L \) is isomorphic to \( M_3 \) --- again a contradiction.
  \end{itemize}

  The obtained contradiction show that meets always distribute over joins, that is, \eqref{eq:def:distributive_lattice/meet_over_join} holds for any triple of elements in \( L \). The dual law \eqref{eq:def:distributive_lattice/join_over_meet} follows via \fullref{thm:lattice_duality}.

  Therefore, \( L \) is distributive.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{thm:distributive_lattice_characterization}\mcite[thm. 101]{Grätzer2011LatticeTheory}
  A \hyperref[def:lattice]{lattice} is \hyperref[def:modular_lattice]{distributive} if and only if no \hyperref[def:lattice/subobject]{sublattice} is isomorphic to either the pentagon lattice \eqref{eq:ex:def:modular_lattice/pentagon} nor the diamond lattice \eqref{eq:ex:def:distributive_lattice/diamond}.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  Follows from \cref{thm:modular_lattice_characterization} and \cref{thm:distributive_modular_lattice_characterization}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Lattice polynomials}

\begin{definition}\label{def:free_lattice}\mimprovised
  We refer to the \hyperref[def:fol_free_term_model]{free term model} \( F(\mscrX) \) of the theory of (possibly \hyperref[def:bounded_lattice]{bounded} or \hyperref[def:distributive_lattice]{distributive}) \hyperref[def:lattice]{lattices} in the set of indeterminates \( \mscrX \) as the \term[en=free lattice (\cite[36]{Birkhoff1967LatticeTheory})]{free lattice} on \( \mscrX \).

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:free_lattice/polynomial} We call the elements of the term model \term[en=lattice polynomial (\cite[29]{Birkhoff1967LatticeTheory})]{lattice polynomials}, in analogy with \hyperref[def:polynomial_algebra/polynomials]{algebraic polynomials}.

    We will use the familiar functional notation like \( f(X_1, \ldots, X_n) \) to make explicit the indeterminates on which the polynomial \( f \) depends.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item \Cref{thm:integer_polynomials_and_logical_terms} demonstrates how algebraic polynomials with integer coefficients can be regarded as equivalence classes of \hyperref[def:fol_term]{logical terms} in the \hyperref[def:ring/theory]{theory of rings}.

  Similarly, lattice polynomials over countably many indeterminates can be regarded as equivalence classes of logical terms in the \hyperref[def:lattice/theory]{theory of lattices}.

  \item In a \hyperref[def:extremal_points/bounds]{bounded} \hyperref[def:distributive_lattice]{distributive lattice}, we have both algebraic and lattice polynomials --- see \cref{rem:algebraic_and_lattice_polynomials}.
\end{comments}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:algebraic_and_lattice_polynomials}
  Consider the \hyperref[def:bounded_lattice/trivial]{two-element bounded lattice} \( L = \set{ \top, \bot } \). As discussed in \cref{ex:def:distributive_lattice/two_element}, it is \hyperref[def:distributive_lattice]{distributive}.

  We can regard \( L \) as a \hyperref[def:lattice_as_semiring]{join-meet semiring} \( (L, \vee, \wedge) \), which allows us to form the \hyperref[def:polynomial_algebra]{polynomial semiring} \( L[\mscrX] \).

  \hyperref[def:polynomial_algebra/monomials]{Monomials} are simply finite meets, for example \( XY = X \wedge Y \) and \( XZ = X \wedge Z \). These are also \hyperref[def:free_lattice/polynomial]{lattice polynomials}. The monomial \( X^3 = X \wedge X \wedge X \) is not a lattice polynomial (or rather, it is congruent to the lattice polynomial \( L \)).

  On the other hand, the lattice polynomial \( (X \vee Y) \wedge Z \) corresponds to the algebraic polynomial \( (X + Y)Z = XZ + YZ \). Distributivity is essential. If we regard \( L \) as the \hyperref[def:lattice_as_semiring]{meet-join semiring} instead, \( (X \vee Y) \wedge Z \) becomes \( XY + Z \).

  So, while lattice polynomials are equivalent to algebraic polynomials over \( L \), they are qualitatively different.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}\label{def:dual_lattice_polynomial}\mimprovised
  To every \hyperref[def:free_lattice/polynomial]{lattice polynomial} \( f \), there corresponds an \term{dual polynomial}
  \begin{equation*}
    f^\oppos \coloneqq \begin{cases}
      \latbot                         &f = \lattop, \\
      \lattop                         &f = \latbot, \\
      X                               &f = X \in \mscrX, \\
      g^\oppos \latvee h^\oppos   &f = g \latwedge h, \\
      g^\oppos \latwedge h^\oppos &f = g \latvee h.
    \end{cases}
  \end{equation*}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Dual polynomials are closely related to \fullref{alg:propositional_formula_dualization} via \fullref{alg:propositional_formula_to_heyting_term}.
\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:free_lattice_opposite}
  The \hyperref[def:dual_lattice_polynomial]{dual polynomial map} \( (\anon*)^\oppos \) is an isomorphism between a \hyperref[def:free_lattice]{free lattice} and its \hyperref[def:lattice/opposite]{dual lattice}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  The definition of opposite polynomial is adjusted so that joins and meets, as well as bottom and top elements, are exchanged.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Complete lattices}

\begin{definition}\label{def:complete_lattice}\mcite[6]{Birkhoff1967LatticeTheory}
  We say that a \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set]{partially ordered set} is a \term[ru=полная (решётка) (\cite[133]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{complete lattice} if every (possibly empty) subset has a supremum and an infimum. We call these operations \term{join} and \term{meet}, as in lattices, and we use the more general notation \( \bigvee A \) and \( \bigwedge A \) (where \( A \) is a subset). A complete lattice is in particular a \hyperref[def:bounded_lattice]{bounded lattice}.

  As shown in \cref{thm:complete_semilattice}, it the existence of arbitrary joins implies the existence of arbitrary meets.

  Complete lattices can be described in \hyperref[def:second_order_logic]{monadic second order logic} --- see \cref{rem:logical_theory_of_complete_lattices}. We list some metamathematical properties without logical formalities:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:complete_lattice/morphism}\mimprovised We say that a function between complete lattices is a \term{homomorphism of complete lattices} if it preserves arbitrary joins and meets.

    Every homomorphism of complete lattices is obviously a \hyperref[def:bounded_lattice/morphism]{homomorphism of bounded lattices} and, by \cref{thm:def:lattice/morphism_respects_order}, also an \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set/morphism]{order homomorphism}.

    \thmitem{def:complete_lattice/category} \hyperref[def:small_set]{Small} complete lattices and their homomorphisms naturally form a \hyperref[def:category]{category}.

    \thmitem{def:complete_lattice/subobject}\mcite[250]{Grätzer2011LatticeTheory} A \term{complete sublattice} of \( L \) is a subset of \( L \) that closed under arbitrary joins and meets.

    As we will show in \cref{thm:complete_sublattice}, subgraphs correspond to \hyperref[def:categorical_subobject]{categorical subobjects}.

    \thmitem{def:complete_lattice/opposite} The \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set/opposite]{dual} \( X^\oppos \) of a complete lattice \( L \) is again a complete lattice.

    Naturally, we will call it the \term{dual complete lattice}.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:complete_sublattice}
  The \hyperref[def:categorical_subobject]{categorical subobjects} in category of small complete lattices correspond exactly to the subgraphs we have defined in \cref{def:complete_lattice/subobject}:
  \begin{displayquote}
    If \( M \) and \( N \) are complete sublattices of \( L \), the corresponding inclusions \( \iota_M: M \to L \) and \( \iota_N: N \to L \) induce the same categorical subobject of \( L \) if and only if \( M = N \).
  \end{displayquote}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Both \( \iota_M \) and \( \iota_N \) induce categorical subobjects because they are monomorphisms --- \( \varphi \bincirc \iota_M = \psi \bincirc \iota_N \) is equivalent to
  \begin{equation*}
    \varphi(x) = \varphi(\iota_M(x)) = \psi(\iota_N(x)) = \psi(x).
  \end{equation*}

  \SufficiencySubProof Suppose that \( \iota_M \) and \( \iota_N \) induce the same subobjects. Then by definition there exists some isomorphism \( \varphi: M \to N \) such that \( \iota_M = \iota_N \bincirc \varphi \). Thus, for every point \( x \) in \( M \),
  \begin{equation*}
    x = \iota_M(x) = \iota_N(\varphi(x)) = \varphi(x),
  \end{equation*}
  so \( \varphi \) is the inclusion of \( M \) into \( N \). Since \( \varphi \) is bijective, we conclude that \( M = N \).

  We conclude that \( M \) and \( N \) coincide.

  \NecessitySubProof Conversely, if \( M = N \), then \( \iota_M = \iota_N \), so the induced subobjects obviously coincide.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:logical_theory_of_complete_lattices}
  \hyperref[def:second_order_logic]{Monadic second order logic} allows us to formalize \hyperref[def:complete_lattice]{complete lattices}. We start with a translation via \fullref{alg:fol_to_hol} of the \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set/theory]{theory of partially ordered sets}.

  We additionally need two constants --- \( \latvee \) and \( \latwedge \), both inhabiting \( (\syn\iota \synimplies \syn\omicron) \synimplies \syn\iota \). For any \hyperref[def:hol_expression/predicate]{predicate} \( a: \syn\iota \synimplies \syn\omicron \), interpreted as a set, \( {\latvee} a \) is its supremum and \( {\latwedge} a \) is its infimum.

  Constructing a \hyperref[def:hol_theory]{theory} requires encoding these requirements. For example, the statement that \( {\latvee} \syna \) is the least upper bound of \( \syna \) can be encoded via the axiom
  \begin{equation*}
    \qforall {\syna^{\syn\iota \synimplies \syn\omicron}} \parens[\big]{ \underbrace{\qforall {\synx^{\syn\iota}} (\syna \synx \synimplies \synx \synleq {\latvee} \syna)}_{{\latvee} \syna \T*{is an upper bound of} \syna} } \synwedge \parens[\big]{ \qforall {\synb^{\syn\iota}} \parens[\big]{ \underbrace{\qforall {\synx^{\syn\iota}} (\syna \synx \synimplies \synx \synleq \synb) }_{\synb \T*{is an upper bound of} \syna} } \synimplies {\latvee} \syna \leq \synb }.
  \end{equation*}

  In particular, if \( \syna \) is assigned the canonically false predicate, this reduces to
  \begin{equation*}
    \qforall {\synb^{\syn\iota}} ({\synvee} \syna \synleq \synb),
  \end{equation*}
  which validates \cref{thm:def:complete_lattice/extrema}.

  For \( {\latwedge} \syna \), we can add a dual axiom.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:complete_semilattice}\mcite[lemma 34]{Grätzer2011LatticeTheory}
  If a partially ordered set has arbitrary joins (resp. meets), then it also has arbitrary meets (resp. joins).
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item This proposition implies that the concept of \hyperref[def:semilattice]{semilattices} only makes sense for non-complete lattices.

  \item It is not sufficient for non-empty joins or meets to exist --- see \cref{ex:def:semilattice/tree}.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  To obtain the meet of an arbitrary set, we can simply take the join of all its lower bounds.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{ex:def:complete_lattice}
  We list several examples of \hyperref[def:complete_lattice]{(in)complete lattices}:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{ex:def:complete_lattice/rational} \Cref{thm:def:lattice/totally_ordered} implies that the ordered field \( \BbbQ \) of \hyperref[def:rational_numbers]{rational numbers} is a lattice.

    It is not a complete lattice --- \cref{thm:nth_root_is_not_rational} implies that, whenever \( p \) is a prime number, the set
    \begin{equation*}
      \set{ x^2 < p \given x \in \BbbQ }
    \end{equation*}
    has no supremum.

    \thmitem{ex:def:complete_lattice/dedekind_macneille} The \hyperref[def:dedekind_macnielle_completion]{Dedekind-MacNeille completion} of a partially ordered set is a complete lattice.

    \thmitem{ex:def:complete_lattice/extended_real} The set \( \BbbR \cup \set{ -\infty, \infty } \) of \hyperref[def:extended_real_numbers]{extended real numbers} is a complete lattice. Removing the infinite points would make the set unbounded, which in turn would make it incomplete.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:def:complete_lattice}
  \hyperref[def:complete_lattice]{Complete lattices} have the following basic properties:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:def:complete_lattice/extrema} We have \( \bigvee \varnothing = \bot \) and \( \bigwedge \varnothing = \top \).

    \thmitem{thm:def:complete_lattice/distributive_inequality} For any element \( x \) and any family \( \seq{ y_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \), we have
    \begin{align}
      \parens*{ \bigwedge_{k \in \mscrK} x_k } \vee y &\geq \bigwedge_{k \in \mscrK} (x \vee y_k), \label{eq:thm:def:complete_lattice/distributive_inequality/join_over_meet} \\
      \parens*{ \bigvee_{k \in \mscrK} x_k } \wedge y &\geq \bigvee_{k \in \mscrK} (x \wedge y_k). \label{eq:thm:def:complete_lattice/distributive_inequality/meet_over_join}
    \end{align}

    \thmitem{thm:def:complete_lattice/finite} Every finite \hyperref[def:lattice]{lattice} is complete.

    \thmitem{thm:def:complete_lattice/embedding} Every \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set/morphism]{order embedding} between complete lattices is an \hyperref[def:lattice/morphism]{embedding of complete lattices}.

    \thmitem{thm:def:complete_lattice/closed_interval} A \hyperref[def:order_interval/closed]{closed interval} in a complete lattice is a complete sublattice.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:complete_lattice/extrema} In a complete lattice \( L \), every element of \( L \) is an upper bound of \( \varnothing \), so the least upper bound is \( \bot \). Similarly, the greatest lower bound of \( \varnothing \) is \( \top \).

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:complete_lattice/distributive_inequality} Our proof of \cref{thm:def:lattice/distributive_inequality} can be adapted straightforwardly.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:complete_lattice/finite} We can use induction to define joins and meets for arbitrary non-zero subsets of a finite lattice. It remains to note that the join of the empty set is the meet of the entire lattice, and similarly for the meet of the empty set.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:complete_lattice/embedding} Our proof of \cref{thm:def:lattice/embedding} can be adapted straightforwardly.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:complete_lattice/closed_interval} Trivial.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Ideals and filters in lattices}

\begin{definition}\label{def:lattice_ideal}\mcite[32; 34]{Grätzer2011LatticeTheory}
  Fix some \hyperref[def:lattice]{lattice} \( L \). We will define concepts analogous to those from \fullref{sec:semiring_ideals}. In some cases the notions coincide --- see \cref{thm:lattice_ideal_as_semiring_ideal}.

  We call the nonempty subset \( I \) a \term[ru=решёточный идеал (\cite[def. 4.5]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{lattice ideal} if it is closed under joins with elements of itself and under arbitrary meets:
  \begin{align}
    &i \in I \T{and} j \in I \T{imply} i \vee j \in I, \label{eq:def:lattice_ideal/lattice_join} \\
    &i \in I \T{and} x \in L \T{imply} i \wedge x \in I. \label{eq:def:lattice_ideal/lattice_meet}
  \end{align}

  Dually, we call the nonempty subset \( F \) a \term[ru=решёточный фильтр (\cite[def. 4.5]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{lattice filter} if it is closed under joins with elements of itself and under arbitrary meets:
  \begin{align}
    &i \in I \T{and} x \in L \T{imply} i \vee x \in I, \label{eq:def:lattice_ideal/filter_join} \\
    &i \in I \T{and} j \in I \T{imply} i \wedge j \in I. \label{eq:def:lattice_ideal/filter_meet}
  \end{align}

  Both ideals and filters are clearly \hyperref[def:lattice/subobject]{sublattices}. Equivalent conditions are given in \cref{thm:def:lattice_ideal/directed_and_closed}.

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:lattice_ideal/principal}
    \begin{paracol}{2}
      \begin{leftcolumn}
        We define the \term[ru=главный (идеал) (\cite[140]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{principal ideal} of \( x \) as the \hyperref[def:order_interval/unbounded]{closed initial segment}
        \begin{equation*}
          L_{\geq x} = \set{ y \in L \given y \leq x }.
        \end{equation*}
      \end{leftcolumn}

      \begin{rightcolumn}
        Dually, we define the \term{principal filter} of \( x \) as the \hyperref[def:order_interval/unbounded]{closed final segment}
        \begin{equation*}
          L_{\leq x} = \set{ y \in L \given y \geq x }.
        \end{equation*}
      \end{rightcolumn}
    \end{paracol}

    \thmitem{def:lattice_ideal/prime}
    \begin{paracol}{2}
      \begin{leftcolumn}
        We say that a proper ideal \( I \) is \term{prime} if \( {x \wedge y \in I} \) implies \( {x \in I} \) or \( {y \in I} \) (or both).
      \end{leftcolumn}

      \begin{rightcolumn}
        Dually, we say that a proper filter \( F \) is \term[ru=простой (фильтр) (\cite[183]{Гуров2013ТеорияРешёток})]{prime} if \( {x \vee y \in F} \) implies \( {x \in F} \) or \( {y \in F} \).
      \end{rightcolumn}
    \end{paracol}

    \thmitem{def:lattice_ideal/completely_prime}
    \begin{paracol}{2}
      \begin{leftcolumn}\mimprovised
        A stronger condition is for the ideal to be \term{completely prime}, meaning that \( \bigwedge_{k \in \mscrK} x_k \in I \) implies that \( x_k \in I \) for at least one \( k \in \mscrK \).
      \end{leftcolumn}

      \begin{rightcolumn}
        Dually, we call a proper filter \( F \) \term{completely prime} if \( \bigvee_{k \in \mscrK} x_k \in I \) implies that \( x_k \in I \) for at least one \( k \in \mscrK \).
      \end{rightcolumn}
    \end{paracol}

    \thmitem{def:lattice_ideal/maximal}
    \begin{paracol}{2}
      \begin{leftcolumn}\mcite[def. 10.10]{DaveyPriestley2002LatticeTheory}
        We call a proper ideal \term{maximal} if it is \hyperref[def:extremal_points/maximal_and_minimal_element]{maximal} with respect to set inclusion.
      \end{leftcolumn}

      \begin{rightcolumn}
        Dually, we call a proper filter is \term{maximal} if it is maximal with respect to inclusion.
      \end{rightcolumn}
    \end{paracol}
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Completely prime filters are defined inline in \cite[41]{Johnstone1982StoneSpaces}. We dualize this definition for ideals.
\end{comments}

\begin{definition}\label{def:directed_set}\mcite[def. III.1.7]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets}
  We say that a nonempty subset \( A \) of a \hyperref[def:preordered_set]{preordered set} is \term[bg=насочено надясно (множество) (\cite[10]{Проданов1982ФункционаленАнализЧаст1})]{right directed} or simply \term{directed} if every pair of elements of \( A \) has an \hyperref[def:extremal_points/bounds]{upper bound}. Right directed sets are also called \term[en=upward directed (\cite[25]{Johnstone1982StoneSpaces})]{upward directed}.

  Dually, if every pair of elements of \( A \) has a lower bound, we say that \( A \) is \term{left directed} or \term[en=downward directed (\cite[167]{Perrone2024StartingCategoryTheory})]{downward directed}.
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item We do not care how many upper bounds exist and how they are related, we simply need one upper bound to exist for every pair of elements of \( P \).
\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:def:lattice_ideal}
  The \hyperref[def:lattice_ideal]{ideals and filters} of a lattice have the following basic properties:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:def:lattice_ideal/directed_and_closed} A nonempty set is an ideal if and only if it is \hyperref[def:directed_set]{upward directed} and \hyperref[def:closed_ordered_subset]{downward closed}.

    Dually, a set is a filter if it is \hyperref[def:directed_set]{downward directed} and \hyperref[def:closed_ordered_subset]{upward closed}.

    \thmitem{thm:def:lattice_ideal/greatest} An ideal (resp. filter) is \hyperref[def:lattice_ideal/principal]{principal} if and only if it has a greatest element (resp. least element). This element is the generator.

    \thmitem{thm:def:lattice_ideal/prime_complement} The complement of a \hyperref[def:lattice_ideal/prime]{prime ideal} (resp. filter) is a prime filter (resp. ideal).

    \thmitem{thm:def:lattice_ideal/completely_prime_complement} A set is a prime principal ideal (resp. filter) if and only if its complement is \hyperref[def:lattice_ideal/completely_prime]{completely prime} filter (resp. ideal).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  We will prove the statements only for ideals and complements of ideals. The statements for complements of filters will follow via \fullref{thm:lattice_duality}.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:lattice_ideal/directed_and_closed} Fix a lattice \( L \) and a subset \( I \).

  \SufficiencySubProof* Suppose that \( I \) is an ideal.

  \begin{itemize}
    \item It is upward directed because, if \( x \in I \) and \( y \in I \), then, since \( I \) is closed under joins, \( x \vee y \in I \).
    \item It is downward closed because, if \( i \in I \) and \( x \leq i \), then \( x = i \wedge x \in I \).
  \end{itemize}

  \NecessitySubProof* Conversely, suppose that \( I \) is upward directed and downward closed.

  \begin{itemize}
    \item It is closed under joins because if \( x \in I \) and \( y \in I \), then directedness gives us an upper bound \( z \). Then \( x \vee y \leq z \), and since \( I \) is downward closed, we conclude that \( x \vee y \in I \).

    \item It is closed under meets with arbitrary elements of the lattice because if \( i \in I \), then \( x \wedge i \leq i \) and downward closedness implies \( x \wedge i \in I \).
  \end{itemize}

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:lattice_ideal/greatest} Fix a lattice \( L \).

  \SufficiencySubProof* Consider the principal ideal \( X_{\leq a} \). Then \( a \) is an upper bound of \( X_{\leq a} \) contained in \( X_{\leq a} \), that is, the greatest element of \( X_{\leq a} \).

  \NecessitySubProof* Suppose that \( I \) is an ideal with greatest element \( a \). \Cref{thm:def:lattice_ideal/directed_and_closed} implies that \( I \) is downward closed, thus \( X_{\leq a} \subseteq I \). Conversely, \( a \) is an upper bound of \( I \), hence \( I \subseteq X_{\leq a} \).

  We conclude that \( I = X_{\leq a} \).

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:lattice_ideal/prime_complement} Let \( I \) be a prime ideal of the lattice \( L \). Define \( I \coloneqq L \setminus F \). Then \( F \) is a filter:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item For any two elements \( i \) and \( j \) of \( F \), either \( i \wedge j \) is in \( I \) or in \( F \). If we suppose that \( i \wedge j \) is in \( I \), since \( I \) is prime, then either \( i \) or \( j \) must be in \( I \) --- a contradiction. Thus, \( i \wedge j \) is in \( F \).

    \item For any element \( x \) of \( L \) and \( i \) in \( F \), either \( i \vee x \) is in \( I \) or in \( F \). If we suppose that \( i \vee x \) is in \( I \), then, since \( I \) is an ideal, we would obtain that \( i = i \wedge (i \vee x) \) belongs to \( I \) --- again, a contradiction. Thus, \( i \vee x \) is in \( F \).
  \end{itemize}

  Furthermore, let \( i \vee j \) be in \( F \). If both \( i \) and \( j \) are in \( I \), since \( I \) is an ideal, the join \( i \vee j \) must be in \( I \) --- a contradiction. Thus, either \( i \) or \( j \) or both must be in \( F \).

  Generalizing, we conclude that \( F \) is a prime filter.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:lattice_ideal/completely_prime_complement} Consider a subset \( I \) of \( L \) and denote its complement by \( F \).

  \SufficiencySubProof* Suppose that \( I \) be a principal prime ideal with generator \( p \). \Cref{thm:def:lattice_ideal/prime_complement} implies that \( F \) is a filter. We will show that it is completely prime.

  Suppose that the join \( \bigvee_{k \in \mscrK} i_k \) belongs to \( F \). Furthermore, suppose that \( i_k \) belongs to \( I \) for each \( k \in \mscrK \). Then \( i_k \leq p \) for every \( i_k \), and hence
  \begin{equation*}
    \bigvee_{k \in \mscrK} i_k \leq p.
  \end{equation*}

  The latter means that \( \bigvee_{k \in \mscrK} i_k \) belongs to \( I \), which contradicts our assumption. Therefore, there exists at least one index \( k \in \mscrK \) such that \( x_k \) belongs to \( F \).

  We conclude that \( F \) is a completely prime filter.

  \NecessitySubProof* Now suppose that \( F \) is a completely prime filter. \Cref{thm:def:lattice_ideal/prime_complement} implies that \( I \) is a prime ideal. Let
  \begin{equation*}
    p \coloneqq \bigvee I.
  \end{equation*}

  Clearly \( I \) belongs to the principal ideal of \( p \). We will now show the converse.

  Let \( x \leq p \). Suppose that \( x \) belongs to \( F \). Then \( p \) also belongs to \( F \) since \( F \) is upwards closed. But \( F \) is completely prime, thus at least one element of \( I \) must belong to \( F \) --- a contradiction. We conclude that \( x \leq p \) implies that \( x \) belongs to \( I \).

  Therefore, \( I \) coincides with the principal ideal of \( p \).
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:def:lattice_ideal}
  We list examples of \hyperref[def:lattice_ideal]{lattice ideals and filters}:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{ex:def:lattice_ideal/trivial} In a bounded lattice, the simplest ideal is \( \set{ \bot } \) and the simplest filter is \( \set{ \top } \).

    \thmitem{ex:def:lattice_ideal/natural_number_lattice_ideal} Consider the (zero-based) natural number divisibility lattice from \cref{thm:natural_number_divisibility_lattice}, whose join is \( \lcm(a, b) \) and whose meet is \( \gcd(a, b) \).

    For any natural number \( n \), the initial segment
    \begin{equation*}
      D_n \coloneqq \set{ k \in \BbbN \colon k \mid n }
    \end{equation*}
    of all divisors of \( n \) is a lattice ideal in \( \BbbN \), actually the principal ideal of \( n \).

    \begin{figure}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[page=1]{output/ex__lattice_ideals}
      \caption{A Hasse diagram for the divisors of \( 24 \).}
      \label{fig:ex:def:lattice_ideal/lattice}
    \end{figure}

    We can verify that \( D_n \) is a lattice ideal:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item If \( n \) is a common multiple of \( k \) and \( m \), their least common multiple \( \lcm(k, m) \) divides \( n \), thus \( D_n \) is closed under joins.
      \item If \( k \) divides \( n \), then, for any \( m \), the meet \( \gcd(k, m) \) is a divisor of \( k \), hence also a divisor of \( n \) --- thus \( D_n \) is closed under arbitrary meets.
    \end{itemize}

    Furthermore, if \( p \) is a prime number, then \( D_p = \set{ 1, p } \), and \( D_n \) is vacuously a prime ideal.

    \thmitem{ex:def:lattice_ideal/natural_number_lattice_filter} For any natural number \( n \), the principal semiring \emph{ideal}
    \begin{equation*}
      \braket{ n } \coloneqq \set{ 0, n, 2n, 3n, \ldots } = \set{ k \in \BbbN \colon n \mid k }
    \end{equation*}
    is a lattice \emph{filter}, actually the principal filter of \( n \).

    We can thoroughly verify that \( \braket{ n } \) is a lattice filter:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item If \( n \) is a divisor of \( k \), then \( n \) divides \( \lcm(k, m) \) for any \( m \), thus \( \braket{ n } \) is closed under arbitrary joins.
      \item If \( n \) is a common divisor of \( k \) and \( m \), it divides their greatest common divisor \( \gcd(k, m) \), thus \( \braket{ n } \) is closed under meets.
    \end{itemize}

    Furthermore, if \( p \) is a \hyperref[def:prime_number]{prime number}, then \( \braket{ p } \) is a prime lattice ideal because, if \( p \) divides \( \gcd(k, m) \), it also divides \( km \), and \fullref{thm:euclids_lemma} implies that \( p \) divides \( k \) or \( m \).

    \thmitem{ex:def:lattice_ideal/subgroups} Given a \hyperref[def:group]{group} \( G \) and a proper \hyperref[def:normal_subgroup]{normal subgroup} \( N \), consider the \hyperref[thm:fol_lattice_of_substructures]{lattice of subgroups} \( L_G \) of \( G \) and the lattice \( L_N \) of subgroups of \( N \).

    Then \( L_N \) is a sublattice of \( L_G \). In order to verify that it is an ideal, we must show that it is closed under arbitrary meets, that is, \( L_N \) contains the meet \( K \cap H \) whenever \( K \in L_N \) and \( H \in L_G \). But this is trivial since \( K \cap H \subseteq K \subseteq N \).

    By \fullref{thm:lattice_duality}, the sublattice of subgroups containing \( N \) (rather than contained in \( N \)) is a filter (rather than an ideal).

    \thmitem{ex:def:lattice_ideal/maximal_not_prime}\mcite{MathSE:non_prime_maximal_ideal} For non-distributive lattices, maximal ideals may fail to be prime. For example, consider the ideal \( \set{ \bot, a } \) in the diamond \eqref{eq:ex:def:distributive_lattice/diamond}.

    If we add \( b \) or \( c \), we would also need to add \( \top = a \vee b = a \vee c \), and if we add \( \top \), we would need to add \( b = \top \wedge b \) and \( c = \top \wedge c \). Therefore, \( \set{ \bot, a } \) is maximal.

    But it is not prime because it contains \( b \wedge c = \bot \), but neither \( b \) nor \( c \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:lattice_divisibility}
  If we identify a \hyperref[def:extremal_points/bounds]{bounded} \hyperref[def:distributive_lattice]{distributive lattice} with its \hyperref[def:lattice_as_semiring]{join-meet semiring}, the \hyperref[thm:semiring_divisibility_order]{divisibility (pre)order} \( \mid \) coincides with the dual order \( \geq \).
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item We can instead identify the lattice with its meet-join semiring, but then the notions of semiring ideal and lattice ideal would not coincide.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  We will show that \( x \mid y \) if and only if \( x \geq y \).

  \SufficiencySubProof Let \( x \mid y \). Then \( y \) equals \( x \wedge m \) for some \( m \), thus
  \begin{equation*}
    y = x \wedge m \leq x.
  \end{equation*}

  \NecessitySubProof Let \( x \geq y \). Then \( y = y \wedge x \), hence \( x \mid y \).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:lattice_ideal_as_semiring_ideal}
  If we identify the \hyperref[def:extremal_points/bounds]{bounded} \hyperref[def:distributive_lattice]{distributive lattice} with its \hyperref[def:lattice_as_semiring]{join-meet semiring}, then the \hyperref[def:lattice_ideal]{lattice ideals} are precisely the \hyperref[def:semiring_ideal]{semiring ideals}.

  Moreover, \hyperref[def:lattice_ideal/prime]{prime} and \hyperref[def:lattice_ideal/maximal]{maximal} lattice ideals are \hyperref[def:semiring_ideal/prime]{prime} and \hyperref[def:semiring_ideal/maximal]{maximal} semiring ideals.
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item The same holds for \hyperref[def:lattice_ideal]{lattice filters} if we instead consider the \hyperref[def:lattice_as_semiring]{meet-join semiring}.

  \item This holds for \hyperref[def:heyting_algebra]{Heyting} and \hyperref[def:boolean_algebra]{Boolean algebras}.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  Straightforward.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:lattice_prime_element}
  We say that the lattice element \( p \) is \term{prime} if any of the following equivalent conditions hold:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:lattice_prime_element/ideals}\mcite[41]{Johnstone1982StoneSpaces} The \hyperref[def:lattice_ideal/principal]{principal ideal} of \( p \) is \hyperref[def:lattice_ideal/prime]{prime}.

    \thmitem{def:lattice_prime_element/direct}\mimprovised If \( x \wedge y \leq p \), then \( x \leq p \) or \( y \leq p \) (or both).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}
