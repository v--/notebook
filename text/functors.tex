\section{Functors}\label{sec:functors}

\paragraph{Functors}

\begin{definition}\label{def:functor}\mcite[13]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory}
  A \term[ru=функтор (\cite[def. III.1.1]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{functor} \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) between the \hyperref[def:category]{categories} \( \cat{C} \) and \( \cat{D} \) is a pair of functions
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
      F_{\obj}: &\obj(\cat{C}) \to \obj(\cat{D}), \\
      F_{\mor}: &\mor(\cat{C}) \to \mor(\cat{D}),
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}
  such that
  \begin{equation*}
    F_{\mor}(f: A \to B): F_{\obj}(A) \to F_{\obj}(B)
  \end{equation*}
  and with compatibility conditions for identities and composition.

  We will use the same notation for both functions, i.e. we write \( F(A) \) for an object \( A \) and \( F(f) \) for a morphism \( f \). This is imprecise because, as per \cref{rem:disjoint_graph_vertices_and_arcs}, we have not assumed that \( \obj(\cat{C}) \) and \( \mor(\cat{C}) \) are disjoint, and thus \( F(A) \) and \( F(f) \) would differ even if \( A = f \). Nevertheless, this notation reflects popular convention, so we stick to it.

  It remains to state the compatibility conditions, which are collectively called \term[en=functoriality axioms (\cite[def. 1.3.1]{Perrone2024StartingCategoryTheory})]{functoriality}:
  \begin{thmenum}[resume=def:functor]
    \thmitem[def:functor/CF1]{CF1} Functors must preserve identities: for any object \( A \) in \( \cat{C} \), the following equality must hold:
    \begin{equation*}
      F(\id_A) = \id_{F(A)}.
    \end{equation*}

    \thmitem[def:functor/CF2]{CF2} Functors must preserve composition: for any pair of morphism \( f: A \to B \) and \( g: B \to C \) in \( \cat{C} \), the following equality must hold:
    \begin{equation*}
      F(g \bincirc f) = F(g) \bincirc F(f).
    \end{equation*}
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item As mentioned in \cref{rem:category_as_graph}, the notion of functor precedes that of category, and is attributed to \cite{EilenbergMacLane1942NaturalIsomorphisms}. The paper establishes \ref{def:functor/CF1} and \ref{def:functor/CF2}, as well as using the same symbol for both the object and arrow mapping.

  \item There is a closely related notion called a \enquote{contravariant functor} --- see \cref{def:contravariant_functor}.
\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:functor_is_graph_homomorphism}
  If, as per \cref{def:category}, we regard categories as \hyperref[def:directed_multigraph]{directed multigraphs}, the \hyperref[def:functor]{functors} are \hyperref[def:directed_multigraph/morphism]{graph homomorphism} (but not vice versa).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Given a functor \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \), for any morphism \( f: A \to B \) in \( \cat{C} \), we have
  \begin{equation*}
    F(\dom(f)) = F(A) = \dom(F(f)),
  \end{equation*}
  which implies \eqref{eq:def:directed_multigraph/morphism/head}. We also have
  \begin{equation*}
    F(\co\dom(f)) = F(B) = \co\dom(F(f)),
  \end{equation*}
  which implies \eqref{eq:def:directed_multigraph/morphism/tail}.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:endofunctor}\mimprovised
  We call a \hyperref[def:functor]{functor} from a category to itself an \term{endomorphism}.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}\label{ex:def:functor}
  We list examples of \hyperref[def:functor]{functors}.

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{ex:def:functor/power_set} The \hyperref[def:basic_set_operations/power_set]{power set} \( \pow: \cat{Set} \to \cat{Set} \) is a canonical example of an \hyperref[def:endofunctor]{endofunctor}. Explicitly:
    \begin{equation*}
      \begin{aligned}
        &\pow: \cat{Set} \to \cat{Set}, \\
        &\pow(A) \coloneqq \set{ S \given S \subseteq A }, \\
        &\pow(f: A \to B) \coloneqq (S \mapsto f[S]). \\
      \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}

    The nuance here is that the functor sends every function \( f: A \to B \) to another function, which takes a subset \( S \) of \( A \) to its \hyperref[def:set_valued_map/image]{set value} \( f[S] \).

    We must verify that it is indeed a functor. \ref{def:functor/CF2} is satisfied because
    \begin{equation*}
      \pow(g) \bincirc \pow(f) = (S \mapsto g[f[S]]) = \pow(g \bincirc f).
    \end{equation*}

    The condition \ref{def:functor/CF1} is also obviously satisfied.

    \thmitem{ex:def:functor/image_no_category}\mcite{MathSE:image_of_functor_is_not_a_category} If, as per \cref{thm:functor_is_graph_homomorphism}, we regard a functor as a \hyperref[def:directed_multigraph/morphism]{graph homomorphism}, we obtain some notion of an image that generalizes \hyperref[def:set_valued_map/image]{images of functions}.

    This image is a directed multigraph, but in general not a category. As an example, consider the functor \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) from \cref{fig:ex:def:functor/image_no_category}.

    \begin{figure}
      \hfill
      \includegraphics[page=1]{output/ex__def__functor}
      \hfill
      \hfill
      \caption{A functor whose image is not a category.}\label{fig:ex:def:functor/image_no_category}
    \end{figure}

    \begin{itemize}
      \item The solid arrows are the morphisms in \( \cat{C} \) and their images in \( F(\cat{C}) \).
      \item The dashed arrows denote the action of the functor \( F \).
      \item The dotted arrow exists in \( \cat{D} \) as the composition of the other two arrows, however it is missing in the image \( F(\cat{C}) \). Thus, composition is not fully defined in \( F(\cat{C}) \), and \( F(\cat{C}) \) fails to be a category.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{thmenum}
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:def:functor}
  \hyperref[def:functor]{Functors} have the following basic properties:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:def:functor/one_sided_inverses} Functors preserve one-sided inverses: for every functor \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) and every morphism \( f: A \to B \) in \( \cat{C} \) with a left inverse \( g: B \to A \), \( F(f) \) is a left inverse of \( F(g) \).

    Equivalently, if \( g \) is a right inverse of \( f \), then \( F(g) \) is a right inverse of \( F(f) \).

    \thmitem{thm:def:functor/two_sided_inverses} Functors preserve two-sided inverses: For every functor \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) and every isomorphism \( f: A \to B \) in \( \cat{C} \), \( F(f^{-1}) \) is the inverse of \( F(f) \).

    In particular, if \( f \) is an \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/isomorphism]{isomorphisms}, so is \( F(f) \), but the converse only holds for some functors --- see \cref{thm:def:functor_invertibility/fully_faithful_reflects_invertible}.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:functor/one_sided_inverses} Let \( g: B \to A \) be a left inverse of \( f: A \to B \) in \( \cat{C} \). Then
  \begin{equation*}
    F(g) \bincirc F(f)
    \reloset {\ref{def:functor/CF2}} =
    F(g \bincirc f)
    =
    F(\id_A)
    \reloset {\ref{def:functor/CF1}} =
    \id_{F(A)}.
  \end{equation*}

  Thus, \( F(f) \) is a right inverse of \( F(g) \). Since \( g \) is a left inverse of \( f \), automatically \( F(g) \) is a left inverse of \( F(f) \).

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:functor/two_sided_inverses} If \( f^{-1} \) is a two-sided inverse of \( f \), \cref{thm:def:functor/one_sided_inverses} implies that \( F(f^{-1}) \) is both a left and right inverse of \( F(f) \).
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:contravariant_functor}\mcite[33]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory}
  In \cref{def:functor}, we have required a functor \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) to obey \ref{def:functor/CF2}:
  \begin{equation*}
    F(g \bincirc f) = F(g) \bincirc F(f).
  \end{equation*}

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem[def:contravariant_functor/CF2]{CF2\( ^\oppos \)} If we take the \hyperref[def:opposite_category]{opposite category} \( \cat{C}^\oppos \) instead of \( \cat{C} \) (or \( \cat{D}^\oppos \) instead of \( \cat{D} \)), this condition becomes
    \begin{equation*}
      F(g \bincirc f) = F(f) \bincirc F(g).
    \end{equation*}
  \end{thmenum}

  We call this a \term[ru=контравариантный функтор (\cite[def. III.1.1]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{contravariant functor} from \( \cat{C} \) to \( \cat{D} \), and we call the usual notion of functor \term[ru=ковариантный (функтор) (\cite[def. III.1.1]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{covariant}.
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Since a contravariant functor is by definition a covariant functor, to avoid confusion, we only use the term \enquote{contravariant} sparingly.

  \item This definition is an instance of the \hyperref[rem:red_herring_principle]{mathematical red herring principle}.
\end{comments}

\begin{example}\label{ex:def:contravariant_functor}
  We list examples of \hyperref[def:contravariant_functor]{contravariant functors}:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{ex:def:contravariant_functor/dual_space}\mcite[33]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory} We can try to na\"ively define a functor that assigns to a \hyperref[def:vector_space]{vector space} its \hyperref[def:dual_vector_space]{algebraic dual}:
    \begin{equation*}
      \begin{aligned}
        &F: \cat[\BbbK]{Vect} \to \cat[\BbbK]{Vect}, \\
        &F(V) \coloneqq V^*, \\
        &F(f: V \to W) \coloneqq (\varphi: W \to \BbbK \mapsto \varphi \bincirc f).
      \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}

    However, \( F(f) \) is actually a function from \( W^* \) to \( V^* \). This makes \( F \) a contravariant functor or, equivalently, a functor from \( \cat[\BbbK]{Vect}^\oppos \) to \( \cat[\BbbK]{Vect} \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{example}

\paragraph{Category of small categories}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:small_functors}
  A \hyperref[def:functor]{functor} \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \), regarded as a pair \( (F_{\obj}, F_{\mor}) \) of functions, is a \hyperref[def:small_set]{small set} if and only if \( F_{\mor} \) is small (under both interpretation of of function smallness from \cref{rem:small_functions}).
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item The general ambiguity of the term \enquote{small functor} is discussed in \cref{rem:small_functors}.

  Under both interpretations, if \( \cat{C} \) and \( \cat{D} \) are small categories, then \( F \) is a small set.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  \SufficiencySubProof* If \( F = (F_{\obj}, F_{\mor}) \) is a small set, \cref{thm:small_set_closure/indexed_family} implies that so is \( F_{\mor} \).

  \NecessitySubProof* Conversely, suppose that \( F_{\mor} \) is a small set.

  \begin{itemize}
    \item Under the interpretation from \cref{rem:small_functions/triple}, where \( F_{\mor} \) is the triple
    \begin{equation*}
      (\gr F_{\mor}, \mor(\cat{C}), \mor(\cat{D})),
    \end{equation*}
    since \( F_{\mor} \) is assumed to be small, so are \( \mor(\cat{C}) \) and \( \mor(\cat{D}) \) are. \Cref{thm:small_set_closure/category} implies that \( \obj(\cat{C}) \) and \( \obj(\cat{D}) \) are also small.

    Thus, the domain and codomain of \( F_{\obj} \) are small, and hence \( F_{\obj} \) itself is small.

    Then \( F = (F_{\obj}, F_{\mor}) \) is also a small set by \cref{thm:small_set_closure/indexed_family}.

    \item Under the interpretation from \cref{rem:small_functions/graph}, where \( F_{\mor} \) is its graph \( \gr F_{\mor} \), since \( F_{\mor} \) is assumed to be small, so are \( \mor(\cat{C}) \) and the image \( \img F_{\mor} \).

    By the same reasoning as above, we conclude that \( F_{\obj} \) is also small, and so is \( F = (F_{\obj}, F_{\mor}) \).
  \end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:small_functors}
  Depending on how we encode the \hyperref[def:functor]{functor} \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) as a set, there are incompatible ways in which it can be a \hyperref[def:small_set]{small set}, i.e. a member of the \hyperref[def:smallest_grothendieck_universe]{smallest Grothendieck universe} \( \mscrU_0 \). This is an extension of our discussion of small functions from \cref{rem:small_functions}.

  Due to the ambiguity, we will generally avoid speaking about \enquote{small functors}. We do, however, need to consider small \hyperref[def:categorical_diagram]{diagrams}, so we adjust their definition in \cref{def:categorical_diagram/small} specifically so that they can be true small sets. There are many examples where the size of a diagram is essential. For example, a \hyperref[def:complete_category]{complete category} is defined as one in which all limits of small diagrams exist.

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{rem:small_functors/triple} One options is to regard \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) as a pair \( F = (F_{\obj}, F_{\mor}) \) of functions, and, as per \cref{rem:function_definition}, then regard these functions as the triples
    \begin{align*}
      \parens[\big]{ \gr F_{\obj}, \obj(\cat{C}), \obj(\cat{D}) }, && \parens[\big]{ \gr F_{\mor}, \mor(\cat{C}), \mor(\cat{D}) }.
    \end{align*}

    Then \cref{thm:small_set_closure/function} implies that \( F \) is a small set if and only if both \( \cat{C} \) and \( \cat{D} \) are \hyperref[def:small_category]{small categories}.

    Unfortunately, this makes it impossible to define a small functor into a large category like \( \cat{Set} \). This is the reason we adjust our definition of diagram in \cref{def:categorical_diagram} so that the phrase \enquote{small diagram} is unambiguous.

    \thmitem{rem:small_functors/graph} Alternatively, we can define \( F \) as the pair \( (\gr F_{\obj}, \gr F_{\mor}) \) so that \( F \) is a small set if and only if \( \cat{C} \) and its image in \( \cat{D} \) are small categories.

    This makes it possible for a functor into \( \cat{Set} \) to itself be small. Since a diagram can be regarded as a functor, small diagrams simply becomes small functors.

    As noted in \cref{rem:small_category/reflection}, \textcite[26]{Shulman2008SetTheoryForCategoryTheory} discusses how in the axiomatization \logic{ZFC/S}, where a weaker notion of universe is used instead of Grothendieck universes, a functor from a small category to \( \cat{Set} \) may fail to be a small set, and so in some cases it makes sense to have a dedicated notion of small functors.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}\label{def:category_of_small_categories}\mcite[14]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory}
  We define the \term{category of small categories} \( \cat{Cat} \) as follows:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:category_of_small_categories/objects} The \hyperref[def:category/objects]{objects} are, unsurprisingly, the \hyperref[def:small_category]{small category}, i.e. the set of all categories \( \cat{C} \) whose set of morphisms \( \mor(\cat{C}) \) belongs to the \hyperref[def:smallest_grothendieck_universe]{smallest Grothendieck universe} \( \mscrU_0 \).

    \thmitem{def:category_of_small_categories/morphisms} The \hyperref[def:category/morphisms]{morphisms} between small categories are the \hyperref[def:functor]{functors} between them.

    Note that, by \cref{thm:small_functors}, functors between small category are themselves small as sets (under both interpretations of functor smallness from \cref{rem:small_functors}).

    \thmitem{def:category_of_small_categories/composition} The \hyperref[def:category/composition]{composition operation} is defined for \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) and \( G: \cat{D} \to \cat{E} \) componentwise, same as the composition of directed multigraph homomorphisms from \cref{def:directed_multigraph/category/composition}.

    \thmitem{def:category_of_small_categories/identity} The \hyperref[def:category/identity]{identity functor} on \( \cat{C} \) is simply the pair of \hyperref[def:set_valued_map/identity]{identity function}
    \begin{equation*}
      \id_{\cat{C}} = \parens[\big]{ \id_{\obj(\cat{C})}, \id_{\mor(\cat{C})} }.
    \end{equation*}
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}
\begin{defproof}
  To see that \( \cat{Cat} \) is indeed a category, we verify the conditions \ref{def:category/C1} and \ref{def:category/C2}.

  \SubProofOf{def:category/C1} For every pair of small categories \( \cat{C} \) and \( \cat{D} \) and every functor \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \), for every object \( A \) in \( \cat{C} \) we have
  \begin{equation*}
    [\id_{\cat{D}} \bincirc F](A)
    =
    \id_{\cat{D}}(F(A))
    =
    F(A)
    =
    F(\id_{\cat{C}}(A))
    =
    [F \bincirc \id_{\cat{C}}](A)
  \end{equation*}
  and analogously for morphisms.

  Therefore, \( \id_{\cat{C}} \) and \( \id_{\cat{D}} \) satisfy \ref{def:category/C1}.

  \SubProofOf{def:category/C2} Associativity of functor composition is inherited from the associativity of function composition.
\end{defproof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:opposite_functor}\mcite[33]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory}
  We define the \term[ru=двойственный функтор (\cite[138]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{opposite functor} of \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) as
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
      &F^\oppos: \cat{C}^\oppos \to \cat{D}^\oppos, \\
      &F^\oppos(A) \coloneqq A, \\
      &F^\oppos(f^\oppos: B \to A) \coloneqq [F(f: A \to B)]^\oppos.
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item This definition somewhat contradicts the general practice of inverting morphisms when taking opposites. Yet, dual functors arise in relation to the natural \hyperref[con:opposite_object]{opposite object functor} in \( \cat{Cat} \) --- see \cref{thm:opposite_category_functor} --- as well as in the opposites of \hyperref[def:functor_category]{functor categories} --- see \cref{thm:opposite_of_functor_category}.
\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:opposite_category_functor}
  Via \hyperref[def:opposite_functor]{opposite functors}, the \hyperref[def:opposite_category]{opposite category} operation extends to an \hyperref[def:categorical_involution]{involution} in the \hyperref[def:category_of_small_categories]{category of small categories}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Thus, \( (\anon*)^\oppos \) is an opposite object functor in the sense of \cref{con:opposite_object}.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  By definition, for every functor \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \), \( (F^\oppos)^\oppos \) is again a functor from \( \cat{C} \) to \( \cat{D} \).

  For every object \( A \) in \( \cat{C} \), we have
  \begin{equation*}
    [(F^\oppos)^\oppos](A) = A.
  \end{equation*}

  Slightly more subtly, for every morphism \( f: A \to B \) in \( \cat{C} \), we have
  \begin{equation*}
    F^\oppos(f^\oppos) = F(f)^\oppos,
  \end{equation*}
  thus, by noting that \( f = (f^\oppos)^\oppos \), we obtain
  \begin{equation*}
    [(F^\oppos)^\oppos](f) = [F^\oppos(f^\oppos)]^\oppos = (F(f)^\oppos)^\oppos = F(f).
  \end{equation*}
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:subcategory}\mcite[15]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory}
  A \term[ru=подкатегория (\cite[15]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{subcategory} of \( \cat{C} \) is a \hyperref[def:directed_multigraph/subobject]{subgraph} of \( \cat{C} \) that is closed under identities and composition.

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:subcategory/inclusion} For every subcategory \( \cat{D} \) of \( \cat{C} \) there exists an \term{inclusion functor} \( \Iota: \cat{D} \to \cat{C} \), which sends every object and morphism of \( \cat{D} \) to itself in \( \cat{C} \).

    \thmitem{def:subcategory/full} We say that the subcategory \( \cat{D} \) is \term[ru=полная (подкатегория) (\cite[15]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{full} if it contains all morphisms from \( \cat{C} \) whose domain and codomain is in \( \cat{D} \), i.e. if it is an \hyperref[def:induced_subgraph]{induced subgraph}.

    By \cref{thm:def:functor_invertibility/full_subcategory}, this is equivalent to the inclusion being a \hyperref[def:functor_invertibility/full]{full functor}.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:def:subcategory}
  \hyperref[def:subcategory]{Subcategories} have the following basic properties:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:def:subcategory/subobject} The subcategories of a small category \( \cat{C} \) correspond exactly to the \hyperref[def:categorical_subobject]{subobjects} of \( \cat{C} \) in \( \cat{Cat} \). More specifically, if \( \cat{D} \) and \( \cat{E} \) are subcategories of \( \cat{C} \), the inclusions \( \Iota_{\cat{D}}: \cat{D} \to \cat{C} \) and \( \Iota_{\cat{E}}: \cat{E} \to \cat{C} \) induce the same \hyperref[def:categorical_subobject]{categorical subobjects} of \( \cat{C} \) if and only if \( \cat{D} = \cat{E} \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:subcategory/subobject} Follows from the analogous result for \hyperref[def:directed_multigraph]{directed multigraphs} shown in \cref{thm:subgraphs}.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:empty_category}\mcite[10]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory}
  There is an obvious \hyperref[def:category]{category} with no objects. We call it the \term[ru=пустая категория (\cite[16]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{empty category} and denote it by \( \cat{0} \).
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item This is an \hyperref[def:universal_objects/initial]{initial object} in the \hyperref[def:category_of_small_categories]{category of small categories} --- see \cref{thm:universal_categories}.
\end{comments}

\begin{definition}\label{def:discrete_category}\mcite[11]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory}
  A \term[ru=дискретная (категория) (\cite[15]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{discrete category} is a category with no morphisms except for the identities.
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Clearly to any set there corresponds exactly one discrete category and vice versa.

  \item The discrete category is \hyperref[con:free_construction]{free construction}; see \fullref{thm:discrete_category_universal_property}.

  \item The concept is based on \hyperref[def:discrete_topology]{discrete topologies} and is unrelated to Aristotelian discrete variables in the sense of \cref{con:discrete_and_continuous_variables}.
\end{comments}

\begin{definition}\label{def:singleton_category}\mimprovised
  We refer to the \hyperref[def:discrete_category]{discrete category} of a \hyperref[def:singleton_set]{singleton set} \( \set{ A } \) as a \term{singleton category}.

  As shown in \cref{thm:universal_categories}, the singleton category of a \hyperref[def:small_set]{small set} is a \hyperref[def:universal_objects/terminal]{terminal object} in the \hyperref[def:category_of_small_categories]{category of small categories}, so it is also called a \term[en=terminal category (\cite[275]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory})]{terminal category}.

  By \cref{thm:def:universal_objects/unique}, a terminal category is unique up to a unique isomorphism. If we need a fixed representative, we can use the singleton category of \( \varnothing \), which we will denote by \( \cat{1} \).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:universal_categories}
  In the \hyperref[def:category_of_small_categories]{category of small categories}, the \hyperref[def:empty_category]{empty category} is an \hyperref[def:universal_objects/initial]{initial object}, while the \hyperref[def:singleton_category]{singleton category} of any \hyperref[def:small_set]{small set} is a \hyperref[def:universal_objects/terminal]{terminal object}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Fix a small category \( \cat{C} \). The empty functor is the unique morphism from the empty category \( 0 \) to \( \cat{C} \), while the unique morphism from \( \cat{C} \) to a singleton category \( \cat{S} \) sends every object and morphism in \( \cat{C} \) to the the unique object and morphism in \( \cat{S} \).
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Categorical diagrams}

\begin{definition}\label{def:graph_source_and_sink}\mimprovised
  In a \hyperref[def:directed_multigraph]{directed (multi)graph}, we call nodes with \hyperref[def:graph_cardinality/directed_degree]{in-degree} \( 0 \) \term[en=source (\cite[201]{Harary1969GraphTheory})]{sources} and nodes with out-degree \( 0 \) a \term[en=sink (\cite[201]{Harary1969GraphTheory})]{sinks}.
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Using these notions in a \hyperref[def:category]{category} may require ignoring the identity morphisms when determining the in-degree and out-degree --- see \cref{rem:categorical_diagram_definition/functor}.

  \item This definition is given for \hyperref[def:acyclic_graph]{directed acyclic graphs} in \cite[201]{Harary1969GraphTheory} and \cite[231]{Erickson2019Algorithms}.
\end{comments}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:categorical_diagram_definition}
  Diagrams in category theory are tricky to define formally. As an example, fix a category \( \cat{C} \) and morphisms \( f: A \to B \) and \( g_1, g_2: B \to C \) such that
  \begin{equation*}
    g_1 \bincirc f = g_2 \bincirc f.
  \end{equation*}

  This condition can be expressed by saying that the following diagram \enquote{commutes}:
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:rem:categorical_diagram_definition/example}
    \begin{aligned}
      \includegraphics[page=1]{output/rem__categorical_diagrams}
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{rem:categorical_diagram_definition/subgraph} The obvious thing to do is to regard a diagram as a \hyperref[def:directed_multigraph/subobject]{directed multigraph} of \( \cat{C} \). This approach is taken by \textcite[50]{Хелемский2014ФункциональныйАнализ}, who defines a diagram as a collection of objects and morphisms. A similar definition is given by \textcite[21]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий}, who additionally require a diagram to contain the identity morphisms of its objects.

    This has the downside of making it difficult to reason about the \enquote{shape} of \eqref{eq:rem:categorical_diagram_definition/example} in cases like \( A = B \) or \( g_1 = g_2 \).

    \thmitem{rem:categorical_diagram_definition/homomorphism} So, instead, we can take the \hyperref[def:directed_multigraph]{directed multigraph}
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:rem:categorical_diagram_definition/shape}
      \begin{aligned}
        \includegraphics[page=2]{output/rem__categorical_diagrams}
      \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    and define a diagram in \( \cat{C} \) as a \hyperref[def:directed_multigraph/morphism]{homomorphism} from \( G \) to \( U(\cat{C}) \), the underlying graph of \( \cat{C} \). Thus, a diagram is a \hyperref[def:labeled_set]{labeling} of \( G \) with objects and morphisms from \( \cat{C} \).

    \Textcite[10; 41]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory} and \textcite[def. 6.1.1]{Schubert1972Categories} calls directed multigraphs \enquote{diagram schemes} and define a diagram \enquote{of type \( G \)} as a homomorphism of \( G \) into \( U(\cat{C}) \).

    \thmitem{rem:categorical_diagram_definition/commutativity} Expressing that the diagram \eqref{eq:rem:categorical_diagram_definition/example} commutes then amounts to evaluating paths. All aforementioned authors call a diagram \( D \) \enquote{commutative} if every pair of \hyperref[def:graph_walk/path]{paths}\fnote{It is irrelevant here whether we consider paths or more general directed walks.}
    \begin{align*}
      &A \reloset {f_1} \to \anon \reloset {f_2} \to \cdots \reloset {f_{n-1}} \to \anon \reloset {f_n} \to B, \\
      &A \reloset {g_1} \to \anon \reloset {g_2} \to \cdots \reloset {g_{n-1}} \to \anon \reloset {g_m} \to B
    \end{align*}
    with the same endpoints evaluate to the same morphism in \( \cat{C} \):
    \begin{equation*}
      D(f_n) \bincirc \cdots \bincirc D(f_1) = D(g_m) \bincirc \cdots \bincirc D(g_1).
    \end{equation*}

    For our diagram \eqref{eq:rem:categorical_diagram_definition/example}, this suggests that
    \begin{equation*}
      g_1 \bincirc f = D(b_1) \bincirc D(a) = D(b_2) \bincirc D(a) = g_2 \bincirc f,
    \end{equation*}
    as desired, but also that
    \begin{equation*}
      g_1 = D(b_1) = D(b_2) = g_2.
    \end{equation*}

    The undesired second equality is overlooked by the authors mentioned earlier. A simple fix is to only consider the paths from \hyperref[def:graph_source_and_sink]{sources} to sinks, which in this case precludes paths from \( v \) to \( w \).

    In this case it is important for \( G \) not to contain loops at sources and sinks.

    \thmitem{rem:categorical_diagram_definition/functor} \Textcite[def. 1.4.21]{Perrone2024StartingCategoryTheory} defines a diagram in \( \cat{C} \) as a \hyperref[def:functor]{functor} \( D: \cat{I} \to \cat{C} \) from some \hyperref[def:small_category]{small category} \( \cat{I} \).

    For our example \eqref{eq:rem:categorical_diagram_definition/example}, we can take \( \cat{I} \) to be the \hyperref[def:directed_multigraph_free_category]{free category} \( F(G) \) (i.e. the category whose morphisms are \hyperref[def:graph_walk/directed]{directed walks} in \( G \)).

    The objects in \( \cat{I} \) are the nodes \( u \), \( v \) and \( w \) of \( G \), while the morphisms in \( \cat{I} \) are the empty walks
    \begin{align*}
      &\id_u,                                   && \id_v,                                   && \id_w, \\
    \intertext{as well as}
      &u \reloset a \to v,                      && v \reloset {b_1} \to w,                  && v \reloset {b_2} \to w, \\
      &                                         && u \reloset a \to v \reloset {b_1} \to w, && u \reloset a \to v \reloset {b_2} \to w.
    \end{align*}

    Commutativity requires that
    \begin{equation*}
      g_1 \bincirc f
      =
      D(b_1) \bincirc D(a)
      \reloset {\ref{def:functor/CF2}} =
      D(b_1 \bincirc a)
      =
      D(b_2 \bincirc a)
      \reloset {\ref{def:functor/CF2}} =
      D(b_2) \bincirc D(a)
      =
      g_2 \bincirc f.
    \end{equation*}

    Thus, the condition for commutativity becomes simpler --- for every source \( u \) and every sink \( w \) \emph{in \( G \)}, all morphisms between them \emph{in \( \cat{I} = F(G) \)} must coincide.

    Note that \( F(G) \) contains a loop at \( u \), so it is important to ignore it when determining whether \( u \) is a source. Thus, we still need the particular structure of \( G \). Instead of trying to recover \( G \) from \( \cat{I} \), it makes sense to define diagrams in \( \cat{C} \) as functors from the free category of \( G \) to \( \cat{C} \).

    Alternatively, we could ignore the identity morphisms when determining the in-degree of \( u \) or out-degree of \( v \).

    \thmitem{rem:categorical_diagram_definition/small} We discuss in \cref{rem:small_functors} how there are multiple interpretations under which a functor can be a \hyperref[def:small_set]{small set}.

    This shows that the definition of a diagram as a functor \( D: F(G) \to \cat{C} \) also has some drawbacks.

    Thus, in \cref{def:categorical_diagram}, we define a diagram in \( \cat{C} \) of shape \( G = (V, A, h, t) \) as a pair
    \begin{equation*}
      D = \parens[\big]{ \seq{ D_v^{\obj} }_{v \in V}, \seq{ D_a^{\mor} }_{a \in A} }
    \end{equation*}
    of indexed families that act as the \hyperref[def:set_valued_map/graph]{function graphs} of the corresponding \hyperref[def:function]{functions}, i.e. they must satisfy
    \begin{align*}
      \dom D_a^{\mor} = D_{h(a)}^{\obj} && \co\dom D_a^{\mor} = D_{t(a)}^{\obj}.
    \end{align*}

    Then \cref{thm:small_set_closure} implies that \( D \) is a small set if and only if both its shape \( G \) and each object \( D_v^{\obj} \) and morphism \( D_f^{\mor} \) are small.

    The structure of a diagram as a graph homomorphism, as a functor, and as a subgraph can easily be recovered from this construction.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}\label{def:categorical_diagram}\mimprovised
  In accordance with all considerations from \cref{rem:categorical_diagram_definition}, in the \hyperref[def:category]{category} \( \cat{C} \), we define a \term[ru=диаграмма (\cite[50]{Хелемский2014ФункциональныйАнализ}), en=diagram (\cite[51]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory})]{diagram} of \term[en=shape (\cite[51]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory})]{shape} \( G \), where \( G = (V, A, h, t) \) is a \hyperref[def:directed_multigraph]{directed multigraph}, as a pair
  \begin{equation*}
    D = \parens[\big]{ \seq{ D_v^{\obj} }_{v \in V}, \seq{ D_a^{\mor} }_{a \in A} }
  \end{equation*}
  of indexed families such that
  \begin{align*}
    \dom D_a^{\mor} = D_{h(a)}^{\obj} && \co\dom D_a^{\mor} = D_{t(a)}^{\obj}.
  \end{align*}

  We call the graph \( G \) a \term[en=diagram scheme (\cite[48]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory})]{diagram scheme}.

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:categorical_diagram/functor} \Fullref{thm:free_category_universal_property} allows us to lift \( D \) to a \hyperref[def:functor]{functor}
    \begin{equation*}
      F(D): F(G) \to \cat{C}
    \end{equation*}
    from the \hyperref[def:directed_multigraph_free_category]{free category} \( F(G) \) to \( \cat{C} \).

    In analogy with indexed families of sets, we we call \( F(G) \) the \term{index category} of \( F(D) \). If we need to recover \( G \) from an abstract index category \( \cat{I} \), we can take the smallest subgraph of \( U(\cat{I}) \) such that \( F(G) = \cat{I} \).

    When the distinction is not important, we will conflate \( D \) and \( F(D) \), just like we conflate indexed families and the functions they induce.

    \thmitem{def:categorical_diagram/commutative} We say that the diagram \( D \) is \term[ru=коммутативная диаграмма (\cite[50]{Хелемский2014ФункциональныйАнализ}), en=commutative diagram (\cite[def. 6.2.5]{Schubert1972Categories})]{commutative} if, for any \hyperref[def:graph_source_and_sink]{source} \( u \) and sink \( v \), all \hyperref[def:graph_walk/path]{paths} from \( u \) to \( v \) in \( G \) evaluate to the same morphism in \( \cat{C} \), i.e. have the same image under \( F(D) \).

    We discuss this condition in more detail in \cref{rem:categorical_diagram_definition/commutativity}.

    \thmitem{def:categorical_diagram/small} In particular, we call the diagram \( D \) \term[en=small diagram (\cite[109]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory})]{small} if \( G \) (or, equivalently, \( F(G) \)) is a small set, and if the relevant objects and morphisms in \( \cat{C} \) are small.

    The latter condition is usually implicit since most categories we deal with, as as \( \cat{Set} \), \( \cat{Grp} \), \( \cat{Top} \) or \( \cat{Cat} \), consist of small objects.

    As per our discussion in \cref{rem:categorical_diagram_definition/small}, because of our choice to define a diagram as a pair of indexed families, this condition coincides with \( D \) being a small set.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Functor invertibility}

\begin{definition}\label{def:functor_invertibility}\mcite[def. 7.1]{Awodey2010CategoryTheory}
  Unlike \hyperref[def:function]{functions}, which can be \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/injective]{injective} and \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/injective]{surjective}, \hyperref[def:functor]{functors} have richer options:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:functor_invertibility/injective} We say that the functor \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) is \term{injective on objects} (resp. \term[en=injective on arrows (\cite[def. 7.1]{Awodey2010CategoryTheory})]{injective on morphisms}) if its restriction to objects (resp. morphisms) is injective; that is, if \( F(A) = F(B) \) implies \( A = B \).

    A functor that is injective on morphisms is also called an \term[en=embedding (\cite[def. 4.1.3]{Schubert1972Categories})]{embedding} because of \cref{thm:small_functor_cancellation/left}.

    \thmitem{def:functor_invertibility/faithful} If, for every pair of objects \( A \) and \( B \) in \( \cat{C} \), the restriction of \( F \) to \( \cat{C}(A, B) \) is injective, we say that \( F \) is \term[ru=унивалентный (функтор) (\cite[132]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{faithful}.

    \thmitem{def:functor_invertibility/surjective} We say that \( F \) is \term{surjective on objects} (resp. \term[en=surjective on arrows (\cite[def. 7.1]{Awodey2010CategoryTheory})]{surjective on morphisms}) if its restriction to objects (resp. morphisms) is surjective; that is, if for every object \( Y \) in \( \cat{D} \), there exist some object \( A \) in \( \cat{C} \) such that \( F(A) = Y \).

    \thmitem{def:functor_invertibility/bijective}\mimprovised Naturally, we say that \( F \) is \term{bijective on objects} (resp. \term{bijective on morphisms}) if its restriction to objects (resp. morphisms) is bijective.

    \thmitem{def:functor_invertibility/essentially_surjective}\mcite[def. 1.5.5]{Perrone2024StartingCategoryTheory} We say that \( F \) is \term{essentially surjective} if it is surjective on objects up to an isomorphism: for every object \( Y \) in \( \cat{D} \), there exists some object \( A \) in \( \cat{C} \) such that \( F(A) \cong Y \).

    \thmitem{def:functor_invertibility/full} If, for every pair of objects \( A \) and \( B \) in \( \cat{C} \), the restriction of \( F \) to \( \cat{C}(A, B) \) is surjective, we say that \( F \) is \term[ru=полный (функтор) (\cite[131]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{full}.

    \thmitem{def:functor_invertibility/fully_faithful}\mcite[def. 1.5.5]{Perrone2024StartingCategoryTheory} Finally, if \( F \) is both faithful and fully, we call it \term{fully faithful}.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:commutative_diagrams_preserved_and_reflected}
  \hyperref[def:functor]{Functors} preserve \hyperref[def:categorical_diagram/commutative]{commutative diagrams}. \hyperref[def:functor_invertibility/faithful]{Faithful} functors also reflect commutative diagrams.

  More explicitly, let \( \cat{C} \) be an arbitrary category, let \( D \) be a diagram in \( \cat{C} \), and let
  \begin{align*}
    &A \reloset {f_1} \to \anon \reloset {f_2} \to \cdots \reloset {f_{n-1}} \to \anon \reloset {f_n} \to B, \\
    &A \reloset {g_1} \to \anon \reloset {g_2} \to \cdots \reloset {g_{n-1}} \to \anon \reloset {g_m} \to B
  \end{align*}
  be two \hyperref[def:graph_walk/directed]{directed walks} with the same endpoints in \( D \).

  For any functor \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \), if
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:commutative_diagrams_preserved_and_reflected/source}
    f_n \bincirc \cdots \bincirc \bincirc f_1 = g_m \bincirc \cdots \bincirc \bincirc g_1,
  \end{equation}
  then
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:commutative_diagrams_preserved_and_reflected/image}
    F(f_n) \bincirc \cdots \bincirc F(f_1) = F(g_m) \bincirc \cdots \bincirc \bincirc F(g_1),
  \end{equation}

  Conversely, if \( F \) is faithful, then \eqref{eq:thm:commutative_diagrams_preserved_and_reflected/image} implies \eqref{eq:thm:commutative_diagrams_preserved_and_reflected/source}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Functors preserve composition by \ref{def:functor/CF2}, hence \eqref{eq:thm:commutative_diagrams_preserved_and_reflected/image} follows from \eqref{eq:thm:commutative_diagrams_preserved_and_reflected/source} directly.

  Now suppose that \eqref{eq:thm:commutative_diagrams_preserved_and_reflected/image} holds for a faithful functor \( F \). \ref{def:functor/CF2} allows us to reduce \eqref{eq:thm:commutative_diagrams_preserved_and_reflected/image} to
  \begin{equation*}
    F(f_n \bincirc \cdots \bincirc f_1) = F(g_m \bincirc \cdots \bincirc \bincirc g_1).
  \end{equation*}

  Then, by injectivity of \( F \) on the \( \hom \)-set \( \cat{C}(A, B) \), \eqref{eq:thm:commutative_diagrams_preserved_and_reflected/source} holds.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:def:functor_invertibility}
  The notions of functor invertibility from \cref{def:functor_invertibility} have the following basic properties:

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:def:functor_invertibility/injective} A functor is injective on morphisms if and only if it is both injective on objects and faithful.

    \thmitem{thm:def:functor_invertibility/surjective} A functor is surjective on morphisms if and only if it is both surjective on objects and full.

    \thmitem{thm:def:functor_invertibility/bijective} A functor is bijective on morphisms if and only if it is bijective on objects and fully faithful.

    \thmitem{thm:def:functor_invertibility/subcategory_inclusion} Every \hyperref[def:subcategory/inclusion]{subcategory inclusion functor} is an embedding (i.e. is injective on morphisms).

    \thmitem{thm:def:functor_invertibility/full_subcategory} A subcategory \( \cat{D} \) of \( \cat{C} \) is \hyperref[def:subcategory]{full} (i.e. an \hyperref[def:induced_subgraph]{induced subgraph}) if and only if the inclusion \( \Iota: \cat{D} \to \cat{C} \) is a full functor (i.e. surjective on every \( \hom \)-set).

    \thmitem{thm:def:functor_invertibility/faithful_reflects_composition} If \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) is faithful, it reflects composition: \( F(h) = F(g) \bincirc F(f) \) implies that \( h = g \bincirc f \).

    \thmitem{thm:def:functor_invertibility/faithful_reflects_cancellation} If \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) is faithful, it reflects monomorphisms and epimorphisms: if \( F(f) \) is a monomorphism (resp. epimorphism), so is \( f \).

    In particular, if \( F(f) \) is a bimorphism, so is \( f \).

    \thmitem{thm:def:functor_invertibility/faithful_reflects_identities} If \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) is faithful, it reflects identities: if \( F(f) = \id_{F(A)} \), then \( f = \id_A \).

    \thmitem{thm:def:functor_invertibility/fully_faithful_reflects_invertible} If \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) is fully faithful, it reflects split monomorphisms and epimorphisms: if \( F(f) \) has a left or right inverse, so does \( f \).

    In particular, if \( F(f) \) is an isomorphism, so is \( f \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:functor_invertibility/injective}
  \SufficiencySubProof* Suppose that \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) is injective on morphisms. It is trivially faithful since faithfulness is a more restrictive condition.

  To see that \( F \) is injective on objects, suppose that \( F(A) = F(B) \). Then \( \id_{F(A)} = \id_{F(B)} \) and hence
  \begin{equation*}
    F(\id_A)
    \reloset {\ref{def:functor/CF2}} =
    \id_{F(A)}
    =
    \id_{F(B)}
    \reloset {\ref{def:functor/CF2}} =
    F(\id_B).
  \end{equation*}

  Since \( F \) is injective on morphisms, it follows that \( \id_A = \id_B \), hence \( A = B \). Thus, \( F \) is injective on objects.

  \NecessitySubProof* Let \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) be faithful and injective on objects. Let \( f: A \to B \) and \( g: C \to D \) be morphisms in \( \cat{C} \) such that \( F(f) = F(g) \).

  Then both \( F(f) \) and \( F(g) \) have the same domain \( F(A) = F(C) \) and codomain \( F(B) = F(D) \). Hence, since \( F \) is injective on objects, we have \( A = C \) and \( B = D \).

  Thus, \( f \) and \( g \) are both morphisms from \( A \) to \( B \). Since \( F \) is also faithful, from \( F(f) = F(g) \) it follows that \( f = g \).

  Therefore, \( F \) is injective on morphisms.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:functor_invertibility/surjective}
  \SufficiencySubProof* Suppose that \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) is surjective on morphisms. It is trivially full since fullness is a more restrictive condition.

  To see that \( F \) is surjective on objects, fix \( Y \in \cat{D} \). Then there exists some morphism \( f: A \to B \) in \( \cat{C} \) such that \( F(f) = \id_Y \). We thus necessarily have \( F(A) = Y = F(B) \).

  \NecessitySubProof* Let \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) be full and injective on objects. Let \( g: C \to D \) be a morphism in \( \cat{D} \).

  Since \( F \) is surjective on objects, there exists preimages \( A \) of \( C \) and \( B \) of \( D \) under \( F \). Thus, \( g \in \cat{D}(F(A), F(B)) \).

  Since \( F \) is also full, there exists some morphism \( f: A \to B \) such that \( F(f) = g \).

  Therefore, \( F \) is surjective on morphisms.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:functor_invertibility/bijective} Follows from \cref{thm:def:functor_invertibility/injective} and \cref{thm:def:functor_invertibility/surjective}.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:functor_invertibility/subcategory_inclusion} Trivial.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:functor_invertibility/full_subcategory} Restatement.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:functor_invertibility/faithful_reflects_composition} Suppose that \( F \) is faithful and that \( F(h) = F(g) \bincirc F(f) \), where \( f: A \to B \) and \( g: B \to C \).

  Since \( F \) is faithful and thus injective on the \( \hom \)-set \( \cat{C}(A, C) \), the equality
  \begin{equation*}
    \underbrace{F(g) \bincirc F(f)}_{F(g \bincirc f)} = F(h)
  \end{equation*}
  implies that \( g \bincirc f = h \).

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:functor_invertibility/faithful_reflects_cancellation} Suppose that \( F \) is faithful and that \( F(g) \) is a monomorphism and let \( f_1, f_2: A \to B \) be parallel morphisms in \( \cat{C} \) such that
  \begin{equation*}
    g \bincirc f_1 = g \bincirc f_2.
  \end{equation*}

  Then, since \( F(g) \) is a monomorphism, we have that \( F(f_1) = F(f_2) \). Since \( F \) is faithful, its restriction to \( \cat{C}(A, B) \) is injective, thus \( f_1 = f_2 \).

  The proof when \( F(g) \) is an epimorphism follows by duality.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:functor_invertibility/faithful_reflects_identities} Suppose that \( F \) is fully faithful and that \( F(f) = \id_{F(A)} \).

  By \ref{def:functor/CF1}, \( \id_{F(A)} = F(\id_A) \) so, since \( F \) is faithful, \( F(f) = F(\id_A) \) implies \( f = \id_A \).

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:functor_invertibility/fully_faithful_reflects_invertible} Suppose that \( F \) is fully faithful. Suppose that, for some \( f: A \to B \), \( F(f) \) is left invertible.

  Let \( q \) be a left inverse of \( F(f) \). Since \( F \) is full, there exists a morphism \( g: B \to A \) such that \( F(g) = q \). Since
  \begin{equation*}
    F(g) \bincirc F(f) = \id_{F(A)},
  \end{equation*}
  \cref{thm:def:functor_invertibility/faithful_reflects_identities} implies that
  \begin{equation*}
    g \bincirc f = \id_A.
  \end{equation*}

  Therefore, \( g \) is a left inverse of \( f \).

  By duality, we conclude that \( F \) reflects right inverses. From \cref{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/left_and_right} it follows that if \( F(f) \) is an isomorphism, so is \( f \).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:small_functor_cancellation}
  In the \hyperref[def:category_of_small_categories]{category of small categories}, \hyperref[def:morphism_cancellation]{morphism cancellation} is related to the notions of functor invertibility from \cref{def:functor_invertibility} as follows:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:small_functor_cancellation/left} A functor is left cancellable if and only if it is an embedding (injective on morphisms).

    \thmitem{thm:small_functor_cancellation/right} If a functor is right cancellable, it is surjective on objects (but, as shown in \cref{ex:def:functor_invertibility/non_surjective_epimorphism}, it may not be surjective on morphisms).

    Conversely, if a functor is surjective on morphisms, it is right cancellable (but this is not a necessary condition).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:small_functor_cancellation/left} We proceed similarly to \cref{thm:function_invertibility_categorical/left_cancellable} for functions.

  \SufficiencySubProof* Suppose that \( G: \cat{D} \to \cat{E} \) is left cancellable and that \( G(f) = G(g) \) for some \( f, g: A \to B \).

  Let \( F: \cat{D} \to \cat{D} \) be the modification of \( \id_{\cat{D}} \) that exchanges \( f \) and \( g \). Then
  \begin{equation*}
    G \bincirc \id_{\cat{D}} = G \bincirc F,
  \end{equation*}
  so \( \id_{\cat{D}} = F \) and
  \begin{equation*}
    f = \id_{\cat{D}}(f) = F(f) = g.
  \end{equation*}

  \NecessitySubProof* Suppose that \( G: \cat{D} \to \cat{E} \) is injective on morphisms and that
  \begin{equation*}
    G \bincirc F_1 = G \bincirc F_2.
  \end{equation*}

  Thus, for any pair \( f, g: A \to B \), we have
  \begin{equation*}
    G(F_1(f)) = G(F_2(g)).
  \end{equation*}

  By injectivity of \( G \), we have \( F_1(f) = F_2(g) \). Since \( f \) and \( g \) were arbitrary, we conclude that \( F_1 = F_2 \), as desired.

  Therefore, \( G \) is left cancellable.

  \SubProofOf{thm:small_functor_cancellation/right}

  \SufficiencySubProof* Suppose that \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) is right cancellable.

  Suppose that \( F \) is not surjective on objects. Then there exists some object \( X \) in \( \cat{D} \) with an empty preimage under \( F \).

  Fix an object \( A \) from \( \cat{C} \) and let \( G \) be the identity functor on \( \cat{D} \) modified to send \( X \) to \( F(A) \).

  Then
  \begin{equation*}
    G \bincirc F = \id_{\cat{D}} \bincirc F,
  \end{equation*}
  so \( G = \id_{\cat{D}} \), which in turn implies that \( X = F(A) \), contradicting the choice of \( X \).

  Therefore, \( F \) must be surjective on objects.

  \NecessitySubProof* Suppose that \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) is surjective on morphisms and that, for some functors \( G_1, G_2: \cat{D} \to \cat{E} \), we have
  \begin{equation*}
    G_1 \bincirc F = G_2 \bincirc F.
  \end{equation*}

  Every object \( X \) in \( \cat{D} \) has some preimage \( A \) in \( \cat{C} \), so
  \begin{equation*}
    G_1(X) = G_1(F(A)) = G_2(F(A)) = G_2(X),
  \end{equation*}
  and similarly for morphisms.

  Thus, \( G_1 = G_2 \), showing that \( F \) is right cancellable.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:small_functor_invertibility}
  In the \hyperref[def:category_of_small_categories]{category of small categories}, \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility]{morphism invertibility} is related to the notions of functor invertibility from \cref{def:functor_invertibility} as follows:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:small_functor_invertibility/left} If a functor is left invertible, it is injective on morphisms.

    \thmitem{thm:small_functor_invertibility/right} If a functor is right invertible, it is surjective on morphisms.

    \thmitem{thm:small_functor_invertibility/two_sided} A functor is invertible if and only if it is bijective on morphisms.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:small_functor_invertibility/left} If \( G: \cat{D} \to \cat{C} \) is a left inverse of \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) in \( \cat{Cat} \), then \( F(f) = F(g) \) implies
  \begin{equation*}
    f = \id_{\cat{C}}(f) = G(F(f)) = G(F(g)) = \id_{\cat{C}}(g) = g,
  \end{equation*}
  so \( F \) is injective on morphisms.

  \SubProofOf{thm:small_functor_invertibility/right} Similarly, if \( G: \cat{D} \to \cat{C} \) is a right inverse of \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) in \( \cat{Cat} \), for any morphism \( h: X \to Y \) in \( \cat{D} \), \( G(h): G(X) \to G(Y) \) is a preimage of \( h \) under \( F \), so \( F \) is surjective on morphisms.

  \SubProofOf{thm:small_functor_invertibility/two_sided}

  \SufficiencySubProof* If \( G: \cat{D} \to \cat{C} \) is the inverse of \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) in \( \cat{Cat} \), \cref{thm:small_functor_invertibility/left} implies that \( F \) is injective on morphisms, while \cref{thm:small_functor_invertibility/right} implies that it is surjective on morphisms.

  \NecessitySubProof* Suppose that \( F: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) is bijective on morphisms. By \cref{thm:def:functor_invertibility/bijective}, it is bijective on objects and fully faithful.

  Denote it pointwise inverse by \( G \). We must show that \( G \) is a functor. Indeed, if \( X = F(A) \), then
  \begin{equation*}
    G(\id_X) = G(\id_{F(A)}) = G(F(\id_A)) = \id_A = \id_{G(A)},
  \end{equation*}
  which demonstrates \ref{def:functor/CF1}.

  Similarly, suppose that \( h = F(f) \) and \( s = F(g) \) for \( h: X \to Y \) and \( s: Y \to Z \). Then
  \begin{equation*}
    G(s \bincirc h) = G(F(g) \bincirc F(f)) = G(F(g \bincirc f)) = g \bincirc f = G(s) \bincirc G(h),
  \end{equation*}
  which demonstrates \ref{def:functor/CF2}.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:def:functor_invertibility}
  We list examples involving the notions of functor invertibility from \cref{def:functor_invertibility}:

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{ex:def:functor_invertibility/power} The power set functor \( \pow: \cat{Set} \to \cat{Set} \) described in \cref{ex:def:functor/power_set} is clearly injective on morphisms. \Cref{thm:def:functor_invertibility/injective} implies that it is also injective on objects and faithful.

    Note every function from \( \pow(A) \) to \( \pow(B) \) has the form \( S \mapsto f[S] \) for some \( f: A \to B \). The complement \( S \mapsto A \setminus S \) is an easy counterexample. So \( \pow \) is not a full functor.

    The power set functor \( \pow \) is also not surjective on objects because not every (small) set is a power set.

    \thmitem{ex:def:functor_invertibility/cat_to_set} The forgetful functor \( D: \cat{Cat} \to \cat{Set} \) sending a category to its set of objects is surjective on morphisms. \Cref{thm:def:functor_invertibility/surjective} implies that it is also surjective on objects and full.

    Since adding or removing morphisms in \( \cat{C} \) does not affect \( D(\cat{C}) \), the functor is not faithful, nor injective on objects.

    \thmitem{ex:def:functor_invertibility/non_surjective_epimorphism} Fix any \hyperref[def:preorder_category]{preorder category} \( \cat{C} \) (with \hyperref[def:subsingleton_set]{subsingleton} \( \hom \)-sets) and let \( \cat{D} \) be the \hyperref[def:discrete_category]{discrete category} on its objects.

    Then \( D \) is a \hyperref[def:subcategory]{subcategory} of \( \cat{C} \) retaining only the identity morphisms. The \hyperref[def:subcategory/inclusion]{inclusion functor} \( \Iota: \cat{D} \to \cat{C} \) is right cancellable because
    \begin{equation*}
      G_1 \bincirc \Iota = G_2 \bincirc \Iota
    \end{equation*}
    implies that \( G_1(A) = G_2(A) \) for every object \( A \), and there is only one choice of how \( G_1 \) or \( G_2 \) can handle morphisms. So \( G_1 \) and \( G_2 \) are equal.

    Furthermore, by construction, \( \Iota \) is surjective on objects, but not on morphisms.

    Therefore, an epimorphism in \( \cat{Cat} \) is not necessarily surjective on morphisms. So epimorphisms in \( \cat{Cat} \) cannot be described as nicely as monomorphisms (which, per \cref{thm:small_functor_cancellation/left}, are precisely the injective on morphisms functors).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{example}
