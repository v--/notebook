\section{Categories}\label{sec:categories}

\paragraph{Categories}

\begin{definition}\label{def:category}\mcite[7-10]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory}
  A \term{category} \( \cat{C} \) is a \hyperref[def:directed_multigraph]{directed multigraph} with additional structure:

  \begin{thmenum}[series=def:category]
    \thmitem{def:category/objects} We call the vertices of the graph \term[ru=объекты (\cite[def. I.1.1]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{objects} and denote the set of all objects by \( \obj(\cat{C}) \).

    As a shorthand for \( A \in \obj(\cat{C}) \), we also write \( A \in \cat{C} \).

    \thmitem{def:category/morphisms} We call the arcs of the graph \term[ru=морфизм (\cite[def. I.1.1]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий}), en=morphism (\cite[def. 1.1.1]{Perrone2024StartingCategoryTheory})]{morphisms} or simply \term[en=arrows (\cite[def. 1.1.1]{Perrone2024StartingCategoryTheory})]{arrows}.

    For every morphism \( f \) from \( A \) to \( B \), we use the notation \( f: A \to B \) or \( A \reloset f \to B \). We call the initial vertex \( A \) the \term{domain} of \( f \) and denote it by \( \dom(f) \), and dually, we call its terminal vertex \( B \) its \term{codomain} and denote it by \( \co\dom(f) \).

    Additionally, we denote the set of all morphisms from \( A \) to \( B \) by \( \cat{C}(A, B) \) or, in a more algebraic tradition, by \( \hom_{\cat{C}}(A, B) \). Naturally, we call \( \hom(A, B) \) a \term{\( \hom \)-set}.

    Both notations highlight that \( \hom(A, B) \), when parameterized, becomes \hyperref[def:functor]{functor} --- see \cref{def:hom_functor}.

    \thmitem{def:category/composition} We generalize \hyperref[def:set_valued_map/composition]{function composition} to a \hyperref[def:set_valued_map/partial]{partial operation} on morphisms, also called \term[ru=композиция (\cite[41]{Хелемский2014ФункциональныйАнализ})]{composition} and denoted by \( {\bincirc} \).

    We allow composing \( g: B \to C \) with \( f: A \to B \) to obtain \( (g \bincirc f): A \to C \).

    The order in which we write the operands is based on function composition rather than multiplication in \hyperref[def:group]{(semi)groups}; this is discussed in \cref{rem:category_composition_order}.

    \thmitem{def:category/identity} Finally, for every object \( A \), we pick a special morphism from \( A \) to itself. We call it the \term{identity} on \( A \) and denote it by \( \id_A \).
  \end{thmenum}

  The definition of a category additionally requires the following conditions to hold:
  \begin{thmenum}[resume=def:category]
    \thmitem[def:category/C1]{C1} For any morphism \( f: A \to B \), the identities \( \id_A \) and \( \id_B \) must acts like \hyperref[def:semigroup_neutral_element]{neutral elements} and satisfy
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:def:category/C1}\tag{\logic{C1}}
      f \bincirc \id_A = \id_B \bincirc f = f.
    \end{equation}

    \thmitem[def:category/C2]{C2} Composition must be \hyperref[def:binary_operation/associative]{associative} --- for each compatible triple of morphism \( f: A \to B \), \( g: B \to C \) and \( h: C \to D \), the following must hold:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:def:category/C2}\tag{\logic{C2}}
      (h \bincirc g) \bincirc f = h \bincirc (g \bincirc f).
    \end{equation}
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Any set-theoretic encoding of a category \( \cat{C} \) must contain the set \( \obj(\cat{C}) \) of objects and, for every pair \( A \) and \( B \) of objects, the set \( \hom(A, B) \) of morphisms between them. The particular encoding is of secondary importance. For definiteness, we can work with \hyperref[def:hol_semantics/model]{models} of the \hyperref[def:many_sorted_fol]{two-sorted first-order} theory presented in \cref{rem:hol_theory_of_categories}.

  \item Categories are unrelated to \hyperref[def:categorical_logical_theory]{logical theory categoricity} --- see \cref{rem:categorical_theory_terminology} --- nor to \hyperref[def:meager_set]{Baire categories} --- see \cref{rem:baire_category_terminology}.

  \item Categories are not commonly defined via graphs, but such a definition is suggested by Saunders Mac Lane, one of the creators of the category theory. This is discussed in \cref{rem:category_as_graph}.

  \item Essential categories require extensions to the underlying set theory, even though the definition of category does not. See \cref{rem:category_size}.
\end{comments}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:category_composition_order}
  In a \hyperref[def:category]{category}, the order of the composition generalizes \hyperref[def:set_valued_map/composition]{function composition} rather than multiplication in \hyperref[def:group]{(semi)groups}.

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{rem:category_composition_order/contravariant} Namely, the composition of \( f: A \to B \) and \( g: B \to C \) is \( g \bincirc f \) in
    \cite[7]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory}
    \cite[def. 1.1.1]{Riehl2016CategoryTheory},
    \cite[def. 1.1.1]{Perrone2024StartingCategoryTheory},
    \cite[def. 1.1.1]{Leinster2014BasicCategoryTheory},

    This also includes some algebra books like
    \cite[def. I.3.1]{Aluffi2009Algebra},
    \cite[53]{Lang2002Algebra},
    \cite[def. 1.1]{Jacobson1989BasicAlgebraII},
    \cite[268]{Шафаревич2019ОсновныеПонятияАлгебры}.

    \thmitem{rem:category_composition_order/covariant} The order \( f \bincirc g \) is instead used in
    \cite[def. I.1.1]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий}.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{remark}

  \end{itemize}
\end{example}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:category_as_graph}
  \Textcite[37]{Johnstone1982StoneSpaces} attributes the concept of a category to the papers \cite{EilenbergMacLane1942NaturalIsomorphisms} and \cite{EilenbergMacLane1945NaturalEquivalences}, both by Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane. The earlier one only discusses \hyperref[def:functor]{functors}, so the latter can be considered \enquote{the} first definition of a category. It presents a category as a pair of \enquote{classes} (without clarifying what a \enquote{class} means) --- one for objects and one for morphisms --- with the rest of the definition laid out explicitly.

  Several decades later, in his quintessential reference for categories, \textcite[ch. I]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory} states a more refined definition. In an informal \hyperref[con:metalanguage]{metalanguage}, he lists axioms for categories in what amounts to be \hyperref[def:many_sorted_fol]{two-sorted first-order logic}. We present a proper formulation in \cref{rem:hol_theory_of_categories}.

  Mac Lane calls any \hyperref[con:logical_model]{model} of these axioms a \enquote{metacategory}, assuming that such a model can be abstract and not necessarily based on sets\fnote{In a modern formulation, he allows a more general \hyperref[def:institution]{institution} than that from \cref{def:hol_institution}.}. In fact, he bases the notion on \enquote{metagraphs} --- abstract models of directed multigraphs. He calls the set-based models \enquote{categories} and \enquote{graphs}, correspondingly.

  Technically, this allows some flexibility in choosing an underlying set theory; this is not a straightforward choice, as we discuss in \cref{rem:category_size}.

  Many authors prefer the earlier approach of taking a class of objects and a class of morphisms and imposing properties on them. This includes
  \cite[7]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory}
  \cite[def. 1.1.1]{Riehl2016CategoryTheory},
  \cite[def. 1.1.1]{Perrone2024StartingCategoryTheory},
  \cite[def. 1.1.1]{Leinster2014BasicCategoryTheory},
  \cite[def. I.1.1]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий},
  as well as some algebra books like
  \cite[def. I.3.1]{Aluffi2009Algebra},
  \cite[53]{Lang2002Algebra},
  \cite[def. 1.1]{Jacobson1989BasicAlgebraII},
  \cite[268]{Шафаревич2019ОсновныеПонятияАлгебры}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:hol_theory_of_categories}
  Categories, as defined in, \cref{def:category} can be described via \hyperref[def:many_sorted_fol]{two-sorted first-order logic}.

  We start with the two-sorted \hyperref[def:hol_signature]{signature} for \hyperref[def:directed_multigraph]{directed multigraphs} from \cref{rem:logical_theory_of_graphs/directed_multigraphs}, where \( \syn\kappa \) is the sort of vertices (objects) and \( \syn\alpha \) is the sort of arcs (morphisms).

  In addition to the \hyperref[def:hol_signature/nl_const]{nonlogical constants}
  \begin{align*}
    \synH&: \syn\alpha \synimplies \syn\kappa, \\
    \synT&: \syn\alpha \synimplies \syn\kappa,
  \end{align*}
  determining the endpoints of a morphism, we must also add
  \begin{align*}
    \synmult&: \syn\alpha \synimplies \syn\alpha \synimplies \syn\alpha, \\
    \syn\id&:  \syn\kappa \synimplies \syn\alpha,
  \end{align*}
  subject to the axioms
  \begin{align*}
    &\qforall {\synf^{\syn\kappa}} \qforall {\syng^{\syn\kappa}} \parens[\big]{ \synT(\synf) \syneq \synH(\syng) \synimplies (\synH(\synf) \syneq \synH(\syng \synmult \synf) \synwedge \synT(\syng) \syneq \synT(\syng \synmult \synf)) }, \\
    &\qforall {\synf^{\syn\kappa}} \qforall {\syng^{\syn\kappa}} \qforall {\synh^{\syn\kappa}} \parens[\big]{ (\synh \synmult \syng) \synmult \synf \syneq \synh \synmult (\syng \synmult \synf) }
  \end{align*}
  for composition and
  \begin{balign*}
    &\qforall {\syna^{\syn\kappa}} \parens[\big]{ \synT(\syn\id(\syna)) \syneq \syna }, \\
    &\qforall {\syna^{\syn\kappa}} \parens[\big]{ \synH(\syn\id(\syna)) \syneq \syna }, \\
    &\qforall {\synf^{\syn\alpha}} \parens[\big]{ \synf \synmult \syn\id(\synH(\synf)) \syneq \synf }, \\
    &\qforall {\synf^{\syn\alpha}} \parens[\big]{ \syn\id(\synT(\synf)) \synmult \synf \syneq \synf }
  \end{balign*}
  for identity morphisms.

  Note that, as per \cref{rem:hol_definite_description/interpretation}, our formulation of higher-order logic does not allow partially-defined operations. Thus, composition must be total, and incompatible morphisms must be composable. For definiteness, we may add the following axiom:
  \begin{equation*}
    \qforall {\synf^{\syn\kappa}} \qforall {\syng^{\syn\kappa}} \parens[\big]{ \synneg (\synT(\synf) \syneq \synH(\syng)) \synimplies (\syng \synmult \synf \syneq \syn\id(\synH(\synf))) }.
  \end{equation*}

  Fixing such an \enquote{error value} helps avoid the case where multiple categories differ only by how they treat ill-defined composition.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Set and category size}

\begin{definition}\label{def:small_set}\mcite[17]{Shulman2008SetTheoryForCategoryTheory}
  We are faced with a conundrum --- we want to study the category of all sets, but set theory cannot express such a construct. Our solution is to work in \hyperref[def:zfcu]{\logic{ZFC+U}}, where in addition to the standard axioms of set theory, we presuppose the existence of at least one \hyperref[def:grothendieck_universe]{Grothendieck universe}, and hence of the \hyperref[def:smallest_grothendieck_universe]{smallest Grothendieck universe} \( \mscrU_0 \). As shown in \cref{thm:grothendieck_universe_is_model_of_zfc}, \( \mscrU \) is itself a \hyperref[def:fol_semantics/model]{model} of \hyperref[def:zfc]{\logic{ZFC}}, so studying only members of \( \mscrU \) is not inherently limited compared to studying arbitrary sets in \logic{ZFC}.

  We refer to the members of \( \mscrU_0 \) as \term{small sets}. This terminology applies more generally to other structures. Groups, lattices or even categories may just as well be small if their set-theoretic encoding is a small set. This is formalized in \cref{thm:hol_small_model_characterization}, \cref{thm:fol_small_model_characterization} and \cref{thm:small_category_characterization}.

  If we want to highlight that a set or other structure is not small, we say that it is \term{large}.
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Alternative approaches for discriminating small and large sets are described in \cref{rem:category_size}.
\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:hol_small_model_characterization}
  A \hyperref[def:hol_structure]{structure of higher-order logic} \( \mscrX = (X, I) \) over the \hyperref[def:hol_frame]{frame} \( X = \seq{ X_\tau }_{\tau \in \op*{\BbbQ_\Sigma}} \) is itself a \hyperref[def:small_set]{small set} if and only if, for every sort \( S \) in \( \Sigma \), the set \( X_\tau \) is small.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  We will prove a more general statement for an arbitrary universe \( \mscrU \) rather than the smallest universe \( \mscrU_0 \).

  \SufficiencySubProof Suppose that, as an \hyperref[def:ordered_tuple]{ordered pair}, \( \mscrX = (X, I) \) belongs to \( \mscrU \)

  We have not specified whether we treat \( \mscrU \) as a \hyperref[def:kuratowski_pair]{Kuratowski pair} or as an \hyperref[def:indexed_family]{indexed family}, but both lead to the same result.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item In the first case, we have
    \begin{equation*}
      \mscrX = \set{ \set{ X }, \set{ X, I } }.
    \end{equation*}

    Since \( \mscrU \) satisfies the transitivity axiom \ref{def:grothendieck_universe/transitive}, the set \( \set{ X, I } \) belongs to \( \mscrU \), and hence so do \( X \) and \( I \).

    \item In the second case, we have
    \begin{equation*}
      \mscrX = \set{ \braket{ 0, X }, \braket{ 1, I } },
    \end{equation*}
    where both \( \braket{ 0, X } \) and \( \braket{ 1, I } \) are Kuratowski pairs. By the above reasoning, both \( X \) and \( I \) are again members of \( \mscrU \).
  \end{itemize}

  Continuing analogously, since \( X \) is an indexed family, we know that, for each quantifiable type \( \tau \), the pair \( \braket{ \tau, X_\tau } = \set{ \set{ \tau }, \set{ \tau, X_\tau } } \) belongs to \( X \), and hence to \( \mscrU \). The the universe \( X_\tau \) of every type \( \tau \) belongs to \( \mscrU \), in particular \( X_S \) for every sort \( S \).

  \NecessitySubProof Suppose that \( X_S \) belongs to \( \mscrU \) for every sort \( S \).

  We use \fullref{thm:induction_on_abstract_syntax} on the quantifiable type \( \tau \) to show that \( X_\tau \) is in \( \mscrU \).
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If \( \tau \) is a sort, this holds by assumption.

    \item Suppose that \( \tau \) is the type of propositions \( \syn\omicron \).

    In the corresponding universe \( X_{\syn\omicron} = \set{ \semtop, \sembot } \), both \( \semtop \) and \( \sembot \) are \hyperref[def:universe_of_hereditary_finite_sets]{hereditarily finite sets} by the assumption from \cref{rem:language_alphabet_cardinality}.

    \Cref{thm:grothendieck_universe_closure/hereditarily_finite} implies that they belong to \( \mscrU \), and \cref{thm:grothendieck_universe_closure/unordered_pair} implies that \( X_{\syn\omicron} = \set{ \semtop, \sembot } \) also belongs to \( \mscrU \).

    \item If \( \tau = \sigma \synimplies \rho \), where the inductive hypothesis holds for \( \sigma \) and \( \rho \), then
    \begin{equation*}
      X_{\tau \synimplies \sigma} \subseteq \fun(X_\tau, X_\sigma) \subseteq X_\tau \times X_\sigma.
    \end{equation*}

    By the inductive hypothesis, both \( X_\tau \) and \( X_\sigma \) belong to \( \mscrU \). \Cref{thm:grothendieck_universe_closure/cartesian_product} implies that their product \( X_\tau \times X_\sigma \) also belongs to \( \mscrU \).

    Any subset of the product, in particular \( X_{\tau \synimplies \sigma} \), also belongs to \( \mscrU \) due to \cref{thm:grothendieck_universe_closure/subset}.
  \end{itemize}

  Thus, we have concluded that \( X_\tau \) belongs to \( \mscrU \) for every quantifiable type \( \tau \). By \cref{thm:grothendieck_universe_closure/indexed_family}, the entire frame \( X = \seq{ X_\tau }_{\tau \in \op*{\BbbQ_\Sigma}} \) also belongs to \( \mscrU \).

  The interpretation \( I \) takes some constant \( c: \tau \) to an element of \( X_\tau \). The signature is assumed to be finite or at least to not go beyond \( \mscrU \), while \( X_\tau \) is, as we have just shown, in \( \mscrU \). \Cref{thm:grothendieck_universe_closure/function} implies that the function \( I \), as a set, also belongs to \( \mscrU \).

  Finally, \cref{thm:grothendieck_universe_closure/indexed_family} implies that the ordered pair \( \mscrX = (X, I) \) belongs to \( \mscrU \).
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:fol_small_model_characterization}
  A \hyperref[def:fol_structure]{structure of first-order logic} \( \mscrX = (X, I) \) is itself a \hyperref[def:small_set]{small set} if and only if \( X \) is small.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Similar to, but simpler than \cref{thm:hol_small_model_characterization}.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:category_of_small_sets}\mcite[12; 22]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory}
  We define the \term{category of small sets} \( \cat{Set} \) as follows:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:category_of_small_sets/objects} The \hyperref[def:category/objects]{objects} are, unsurprisingly, the \hyperref[def:small_set]{small sets}, i.e. the set of objects is the \hyperref[def:smallest_grothendieck_universe]{smallest Grothendieck universe} \( \mscrU_0 \).

    \thmitem{def:category_of_small_sets/morphisms} The \hyperref[def:category/morphisms]{morphisms} between small sets are the \hyperref[def:function]{total single-valued functions} between them.

    Note that, by \cref{thm:grothendieck_universe_closure/function}, functions between small sets are themselves small as sets.

    \thmitem{def:category_of_small_sets/composition} The \hyperref[def:category/composition]{composition operation} is defined for \( f: A \to B \) and \( g: B \to C \) as their \hyperref[def:set_valued_map/composition]{set-theoretic composition}.

    \thmitem{def:category_of_small_sets/identity} The \hyperref[def:category/identity]{identity morphism} on \( A \) is simply the \hyperref[def:set_valued_map/identity]{identity function} on \( A \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}
\begin{defproof}
  We must show that \( \cat{Set} \) is indeed a category.

  \SubProofOf{def:category/C1} For every pair of small sets \( A \) and \( B \) and every function \( f: A \to B \), for all \( x \) in \( A \) we have
  \begin{equation*}
    [\id_B \bincirc f](x)
    =
    \id_B(f(x))
    =
    f(x)
    =
    f(\id_A(x))
    =
    [f \bincirc \id_A](x).
  \end{equation*}

  Therefore, \( \id_A \) and \( \id_B \) satisfy \eqref{eq:def:category/C1}.

  \SubProofOf{def:category/C2} Associativity of function composition is proved in \cref{thm:def:set_valued_map/associative}.
\end{defproof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:category_of_small_sets}
  We collect here important properties of the category \hyperref[def:category_of_small_sets]{\( \cat{Set} \)} of \hyperref[def:small_set]{small sets}. Most of them require forward references.

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{rem:category_of_small_sets/large} It is itself a \hyperref[def:category_size]{large category} because its set of objects, the \hyperref[def:smallest_grothendieck_universe]{smallest Grothendieck universe} \( \mscrU_0 \), is itself a large set (otherwise it would contain itself, contradicting the \hyperref[def:zfc/foundation]{axiom of foundation}).

    Still, \( \cat{Set} \) is \hyperref[def:category_size]{locally small}. Indeed, by \cref{thm:grothendieck_universe_closure/function}, every member of \( \cat{Set}(A, B) \) belongs to \( \mscrU_0 \), and thus, by \cref{thm:grothendieck_universe_closure/subset}, \( \cat{Set}(A, B) \) itself belongs to \( \mscrU_0 \).

    \thmitem{rem:category_of_small_sets/morphism_invertibility} All \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/right_cancellative]{epimorphisms} and \hyperref[def:set_valued_map/empty]{nonempty} \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/left_cancellative]{monomorphisms} \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/left_invertible]{split} and are precisely the \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/surjective]{surjective} and nonempty \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/injective]{injective functions}, respectively.

    This is stated in \cref{thm:function_invertibility_categorical}. See also \fullref{thm:epimorphisms_split_in_set}.

    \thmitem{rem:category_of_small_sets/universal_objects} The empty set \( \varnothing \) is an \hyperref[def:universal_objects/initial]{initial object} and any singleton set \( \set{ A } \) is a \hyperref[def:universal_objects/terminal]{terminal object}. No \hyperref[def:universal_objects/zero]{zero objects} exist in \( \cat{Set} \).

    This is discussed in \cref{ex:def:universal_objects}.

    \thmitem{rem:category_of_small_sets/discrete_category} The \hyperref[def:discrete_category]{discrete category} functor \( D: \cat{Set} \to \cat{Cat} \) is left adjoint to the forgetful functor \( U: \cat{Cat} \to \cat{Set} \)

    This is discussed in \cref{ex:def:category_adjunction/set_cat}.

    \thmitem{rem:category_of_small_sets/limits} The \hyperref[def:discrete_category_limits]{products} and \hyperref[def:discrete_category_limits]{coproducts} are the \hyperref[def:cartesian_product]{Cartesian products} and the \hyperref[def:disjoint_union]{disjoint unions}, respectively.

    This is shown in \fullref{thm:cartesian_product_universal_property} and \fullref{thm:disjoint_union_universal_property}.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:small_category_characterization}
  A \hyperref[def:category]{category} is itself a \hyperref[def:small_set]{small set} if and only if the set of all its morphisms is small.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Let \( \mscrX = (X, I) \) be a \hyperref[def:hol_structure]{structure of higher-order logic} that satisfies the theory of categories presented in \cref{rem:hol_theory_of_categories}.

  \Cref{thm:hol_small_model_characterization} implies that \( \mscrX \) is small if and only if \( X_{\syn\kappa} \), the set of objects, and \( X_{\syn\alpha} \), the set of morphisms, are small.

  But \( X_{\syn\alpha} \) contains a distinct identity morphism for each object in \( X_{\syn\kappa} \), so if \( X_{\syn\alpha} \) if small, so is \( X_{\syn\kappa} \).

  Therefore, \( \mscrX \) is a small set if and only if \( X_{\syn\alpha} \) is.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:category_size}\mcite[3; 10]{Shulman2008SetTheoryForCategoryTheory}
  We call a \hyperref[def:category]{category} \term[ru=малая категория (\cite[15]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{small} if the set of all its morphisms is \hyperref[def:small_set]{small}. \Cref{thm:small_category_characterization} shows that this is equivalent to the category itself being a small set.

  If the individual \( \hom \)-sets are small, but not necessarily the set of all morphisms, we call the category \term[ru=локально малая категория (\cite[26]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{locally small}.

  As in \cref{def:small_set}, if we want to highlight that a category is not small, we say that it is \term{large}.
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Alternative approaches for discriminating small and large categories are described in \cref{rem:category_size}.
\end{comments}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:category_size}
  In \cref{def:small_set}, we have introduced a distinction between \enquote{small sets}, those that belong to the \hyperref[def:smallest_grothendieck_universe]{smallest Grothendieck universe}, and \enquote{large sets}, those that do not. This also allowed us to distinguish small from large categories in \cref{def:category_size}.

  In this, we went beyond the conventional \hyperref[def:zfc]{Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory} by assuming the existence of a \hyperref[def:grothendieck_universe]{Grothendieck universe}. \Cref{thm:grothendieck_universe_iff_regular_strong_limit} shows that this assumption is equivalent to the existence of a \hyperref[rem:strongly_inaccessible_cardinal]{strongly inaccessible cardinal}.

  Fix a standard model \( \mscrV = (V, I) \) of \logic{ZFC}. Our assumption amounts to the existence if \( V \) of some universe \( \mscrU \). By taking the intersection of all such universes, we arrive at the smallest one, which we denote by \( \mscrU_0 \).

  This is essentially the approach used by \textcite[12]{MacLane1971CategoricalAlgebraFoundations}. We list some other approaches for comparison.

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{rem:category_size/grothendieck} We can go further and assume that each set \( A \) belongs to some universe \( \mscrU \). In particular, \( A \) can itself be universe. We call this the \term{axiom of universes}. \Textcite[234]{MacLane1971CategoricalAlgebraFoundations} attributes this approach to \enquote{Grothendieck and his followers}.

    This gives us a sequence of nested universes, somewhat alike the sequence of type universes from \cref{con:type_universe}.

    However, instead of a single category \( \cat{Set} \) of all small sets, we now have a category \( \cat[\mscrU]{Set} \) of \( \mscrU \)-small sets for every universe \( \mscrU \). An obvious drawback is what \textcite[22]{Shulman2008SetTheoryForCategoryTheory} calls \enquote{universe-juggling}. We may either by default assume a fixed universe, which has little advantage of working with \( \mscrU_0 \), or make the universes explicit, which is cumbersome.

    \thmitem{rem:category_size/classes} An alternative is to work in an extension of \( \logic{ZFC} \) that supports classes, such as \hyperref[def:godel_bernays_set_theory]{von Neumann-G\"odel-Bernays set theory} (\logic{NBG}). This approach is used by \textcite[def. I.1.1]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий}.

    Here, we can start with an ordinary standard model \( \mscrV = (V, I) \) of \logic{ZFC} and build a model \( \mscrV' = (\pow(V), I') \) of \logic{NBG}, where the members of \( V \) are considered sets and the subsets of \( V \), i.e. the members of \( \pow(V) \), are considered proper classes.

    Given a class \( O \) of objects and a class \( M \) of morphisms, \cref{thm:ordered_tuple_via_disjoint_union} suggests that the set \( (\set{ 0 } \times O) \cup (\set{ 1 } \times M)) \) can be used to emulate an \hyperref[def:ordered_tuple]{ordered tuple}, and thus to formally encode a category as a class.

    We can form the category \( \cat{Set} \) of all sets and the category \( \cat{Cat} \) of small categories, i.e. those that are sets. Both categories are \enquote{large} and belong to the even larger category \( \cat{CAT} \) of large categories.

    This approach requires us to be very careful with our formalisms because important constructs like the \hyperref[def:functor_category]{functor category} can only be properly defined for small categories.

    \thmitem{rem:category_size/reflection} Yet another approach is to construct a \hyperref[def:fol_theory/conservative]{conservative} extension of \logic{ZFC} by introducing a \hyperref[def:fol_signature/constants]{constant symbol} \( \syn\BbbS \) with some axioms that make it usable as a universe. \Textcite[25]{Shulman2008SetTheoryForCategoryTheory} calls this theory \logic{ZFC/S} and attributes it to Solomon Feferman.

    First, this pseudo-universe \( \syn\BbbS \) must satisfy some basic coherence axioms
    \begin{align*}
      \qforall {\synx \synin \syn\BbbS} \qforall {\syny \synin \synx} \syny \synin \syn\BbbS,
      \qforall {\synx \synin \syn\BbbS} \qforall {\syny \synsubseteq \synx} \syny \synin \syn\BbbS.
    \end{align*}

    More importantly, \( \syn\BbbS \) must satisfy the \term{reflection principle} --- for every formula \( \varphi \) in plain \logic{ZFC} with free variables \( x_1, \ldots, x_n \), we need the axiom
    \begin{equation*}
      \qforall* {x_1 \in \syn\BbbS} \ldots \qforall* {x_n \in \syn\BbbS} (\varphi \syniff \varphi^\syn\BbbS),
    \end{equation*}
    where \( \varphi^\syn\BbbS \) \hyperref[rem:fol_formula_conventions/relativization]{relativizes} every quantifier in \( \varphi \) to \( \syn\BbbS \). Thus, if a (closed) sentence is proved for all members of \( \syn\BbbS \), this proof should transfer to all sets in \logic{ZFC}.

    Although \( \syn\BbbS \) is usable as a universe, it has a major drawback --- a function between members of \( \syn\BbbS \) does not necessarily belong to \( \syn\BbbS \). This breaks many concepts such as \hyperref[def:functor_category]{functor categories}.

    \Textcite[26]{Shulman2008SetTheoryForCategoryTheory} claims that if a form of the \hyperref[def:zfc/replacement]{axiom of replacement} is introduced for \( \syn\BbbS \) to mitigate this issue, the resulting theory is stronger than \logic{ZFC} with the axiom of universe.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Opposite categories}

\begin{definition}\label{def:opposite_directed_multigraph}\mcite[def. 1.1.8]{Knauer2019AlgebraicGraphTheory}
  The \term{opposite} or \term{dual} of the \hyperref[def:directed_multigraph]{directed multigraph} \( G = (V, A, i, t) \) is obtained by swapping \( i \) and \( t \).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}\label{def:opposite_category}\mcite[def. 1.1.9]{Leinster2014BasicCategoryTheory}
  The \term{opposite} category of \( \cat{C} \) is obtained by \enquote{reversing} all arrows. This reversing is merely a relabeling of the domain and codomain --- the underlying morphisms are the same. This concept is quite powerful because it allows performing constructions and proofs by duality --- see \fullref{thm:categorical_principle_of_duality}.

  Formally, the category \( \cat{C}^\oppos \) is based on the \hyperref[def:opposite_directed_multigraph]{opposite multigraph} and is defined as follows:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The \hyperref[def:category/objects]{set of objects} \( \obj(\cat{C}^\oppos) \) is the set of objects \( \obj(\cat{C}) \) of \( \cat{C} \).

    \item The \hyperref[def:category/morphisms]{set of morphisms} \( \cat{C}^\oppos(A, B) \) is the set \( \cat{C}(B, A) \). Thus, any morphism \( f^\oppos: A \to B \) in the opposite category \( \cat{C}^\oppos \) is a morphism \( f: B \to A \) in \( \cat{C}^\oppos \).

    The superscript here is used solely to distinguish between \( f \) being regarded as a morphism of \( \cat{C} \) and of \( \cat{C}^\oppos \) --- the morphisms in \( \cat{C} \) are exactly those of \( \cat{C}^\oppos \), simply relabeled.

    \item The \hyperref[def:category/composition]{composition of the morphisms}
    \begin{align*}
      f^\oppos &\in \cat{C}^\oppos(A, B) = \cat{C}(B, A) \\
      g^\oppos &\in \cat{C}^\oppos(B, C) = \cat{C}(C, B)
    \end{align*}
    is the morphism
    \begin{equation*}
      \underbrace{g^\oppos \bincirc f^\oppos}_{\cat{C}^\oppos(A, C)} \coloneqq \underbrace{f \bincirc g}_{\cat{C}(C, A)}.
    \end{equation*}

    \item The \hyperref[def:category/identity]{identity morphism} on the object \( A \in \cat{C} \) is again \( \id_A \).
  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:double_opposite_category}
  The double-opposite of a category or morphism is obviously the original. This is made precise with the oppositization functor defined in \cref{def:opposite_functor}.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}\label{ex:def:opposite_category}
  A morphism \( f^\oppos: A \to B \) in the category \( \cat{Set}^\oppos \) is a function from the set \( B \) to the set \( A \). We cannot apply \( f \) to a point in \( B \) unless \( B \subseteq A \). Thus, we cannot regard, in general, the morphism \( f^\oppos \) as a function, although only the \hyperref[def:function]{signature} of \( f \) is different from that of \( f^\oppos \) --- their \hyperref[def:set_valued_map/graph]{graphs} are the same.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:categorical_principle_of_duality}
  We can extend the principle of duality for preordered sets discussed in \fullref{thm:order_duality} to categories. Since we have defined categories in \hyperref[def:zfcu]{\logic{ZFC+U}} rather than as a first-order theory, we will state this principle informally:
  \begin{displayquote}
    If a statement holds for every category, its dual statement obtained by \enquote{reversing} all morphisms as in \cref{def:opposite_category}, also holds for every category.
  \end{displayquote}

  See \cref{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/split_epimorphism} for how this principle can be utilized easily.

  We list here results that heavily utilize this principle. Note that it is now always obvious what exactly needs to reversed in order for this principle to hold. For example, as discussed in \cref{def:opposite_functor}, for opposite functors we have
  \begin{equation*}
    [F \bincirc G]^\oppos = F^\oppos \bincirc G^\oppos,
  \end{equation*}
  which is somewhat unexpected.

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:categorical_principle_of_duality/morphism_invertibility} \Cref{thm:morphism_invertibility_duality}: A morphism \( f: A \to B \) in \( \cat{C} \) is a \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/left_invertible]{(split)} \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/left_cancellative]{monomorphism} if and only if \( f^\oppos: B \to A \) in the opposite category \( \cat{C}^\oppos \) is a \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/right_invertible]{(split)} \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/right_cancellative]{epimorphism}.

    In particular, \( f \) is an \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/isomorphism]{isomorphism} in \( \cat{C} \) if and only if \( f^\oppos \) is an isomorphism in \( \cat{C}^\oppos \).

    \thmitem{thm:categorical_principle_of_duality/universal_objects} \Cref{thm:universal_object_duality}: An object is \hyperref[def:universal_objects/initial]{initial} if and only if it is a \hyperref[def:universal_objects/terminal]{terminal object} the \hyperref[def:opposite_category]{opposite category}.

    \thmitem{thm:categorical_principle_of_duality/functor_categories} \Cref{thm:opposite_of_functor_category}: For the \hyperref[def:opposite_category]{opposite} of the \hyperref[def:functor_category]{functor category} \( [\cat{C}, \cat{D}] \) we have
    \begin{equation*}
      [\cat{C}, \cat{D}]^\oppos = [\cat{C}^\oppos, \cat{D}^\oppos].
    \end{equation*}

    \thmitem{thm:categorical_principle_of_duality/equivalences} \Cref{thm:opposite_of_category_equivalence}: The \hyperref[def:opposite_category]{duals} of \hyperref[def:category_equivalence]{equivalent categories} are equivalent.

    \thmitem{thm:categorical_principle_of_duality/adjunctions} \Cref{thm:category_adjunction_duality}: The functor \( F \) is \hyperref[def:category_adjunction]{left adjoint} to \( G \) if and only if the \hyperref[def:opposite_functor]{dual functor} \( F^\oppos \) is right adjoint to \( G^\oppos \).

    \thmitem{thm:categorical_principle_of_duality/limits} \Cref{thm:categorical_limit_duality}: For every \hyperref[def:category_of_cones/cone]{cone} \( (A, \alpha) \) of the \hyperref[def:categorical_diagram]{diagram} \( D \) in \( \cat{C} \), \( (A, \alpha^\oppos) \) is a \hyperref[def:category_of_cones/cone]{cocone} of \( D^\oppos \) in \( \cat{C}^\oppos \).

    Even more, for every \hyperref[def:category_of_cones/limit]{limit} \( (L, \pi) \) of \( D \) in \( \cat{C} \), \( (L, \pi^\oppos) \) is a \hyperref[def:category_of_cones/colimit]{colimit} of \( D^\oppos \) in \( \cat{C}^\oppos \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}

\begin{definition}\label{def:morphism_invertibility}
  In connection with \cref{def:function_invertibility} and \cref{def:fol_embedding}, we introduce the following terminology:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:morphism_invertibility/left_cancellative} The morphism \( g: B \to C \) is \term{left-cancellative} if, for any pair of morphisms \( f_1, f_2: A \to B \), the equality \( g \bincirc f_1 = g \bincirc f_2 \) implies \( f_1 = f_2 \).

    Left-cancellative morphisms are also called \term{monic morphisms} or \term{monomorphisms}.

    \thmitem{def:morphism_invertibility/left_invertible} The morphism \( f: A \to B \) is \term{left-invertible} if there exists a morphism \( g: B \to A \) such that \( g \bincirc f = \id_A \). We call \( g \) a \term{left inverse} of \( f \).

    Using forward references to \cref{def:categorical_diagram}, we can restate this condition by saying that the following diagram commutes:

      \begin{aligned}
        \includegraphics[page=1]{output/def__morphism_invertibility}
      \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}

    Left-invertible morphisms are sometimes called \term{split monomorphisms} because they \enquote{split} the identity \( \id_A \) into a composition of \( f \) and \( g \).

    \thmitem{def:morphism_invertibility/right_cancellative} \hyperref[thm:categorical_principle_of_duality]{Dually}, the morphism \( f: A \to B \) is \term{right-cancellative} if, for any pair of morphisms \( g_1, g_2: B \to C \), the equality \( g_1 \bincirc f = g_2 \bincirc f \) implies \( g_1 = g_2 \).

    Right-cancellative morphisms are also called \term{epic morphisms} or \term{epimorphisms}.

    \thmitem{def:morphism_invertibility/right_invertible} The morphism \( g: B \to A \) is \term{right-invertible} if there exists a morphism \( f: A \to B \) such that \( f \bincirc g = \id_B \). We call \( g \) a \term{right inverse} of \( f \).

    Using forward references to \cref{def:categorical_diagram}, we can restate this condition by saying that the following diagram commutes:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:def:morphism_invertibility/right_invertible}
      \begin{aligned}
        \includegraphics[page=2]{output/def__morphism_invertibility}
      \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}

    Right-invertible morphisms are sometimes called \term{split epimorphisms} because they \enquote{split} the identity \( \id_B \) into a composition of \( g \) and \( f \).

    \thmitem{def:morphism_invertibility/isomorphism} The morphism \( f: A \to B \) is \term{fully invertible} it is both left-invertible and right-invertible. By \cref{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/left_and_right}, in this case, there exists a unique morphism \( f^{-1}: B \to A \) that is a \term{two-sided inverse}, i.e. it is both a left inverse and a right inverse.

    A fully invertible morphism is usually called an \term{isomorphism}. If there exists an isomorphism between \( A \) and \( B \), we say that they are \term{isomorphic} and write \( A \cong B \).

    \thmitem{def:morphism_invertibility/endomorphism} A morphism \( f: A \to A \) from an object to itself is called an \term{endomorphism}.

    \thmitem{def:morphism_invertibility/automorphism} A morphism that is both an endomorphism and an isomorphism is called an \term{automorphism}.

    \thmitem{def:morphism_invertibility/involution} If an automorphism is its own inverse, we call it an \term[ru=инволюция (\cite[97]{Розенфельд1963ОсновныеПонятиеГеометрии}), en=involution (of vector spaces) (\cite[32]{Dieudonne1969LinearAlgebraAndGeometry})]{involution}.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item The term \enquote{involution} is also used for self-inverse group elements; see \cref{def:group_element_order}.
\end{comments}

\begin{example}\label{ex:def:morphism_invertibility}

  A very simple example of a monomorphism which does not split is the empty function with nonempty domain. These are discussed in \cref{thm:function_invertibility_categorical/empty}.

  \Fullref{thm:surjective_functions_are_right_invertible} is important enough to have a categorical interpretation via \fullref{thm:epimorphisms_split_in_set}, where its relation to the \hyperref[def:zfc/choice]{axiom of choice} is also discussed.
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:morphism_invertibility_duality}
  A morphism \( f: A \to B \) in \( \cat{C} \) is a \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/left_invertible]{(split)} \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/left_cancellative]{monomorphism} if and only if \( f^\oppos: B \to A \) in the opposite category \( \cat{C}^\oppos \) is a \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/right_invertible]{(split)} \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/right_cancellative]{epimorphism}.

  In particular, \( f \) is an \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/isomorphism]{isomorphism} in \( \cat{C} \) if and only if \( f^\oppos \) is an isomorphism in \( \cat{C}^\oppos \).

  This is part of the duality principles listed in \fullref{thm:categorical_principle_of_duality}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Trivial.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:def:morphism_invertibility}
  Morphisms have the following basic properties regarding their \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility]{invertibility} (compare to \cref{thm:function_composition_invertibility}):

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/split_monomorphism} Any \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/left_invertible]{left-invertible morphism} is \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/left_cancellative]{left-cancellative}.

    In more categorical terms, every split monomorphism is a monomorphism.

    \thmitem{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/split_epimorphism} Any \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/right_invertible]{right-invertible morphism} is \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/right_cancellative]{right-cancellative}.

    In more categorical terms, every split epimorphism is an epimorphism.

    \thmitem{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/at_most_one_inverse}\mcite[exerc. 1.1.13]{Leinster2014BasicCategoryTheory} Any morphism has at most one two-sided inverse.

    \thmitem{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/left_and_right} If a morphism is both left-invertible and right-invertible, the two inverses are equal, and the morphism is fully invertible.

    \thmitem{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/inverse_interchanges} The morphism \( f: A \to B \) is a right inverse of \( g: B \to A \) if and only if \( g \) is a left inverse of \( f \).

    \thmitem{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/monomorphism_and_split_epimorphism} If a morphism left-cancellative and right-invertible, it is an isomorphism.

    \thmitem{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/split_monomorphism_and_epimorphism} If a morphism left-invertible and right-cancellative, it is an isomorphism.

    \thmitem{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/cancellative_composition} The composition of two monomorphisms (resp. epimorphisms) is again a monomorphism (resp. epimorphism).

    \thmitem{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/invertible_composition} The composition of two split monomorphisms (resp. epimorphisms) is again a split monomorphism (resp. epimorphism).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/split_monomorphism} Suppose that \( g: B \to C \) is left-invertible with inverse \( h: C \to B \). Suppose that \( f_1, f_2: A \to B \) are morphisms such that
  \begin{equation*}
    g \bincirc f_1 = g \bincirc f_2.
  \end{equation*}

  Then
  \begin{equation*}
    f_1
    \reloset {\eqref{eq:def:category/C1}} =
    \id_B \bincirc f_1
    =
    (h \bincirc g) \bincirc f_1
    \reloset {\eqref{eq:def:category/C2}} =
    h \bincirc (g \bincirc f_1)
    =
    h \bincirc (g \bincirc f_2)
    =
    \cdots
    =
    f_2.
  \end{equation*}

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/split_epimorphism} This is an exemplar proof using duality. By \cref{thm:morphism_invertibility_duality}, every split epimorphism \( f: A \to B \) in \( \cat{C} \) is a split monomorphism in \( \cat{C}^\oppos \). By \cref{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/split_monomorphism}, \( f^\oppos \) is a monomorphism. Then gain by \cref{thm:morphism_invertibility_duality}, \( f \) is an epimorphism.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/at_most_one_inverse} If \( f: A \to B \) has no inverse, it vacuously has at most one inverse.

  Now assume that \( f: A \to B \) has two inverses \( g_1: B \to A \) and \( g_2: B \to A \):
  \begin{align*}
    g_1 \bincirc f = \id_A &&& f \bincirc g_1 = \id_B, \\
    g_2 \bincirc f = \id_A &&& f \bincirc g_2 = \id_B.
  \end{align*}

  Then
  \begin{equation*}
    g_1
    \reloset {\eqref{eq:def:category/C1}} =
    g_1 \bincirc \id_B
    =
    g_1 \bincirc (f \bincirc g_2)
    \reloset {\eqref{eq:def:category/C2}} =
    (g_1 \bincirc f) \bincirc g_2
    =
    \id_A \bincirc g_2
    \reloset {\eqref{eq:def:category/C1}} =
    g_2.
  \end{equation*}

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/left_and_right} Suppose that \( f: A \to B \) has a left-inverse \( l: B \to A \) and a right-inverse \( r: B \to A \). Then

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/inverse_interchanges} Trivial.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/monomorphism_and_split_epimorphism} Let \( g: B \to A \) be left-cancellative and right-invertible. Let \( f: A \to B \) be a right inverse of \( g \). Then
  \begin{equation*}
    f
    =
    \reloset {\eqref{eq:def:category/C1}} =
    f \bincirc \id_A
    =
    f \bincirc (g \bincirc f)
    =
    (f \bincirc g) \bincirc f.
  \end{equation*}

  Because \( g \) is a left inverse of \( f \), from \cref{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/split_monomorphism} it follows that \( f \) is left-cancellative. Since we have
  \begin{equation*}
    \id_B \bincirc f
    =
    (f \bincirc g) \bincirc f,
  \end{equation*}
  it follows that \( f \bincirc g = \id_B \).

  Therefore, \( f \) is a left inverse of \( g \) and hence an isomorphism.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/split_monomorphism_and_epimorphism} The proof is analogous to \cref{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/monomorphism_and_split_epimorphism}.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/cancellative_composition} Let \( g: B \to C \) and \( h: C \to D \) be monomorphisms (left-cancellative).

  Let \( f_1, f_2: A \to B \) be two arbitrary morphisms with codomain \( B \). Suppose that
  \begin{equation*}
    (h \bincirc g) \bincirc f_1 = (h \bincirc g) \bincirc f_2.
  \end{equation*}

  Then, by \ref{def:category/C2},
  \begin{equation*}
    h \bincirc (g \bincirc f_1) = h \bincirc (g \bincirc f_2).
  \end{equation*}

  Since \( h \) is left-cancellative, it follows that
  \begin{equation*}
    g \bincirc f_1 = g \bincirc f_2.
  \end{equation*}

  Since \( g \) is also left-cancellative, \( f_1 = f_2 \).

  Therefore, \( h \bincirc g \) is a monomorphism.

  The proof for composition of epimorphisms is identical.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/invertible_composition} Let \( f: A \to B \) and \( g: B \to C \) be split monomorphisms (left-invertible).

  Then there exist left inverses \( l_f: B \to A \) and \( l_g: C \to B \) of \( f \) and \( g \), respectively. We have
  \begin{equation*}
    (l_f \bincirc l_g) \bincirc (g \bincirc f)
    \reloset {\eqref{eq:def:category/C2}} =
    l_f \bincirc (l_g \bincirc g) \bincirc f
    =
    l_f \bincirc \id_B \bincirc f
    \reloset {\eqref{eq:def:category/C1}} =
    l_f \bincirc f
    =
    \id_A.
  \end{equation*}

  Therefore, \( g \bincirc f \) is also left-invertible.

  The proof for composition of split epimorphisms is identical.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Epimorphisms split in Set]\label{thm:epimorphisms_split_in_set}
  Every \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/right_cancellative]{epimorphism} in \hyperref[def:category_of_small_sets]{\( \cat{Set} \)} splits. That is, all epimorphisms in \( \cat{Set} \) are \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/right_invertible]{split epimorphisms}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{comments}
  \item Assuming the existence of the \hyperref[def:grothendieck_universe]{Grothendieck universe} containing \( \cat{Set} \), in \hyperref[def:zfc]{\logic{ZF}} this theorem is equivalent to the \hyperref[def:zfc/choice]{axiom of choice} --- see \cref{thm:axiom_of_choice_equivalences/epimorphisms}.

  \item Since not every epimorphism splits in a general category, this theorem is sometimes considered to be a categorical statement of the axiom of choice, which holds in some categories but not in others.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  By \cref{thm:function_invertibility_categorical/right_cancellative}, a function is an epimorphism if and only if it is surjective. Thus, the theorem is equivalent to \fullref{thm:surjective_functions_are_right_invertible}.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:universal_objects}\mcite[20]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory}
  Fix a category \( \cat{C} \).

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:universal_objects/initial} We call the object \( I \in \cat{C} \) an \term[ru=начальный объект (\cite[80]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{initial object} if for any other object \( A \in \cat{C} \) there exists a unique morphism \( f: I \to A \),

    \thmitem{def:universal_objects/terminal} \hyperref[thm:categorical_principle_of_duality]{Dually}, we call the object \( T \in \cat{C} \) a \term[ru=конечный объект (\cite[80]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{terminal object} or \term{final object} if for any other object \( A \in \cat{C} \) there exists a unique morphism \( f: A \to T \).

    The initial and terminal objects are collectively called \term{universal objects}.

    \thmitem{def:universal_objects/zero} If \( Z \) is both an initial and a terminal object, we say that \( Z \) is a \term[ru=нулевой объект (\cite[80]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{null object} or \term{zero object}.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}\label{ex:def:universal_objects}
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{ex:def:universal_objects/set} In the category \hyperref[def:category_of_small_sets]{\( \cat{Set} \)} of small sets, for any set \( A \) there is a unique \hyperref[def:set_valued_map/empty]{empty function} from \( \varnothing \) to \( A \). Therefore, \( \varnothing \) is an \hyperref[def:universal_objects/initial]{initial object} in \( \cat{Set} \).

    For any set \( A \), there is a unique function that contracts any set \( B \) to \( \set{ A } \). Therefore, every singleton set is a \hyperref[def:universal_objects/terminal]{final object} in \( \cat{Set} \).

    We often denote the initial and terminal objects in \( \cat{Set} \) by \( 0 \) and \( 1 \) respectively, which corresponds to their definition as \hyperref[def:ordinal]{ordinals}.

    By \cref{thm:def:universal_objects/no_zero}, \( \cat{Set} \) has no zero object.

    \thmitem{ex:def:universal_objects/grp} In the category \hyperref[def:group/category]{\( \cat{Grp} \)} of small groups, the \hyperref[def:group/trivial]{trivial group} is a \hyperref[def:universal_objects/zero]{zero object}. This holds more generally for pointed sets rather than groups.

    Indeed, it can be embedded into any other group and any group can be contracted into the corresponding trivial group. Furthermore, all trivial groups are isomorphic.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:universal_object_duality}
  An object is \hyperref[def:universal_objects/initial]{initial} if and only if it is a \hyperref[def:universal_objects/terminal]{terminal object} the opposite category.

  This is part of the duality principles listed in \fullref{thm:categorical_principle_of_duality}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Trivial.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:def:universal_objects}
  \hfill
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:def:universal_objects/initial} An \hyperref[def:universal_objects/initial]{initial object} is unique up to an isomorphism.
    \thmitem{thm:def:universal_objects/terminal} \hyperref[thm:categorical_principle_of_duality]{Dually}, a \hyperref[def:universal_objects/initial]{terminal object} is also unique up to an isomorphism.
    \thmitem{thm:def:universal_objects/zero} If a category has an initial and a terminal object and if they are isomorphic, then both are zero objects.

    In particular, a zero object is unique up to an isomorphism.

    \thmitem{thm:def:universal_objects/no_zero} If an initial and a terminal object exists and are not isomorphic, then there exist no zero objects.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:universal_objects/initial} Suppose that \( A \) and \( B \) are both initial objects in \( \cat{C} \). Then there exist morphisms \( f: A \to B \) and \( g: B \to A \). Their composition \( g \bincirc f \) is an \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/endomorphism]{endomorphism} on \( A \).

  But there exists a unique \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/endomorphism]{endomorphism} on \( A \), which must be the identity \( \id_A \). Thus, \( g \bincirc f = \id_A \) and \( g \) is a left inverse of \( f \).

  We can analogously show that \( g \) is a right inverse of \( f \). Therefore, \( f \) is fully invertible, and \( A \) and \( B \) are isomorphic.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:universal_objects/terminal} If \( T' \) and \( T^\dprime \) are terminal objects in \( \cat{C} \), by \cref{thm:universal_object_duality}, they are initial objects in \( \cat{C}^\oppos \). By \cref{thm:def:universal_objects/initial}, they are isomorphic in \( \cat{C}^\oppos \) and by \cref{thm:morphism_invertibility_duality}, they are isomorphic in \( \cat{C} \).

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:universal_objects/zero} Suppose that \( A \) is an initial object and that \( B \) is a final object in \( \cat{C} \). Let \( f: A \to B \) be an isomorphism between them.

  Let \( C \in \cat{C} \) be any other object and let \( g: C \to B \) be the unique morphism to \( B \). Then \( f^{-1} \bincirc g: C \to A \) is a morphism from \( C \) to \( A \). The inverse \( f^{-1}: B \to A \) is unique by \cref{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/at_most_one_inverse}, therefore its composition with \( g: C \to B \) is also unique. Hence, any object has a unique morphism to \( A \). This makes \( A \) a terminal object and thus a zero object.

  We can analogously show that \( B \) is a zero object.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:universal_objects/no_zero} By \cref{thm:def:universal_objects/zero}, all zero objects are isomorphic. By \cref{thm:def:universal_objects/initial}, all initial objects are isomorphic and analogously for terminal objects. Hence, if a zero object exists, all initial objects are isomorphic to all terminal objects.

  If some initial object is not isomorphic to some terminal object, then by contraposition it follows that no zero object exists.
\end{proof}
