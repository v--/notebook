\paragraph{Cones and limits}

\begin{definition}\label{def:category_of_cones}\mimprovised
  We define the \term[en=category of cones (over \( D \)) (\cite[157]{Perrone2024StartingCategoryTheory})]{category of cones} over the \hyperref[def:categorical_diagram]{diagram} \( D: \cat{I} \to \cat{C} \) as the \hyperref[def:comma_category]{comma category}
  \begin{equation*}
    \cat{Cone}(D) \coloneqq \parens[\big]{ \Delta_{\cat{C}}^{\cat{I}}, \Delta_{[\cat{I}, \cat{C}]}^{\cat{1}}(D) }.
  \end{equation*}

  This definition features two \hyperref[def:diagonal_functor]{diagonal functors}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The functor \( \Delta_{\cat{C}}^{\cat{I}}: \cat{C} \to [\cat{I}, \cat{C}] \) maps \( \cat{C} \) to constant functors from \( \cat{I} \) to \( \cat{C} \).

    \item The functor \( \Delta_{[\cat{I}, \cat{C}]}^{\cat{I}}: [\cat{I}, \cat{C}] \to [\cat{1}, [\cat{I}, \cat{C}]] \) has a more complicated signature; its instance \( \Delta_{[\cat{I}, \cat{C}]}^{\cat{I}}(D): \cat{1} \to [\cat{I}, \cat{C}] \) maps the \hyperref[def:singleton_category]{terminal category} \( \cat{1} \) to \( D \).
  \end{itemize}

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:category_of_cones/objects} Unsurprisingly, we call the objects of this category \term[ru=конусы (\cite[22]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий}), en=cone (over \( D \)) (\cite[def. 3.1.2]{Perrone2024StartingCategoryTheory})]{cones} over \( D \). \begin{definition}\label{def:categorical_limit}\mcite[def. 5.16]{Awodey2010CategoryTheory}
  A \term[ru=предел (\cite[152]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{limit} of the \hyperref[def:categorical_diagram]{diagram} \( D: \cat{I} \to \cat{C} \) is a \hyperref[def:universal_objects/terminal]{terminal object} in the \hyperref[def:category_of_cones]{category of cones}. A limit is thus a \hyperref[def:universal_morphism/sink]{universal morphism}, for which reason it is also called a \term[ru=универсальный конус (\cite[33]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий}), en=universal cone (\cite[67]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory})]{universal cone}.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:categorical_limit_definition}
  In \cref{def:category_of_cones}, we define the category of cones via comma categories. This allows us to easily define a limit as a terminal object in \cref{def:categorical_limit}. We give an overview how different authors define cones and limits.

  \begin{itemize}
    \item \Textcite[101; 102]{Awodey2010CategoryTheory} constructs a category of cones by explicitly defining its objects and morphisms. This still allows him to define limits as terminal objects.

    \item \Textcite[67]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory} defines a limit as a universal morphism \enquote{from \( \Delta \) to \( D \)}. He only briefly mentions cones or cocones without going into detail. Still, his formalisms are close to ours.

    \item \Textcite[def. 5.1.19]{Leinster2014BasicCategoryTheory} and \textcite[\S 7.1]{Schubert1972Categories} define both cones and limits explicitly (without relying on comma categories or natural transformations).

    \item \Textcite[22; 32; 152]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий} also define cones and cocones explicitly. They define \enquote{universal cones} (\enquote{\textru{универсальные конусы}}) only for diagrams that are cocones, and later define the \enquote{limits} (\enquote{\textru{грани}}) for arbitrary diagrams.

    \item \Textcite[\S 3.1]{Perrone2024StartingCategoryTheory} defines a cone over \( D \) as a natural transformation from \( \Delta_{\cat{C}}^{\cat{I}}(X) \) to \( D \) for some object \( X \). He proceeds to define the presheaf \( \cat{Cone}(\anon*, D) \) on \( \cat{C} \) as follows:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Each object \( X \) goes to the set of cones with vertex \( X \).
      \item Each morphism \( g: Y \to X \) goes to the map transforming a cone \( \xi: \Delta_{\cat{C}}^{\cat{I}}(X) \Rightarrow D \) to \( \seq{ \xi_C \bincirc f }_{C \in \obj(\cat{C})} \).
    \end{itemize}

    Finally, he defines a limit as a \hyperref[def:functor_representation]{representation} of \( \cat{Cone}(\anon*, D) \). Later, in \cite[exerc. 3.2.48]{Perrone2024StartingCategoryTheory}, he asks proving that a limit cone is a terminal object in a slice category.

    We explain the connection between representations and universal morphisms in \cref{rem:representable_functor_universal_morphism}.

    Perrone's presentation is based on the less explicit one by \textcite[\S 3.1]{Riehl2016CategoryTheory}.
  \end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:cones_in_thin_categories}
  Let \( (P, \leq) \) be a \hyperref[def:partially_ordered_set]{partially ordered set} and let \( \cat{P} \) be its corresponding \hyperref[def:thin_category]{thin category} induced by \fullref{thm:order_category_isomorphism}.

  For any \hyperref[def:categorical_diagram]{diagram} \( D: \cat{I} \to \cat{P} \), denote the set of objects in its image by
  \begin{equation*}
    P_D \coloneqq \set{ D(i) \given i \in \obj(\cat{I}) }.
  \end{equation*}

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:cones_in_thin_categories/cone} The \hyperref[def:category_of_cones/objects]{cone} over \( D \) correspond to lower bound of \( P_D \).

    More precisely, if \( (x, \xi) \) is a \hyperref[def:category_of_cones/objects]{cone} over \( D \), then \( x \) is a lower bound of \( P_D \).

    Conversely, if \( x \) is a lower bound of \( D \) and \( \xi_i \) is defined by \( \xi_i = (x, D(i)) \), then \( (x, \xi) \) is a cone over \( D \).

    \thmitem{thm:cones_in_thin_categories/limit} The \hyperref[def:categorical_limit]{limits} of \( D \) correspond to infima of \( P_D \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item A dual statement holds for \hyperref[def:category_of_cocones/objects]{cocones} and \hyperref[def:categorical_colimit]{colimits}.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:cones_in_thin_categories/cone} If \( (x, \xi) \) is a cone over \( D \), the only information the natural transformation \( \xi \) carries is that there is a morphism \( \xi_i: x \to D(i) \) (that is, \( x \leq D(i) \)) for every object \( i \) in \( \cat{I} \).

  The morphisms in \( \cat{I} \) are irrelevant because the image of \( p: i \to j \) is a morphism from \( D(i) \) to \( D(j) \); such a morphism exists if and only if \( D(i) \leq D(j) \), so \( D \) is forced to obey this relation.

  Thus, \( x \) is a lower bound of \( P_D \). The converse is obvious.

  \SubProofOf{thm:cones_in_thin_categories/limit} Trivial considering \cref{thm:cones_in_thin_categories/cone}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:definition_via_product_universal_property}
  Consider the function \( f(t) = (\cos t, \sin t) \) mapping an angle to its Cartesian coordinates on the unit circle.

  The definition of this function relies on our ability to construct an arbitrary \hyperref[def:ordered_tuple]{ordered pair}. \Fullref{thm:cartesian_product_universal_property} provides an alternative that generalizes to other \hyperref[def:categorical_product]{categorical products}. In its full generality, this will be useful, for example, in \fullref{thm:product_category_universal_property}.

  Let \( \pi_1 \) and \( \pi_2 \) be the projections of \( \BbbR \times \BbbR \). Both \( \cos \) and \( \sin \) are endofunctions on \( \BbbR \), hence \( (\BbbR, \set{ \cos, \sin }) \) is a \hyperref[def:category_of_cones]{cone} for the product diagram. By the universal property, there exists a \emph{unique} function \( l: \BbbR \to \BbbR \times \BbbR \) such that \( \pi_1 \bincirc l = \cos \) and \( \pi_2 \bincirc l = \sin \).

  By \cref{thm:indexed_family_extensionality}, we have \( l(t) = (\cos t, \sin t) = f(t) \) for every \( t \).
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:equalizer_is_monomorphism}
  For every \hyperref[def:categorical_equalizer]{equalizer cone} \( (L, \iota) \), the morphism \( \iota \) is a \hyperref[def:morphism_cancellation/left]{monomorphism}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Fix two parallel morphisms \( h_1, h_2: X \to A \) such that
  \begin{equation*}
    \iota \bincirc h_1 = \iota \bincirc h_2.
  \end{equation*}

  Since \( f \bincirc \iota = g \bincirc \iota \), we have
  \begin{equation*}
    f \bincirc (\iota \bincirc h_1) = g \bincirc (\iota \bincirc h_1),
  \end{equation*}
  and thus both \( (X, \iota \bincirc h_1) \) and \( (X, \iota \bincirc h_2) \) are cones.

  From the universal property, it follows that there exists a unique morphism \( l_X: X \to A \) such that the following diagram commutes:
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
      \includegraphics[page=1]{output/thm__equalizer_is_monomorphism}
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}

  Because of the uniqueness of \( l_X \), we conclude that \( l_X = h_1 = h_2 \). Since \( h_1 \) and \( h_2 \) were arbitrary, it follows that \( \iota \) is a monomorphism.
\end{proof}

    \thmitem{ex:def:categorical_equalizer/set} We will first analyze equalizers in the \hyperref[def:category_of_small_sets]{category of small sets} \( \cat{Set} \).

    Fix small sets \( A \) and \( B \) and a pair of parallel functions \( f, g: A \to B \). Define the set
    \begin{equation*}
      L \coloneqq \set{ x \in A \given f(x) = g(x) }.
    \end{equation*}

    With the inclusion map \( \iota: L \to A \), this is an equalizer cone for \( f \) and \( g \).

    We will now prove that it is a limit cone. For any cone \( (X, \xi) \), we must find a function \( l_X: X \to L \) such that the following diagram commutes:
    \begin{equation*}
      \begin{aligned}
        \includegraphics[page=1]{output/ex__def__categorical_equalizer}
      \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}

    In order for \( (X, \xi) \) to be a cone, the set-theoretic image \( \xi[X] \) must be a subset of \( L \). This enforces the definition of \( l_A \) as the \hyperref[def:set_valued_map/restriction]{corestriction} of \( \xi \) to \( L \).

    It follows that \( (L, \iota) \) is a limit cone.

    \thmitem{ex:def:categorical_equalizer/fol} Equalizers in the \hyperref[def:fol_theory_model_functor/obj]{category of small models} \( \cat{Mod}(T) \) of an \hyperref[def:fol_equational_theory]{equational theory} \( T \) are similar to those in the category of small sets.

    More concretely, fix \hyperref[def:fol_theory/model]{models} \( \mscrX = (X, I) \) and \( \mscrY = (Y, I) \) of \( T \) and parallel \hyperref[def:fol_homomorphism]{homomorphisms} \( g, h: \mscrX \to \mscrY \). Let \( \mscrL = (L, I\restr_L) \) be the substructure \hyperref[def:fol_generated_substructure]{generated} by the set-theoretic equalizer
    \begin{equation*}
      \set{ x \in X \given g(x) = h(x) }.
    \end{equation*}

    \Cref{thm:fol_equational_theory_models/substructure} implies that \( \mscrL \) is a submodel of \( \mscrX \).

    With the inclusion homomorphism, \( \mscrL \) becomes an equalizer in \( \cat{Mod}(T) \).

    \thmitem{ex:def:categorical_equalizer/cat} Equalizers in the \hyperref[def:category_of_small_categories]{category of small categories} are more interesting.

    Consider two parallel functors \( F, G: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) and define the sets
    \begin{align*}
      L_{\obj} &\coloneqq \set{ A \in \obj(\cat{C}) \given F(A) = G(A) }, \\
      L_{\mor} &\coloneqq \set{ f \in \mor(\cat{C}) \given F(f) = G(f) }.
    \end{align*}

    We must show that, with operations inherited from \( \cat{C} \), \( L_{\obj} \) and \( L_{\mor} \) constitute a well-defined category \( \cat{L} \):
    \begin{itemize}
      \item The endpoints of morphisms from \( L_{\mor} \) belong to \( L_{\obj} \) because, by \ref{def:functor/CF0}, for every morphism \( f: A \to B \) in \( L_{\mor} \), we have
      \begin{equation*}
        F(A) = F(\dom f) = \dom F(f) = \dom G(f) = G(\dom f) = F(A),
      \end{equation*}
      and similarly \( F(B) = G(B) \).

      \item The identity morphisms of objects from \( L_{\obj} \) belong to \( L_{\mor} \) because, by \ref{def:functor/CF1}, for every \( A \) in \( L_{\obj} \) we have
      \begin{equation*}
        F(\id_A) = \id_{F(A)} = \id_{G(A)} = G(\id_A),
      \end{equation*}

      \item \( L_{\mor} \) is closed under composition: if \( f: A \to B \) and \( g: B \to C \) belong to \( L_{\mor} \), so does \( g \bincirc f \), because, by \ref{def:functor/CF2}, we have
      \begin{equation*}
        F(g \bincirc f) = F(g) \bincirc F(f) = G(g) \bincirc G(f) = G(g \bincirc f).
      \end{equation*}
    \end{itemize}

    Thus defined, \( \cat{L} \) is a subcategory of \( \cat{C} \), and, with the inclusion functor \( \iota: \cat{L} \to \cat{C} \), it is an equalizer cone for \( F \) and \( G \).

    We want to define, for any cone \( (\cat{X}, \xi) \), a unique functor \( L_{\cat{X}} \) so that the following diagram commutes:
    \begin{equation*}
      \begin{aligned}
        \includegraphics[page=2]{output/ex__def__categorical_equalizer}
      \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}

    Similarly to the category of small sets, this amounts to defining \( L_{\cat{X}} \) as the corestriction of \( \xi \) to \( \cat{L} \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{example}

\begin{definition}\label{def:complete_category}\mcite[def. 7.4.1]{Schubert1972Categories}
  We say that a category is \term[ru=полная (категория) (\cite[154]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{complete} if it has all \hyperref[def:categorical_limit/small]{small limits}; that is, if every \hyperref[def:categorical_diagram/small]{small diagram} has a limit.

  In accordance with \cref{rem:cardinality_auxiliary_terminology}, depending on the \hyperref[def:categorical_diagram/cardinality]{diagram cardinality} allowed, we additionally use cardinality-related terminology like \enquote{finitely complete}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:category_completeness_via_products_and_equalizers}\mcite[thm. 7.4.2]{Schubert1972Categories}
  A category is \hyperref[def:complete_category]{complete} (resp. finitely complete) if and only if it has all \hyperref[def:categorical_equalizer]{equalizers} and \hyperref[def:categorical_diagram/small]{small} (resp. finite) \hyperref[def:categorical_product]{products}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item We will prove only the case of small limits; for finite limits, it suffices to substitute \enquote{small} for \enquote{finite} in the proof.

  In this case, because of the cardinality technicalities for finite diagrams described in \cref{rem:categorical_diagram_definition/cardinality}, it is important for the index category \( \cat{I} \) to be finite (and not merely be the \hyperref[def:free_category]{free category} of a finite graph).
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  \SufficiencySubProof If a category is complete, by definition it has all small limits, including equalizers and products.

  \NecessitySubProof Suppose that \( \cat{C} \) has all equalizers and small products. Consider an arbitrary small diagram \( D: \cat{I}
  \to \cat{C} \). To avoid the cardinality considerations arising from cycles, which we describe in \cref{def:categorical_diagram/cardinality}, we assume that \( \cat{I} \) is small (resp. finite); if not, we can redefine \( D \) based on another category.

  Define the products
  \begin{align*}
    A &\coloneqq \prod_{\mathclap{i \in \obj(\cat{I})}} D(i), \\
    B &\coloneqq \prod_{\mathclap{p \in \mor(\cat{I})}} D(\cod(p)).
  \end{align*}

  Let \( \varphi_i: A \to D(i) \) the projection of \( A \) at \( i \) and let \( \psi_p: B \to D(\cod(p)) \) be the projection of \( B \) at \( p \). As in \cref{rem:definition_via_product_universal_property}, the universal property of products gives us morphisms \( f, g: A \to B \) such that, for every morphism \( p: i \to j \) in \( \cat{I} \),
  \begin{align*}

    \psi_p \bincirc g &= D(p) \bincirc \varphi_i.
  \end{align*}

  Let \( (L, \iota) \) be an equalizer cone for \( f \) and \( g \). For every morphism \( p: i \to j \) in \( \cat{I} \), the following diagram must commute:

\begin{corollary}\label{thm:category_of_small_sets_is_complete}

\begin{definition}\label{def:coproduct_category}\mimprovised
  We define the \term{coproduct category} \( \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} \cat{C}_k \) of the \hyperref[def:indexed_family]{indexed family} \( \seq{ \cat{C}_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \) of categories as follows:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:coproduct_category/objects} The \hyperref[def:category/objects]{set of objects} is the \hyperref[def:disjoint_union]{disjoint union}
    \begin{equation*}
      \obj\parens*{ \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} \cat{C}_k } \coloneqq \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} \obj(\cat{C}_k).
    \end{equation*}

    \thmitem{def:coproduct_category/morphisms} The \hyperref[def:category/morphisms]{set of morphisms} is similarly the disjoint union
    \begin{equation*}
      \mor\parens*{ \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} \cat{C}_k } \coloneqq \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} \mor(\cat{C}_k).
    \end{equation*}

    For every index \( m \) in \( \mscrK \), we combine the inclusion functions of objects and morphisms of \( \cat{C}_k \) into a functor
    \begin{equation*}
      \iota_m: \cat{C}_m \to \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} \cat{C}_k.
    \end{equation*}

    For the morphism \( \iota_k(f) \), where \( f: A \to B \) is a morphism in \( \cat{C}_k \), the domain and codomain are \( \iota_k(A) \) and \( \iota_k(B) \), respectively.

    \thmitem{def:coproduct_category/composition} \hyperref[def:category/composition]{Composition} in \( \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} \cat{C}_k \) is defined in an obvious way for only morphisms in the same underlying category.

    \thmitem{def:coproduct_category/identity} The \hyperref[def:category/identity]{identity morphism} on \( \iota_k(A) \) is the corresponding tuple of identity morphisms.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}

\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:coequalizer_is_epimorphism}
  For every \hyperref[def:categorical_coequalizer]{coequalizer cone} \( (L, \iota) \), the morphism \( \iota \) is a \hyperref[def:morphism_cancellation/left]{epimorphism}.

\begin{example}\label{ex:def:categorical_coequalizer}
  We list some examples of \hyperref[def:categorical_coequalizer]{coequalizers}:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{ex:def:categorical_coequalizer/set} We will first analyze equalizers in the \hyperref[def:category_of_small_sets]{category of small sets} \( \cat{Set} \).

    Fix small sets \( A \) and \( B \) and a pair of parallel functions \( f, g: A \to B \). Let \( {\cong} \) be the equivalence relation on \( B \) generated, in accordance with \cref{thm:generated_equivalence_relation}, by the relation \( {\sim} \) defined by \( f(a) \sim g(a) \) for every \( a \) in \( A \).

    Let \( L \) be the partition \( B / {\cong} \) and let \( \pi: B \to L \) be the corresponding projection map. Then \( (L, \pi) \) is a cocone because, by definition, \( \pi(f(a)) = \pi(g(a)) \) for every \( a \) in \( A \).

    Fix a cocone \( (X, \xi) \). We will now construct a map \( l_Y: L \to Y \) such that the following diagram commutes:
    \begin{equation*}
      \begin{aligned}
        \includegraphics[page=1]{output/ex__coequalizers_in_set}
      \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}

    In particular, we must have \( l_X(\pi(b)) = \xi(b) \). We can use this as a definition as long \( \xi(b) = \xi(b') \) whenever \( b \cong b' \).

    We will use \fullref{thm:induction_on_recursively_defined_relations} on \( b \cong b' \) to show that \( \xi(b) = \xi(b') \).
    \begin{itemize}
      \item If \( b \cong b' \) due to \ref{inf:thm:generated_equivalence_relation/intro}, there exists some \( a \) such that \( b = f(a) \) and \( b' = g(a) \). Then \( \xi(f(a)) = \xi(g(a)) \) since \( (X, \xi) \) is a cocone.

      \item If \( b \cong b' \) due to \ref{inf:thm:generated_equivalence_relation/refl}, then \( b = b' \) and clearly \( \xi(b) = \xi(b') \).

      \item If \( b \cong b' \) due to \ref{inf:thm:generated_equivalence_relation/symm}, then \( b' \cong b \), to which the inductive hypothesis applies.

      Then \( \xi(b') = \xi(b) \), so naturally \( \xi(b) = \xi(b') \).

      \item Finally, if \( b \cong b' \) due to \ref{inf:thm:generated_equivalence_relation/trans}, there exists some \( b^\dprime \) such that \( b \cong b^\dprime \) and \( b^\dprime \cong b' \), and to which the inductive hypothesis applies.

      Then \( \xi(b) = \xi(b^\dprime) = \xi(b') \).
    \end{itemize}

    Therefore, we can indeed define \( l_X(\pi(b)) \) as \( \xi(b) \), and this map would satisfy the universal property. Uniqueness of \( l_X \) follows from the requirement that \( l_X(\pi(b)) \) must equal \( \xi(b) \).

    This example will be important for us when discussing cokernels in certain categories of first-order models in \cref{thm:equational_theory_zero_morphisms}.

    \thmitem{ex:def:categorical_coequalizer/fol} Coequalizers in the \hyperref[def:fol_theory_model_functor/obj]{category of small models} of an \hyperref[def:fol_equational_theory]{equational theory} are similar to those in the category of small sets.

    More concretely, fix models \( \mscrX = (X, I) \) and \( \mscrY = (Y, I) \) of \( T \) and parallel homomorphisms \( g, h: \mscrX \to \mscrY \). We again start with the relation \( {\sim} \) defined by \( g(x) \sim h(x) \) for every \( x \) in \( X \). Rather than working with the \hyperref[def:equivalence_closure]{equivalence closure} of \( {\sim} \), however, we must use its \hyperref[def:fol_generated_congruence]{generated congruence}. Denote this congruence by \( {\cong} \).

    Then, by \cref{thm:fol_equational_theory_models/quotient}, the \hyperref[def:fol_quotient_structure]{quotient} \( \mscrY / {\cong} \) is a model of \( T \). With the projection homomorphism \( \pi \), \( \mscrY / {\cong} \) becomes a coequalizer in \( \cat{Mod}(T) \).

    To show correctness for a given cone \( (\mscrA, \alpha) \), we must again proceed by \fullref{thm:induction_on_recursively_defined_relations} on \( y \cong y' \) to show that \( \alpha(y) = \alpha(y') \), with the additional case \ref{inf:def:fol_generated_congruence/fun} covered by \( \pi \) being a homomorphism.

    \thmitem{ex:def:categorical_coequalizer/cat} Coequalizers in the \hyperref[def:category_of_small_categories]{category of small categories} are similar, but ordinary \hyperref[def:congruence_in_category]{congruences of categories} are insufficient to describe them since they only allow identifying morphisms.

    \Textcite[def. 3.6]{BednarczykEtAl1999GeneralizedCongruences} define a \enquote{generalized congruence} by combining an ordinary congruence with an equivalence relations on object and imposing several compatibility conditions.

    The authors then show that a coequalizer of \( F, G: \cat{C} \to \cat{D} \) can be constructed using the generalized equivalence generated by the relation \( {\sim} \) defined by \( F(A) \sim G(A) \) and \( F(f) \sim G(f) \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{example}
\begin{comments}
  \item See also \cref{ex:def:categorical_equalizer} for a discussion of equalizers.
\end{comments}

\begin{definition}\label{def:cocomplete_category}\mcite[def. 8.4.1]{Schubert1972Categories}
  We say that a category is \term[ru=кополная (категория) (\cite[157]{ЦаленкоШульгейфер1974ОсновыТеорииКатегорий})]{cocomplete} if it has all \hyperref[def:categorical_colimit/small]{small colimits}; that is, if every \hyperref[def:categorical_diagram/small]{small diagram} has a colimit.

  In accordance with \cref{rem:cardinality_auxiliary_terminology}, depending on the \hyperref[def:categorical_diagram/cardinality]{diagram cardinality} allowed, we additionally use cardinality-related terminology like \enquote{finitely cocomplete}.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:categorical_limit_duality}
  For every \hyperref[def:category_of_cones/objects]{cone} \( (X, \xi) \) of the \hyperref[def:categorical_diagram]{diagram} \( D: \cat{I} \to \cat{C} \) in \( \cat{C} \), \( (X, \xi^\oppos) \) is a \hyperref[def:category_of_cones/objects]{cocone} of \( D^\oppos \) in the \hyperref[def:opposite_category]{opposite category} \( \cat{C}^\oppos \).

  Moreover, the cone \( (L, \pi) \) over \( D \) is a \hyperref[def:categorical_limit]{limit} (resp. \hyperref[def:categorical_product]{product}, \hyperref[def:categorical_equalizer]{equalizer} or \hyperref[def:projective_limit]{projective limit}) if and only if \( (L, \pi^\oppos) \) is a \hyperref[def:categorical_colimit]{colimit} (resp. \hyperref[def:categorical_coproduct]{coproduct}, \hyperref[def:categorical_coequalizer]{coequalizer} or \hyperref[def:inductive_limit]{inductive limit}).
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item This is part of the categorical duality principles listed in \cref{ex:thm:category_duality}.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  All relevant pairs of diagrams are dual.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:category_cocompleteness_via_coproducts_and_coequalizers}\mcite[thm. 7.4.2]{Schubert1972Categories}
  A category is \hyperref[def:cocomplete_category]{cocomplete} (resp. finitely cocomplete) if and only if it has all \hyperref[def:categorical_coequalizer]{coequalizers} and \hyperref[def:categorical_diagram/small]{small} (resp. finite) \hyperref[def:categorical_coproduct]{coproducts}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SufficiencySubProof If a category is cocomplete, by definition it has all small colimits, including coequalizers and coproducts.

  \SufficiencySubProof Suppose that \( \cat{C} \) has coequalizers and small coproducts.

  By \cref{thm:categorical_limit_duality}, \( \cat{C}^\oppos \) has equalizers and small products, so \cref{thm:category_completeness_via_products_and_equalizers} implies that \( \cat{C}^\oppos \) is complete.

  Again by duality, we conclude that \( \cat{C} \) is cocomplete.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{thm:category_of_small_sets_is_cocomplete}
  The \hyperref[def:category_of_small_sets]{category of small sets} is \hyperref[def:cocomplete_category]{cocomplete}.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  \Fullref{thm:disjoint_union_universal_property} implies that \( \cat{Set} \) has arbitrary coproducts. We show in \cref{ex:def:categorical_coequalizer/set} that \( \cat{Set} \) has coequalizers. Then, by \cref{thm:category_cocompleteness_via_coproducts_and_coequalizers}, \( \cat{Set} \) has all small colimits.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{thm:category_of_small_categories_is_cocomplete}
  The \hyperref[def:category_of_small_categories]{category of small categories} is \hyperref[def:cocomplete_category]{cocomplete}.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  \Fullref{thm:coproduct_category_universal_property} implies that \( \cat{Cat} \) has arbitrary coproducts. We show in \cref{ex:def:categorical_equalizer/cat} that \( \cat{Cat} \) has coequalizers. Then, by \cref{thm:category_cocompleteness_via_coproducts_and_coequalizers}, \( \cat{Cat} \) has all small colimits.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Projective and inductive limits}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:direct_and_inverse_limit_terminology}
  When referring to diagrams and limits, the terms \enquote{projective} and \enquote{inductive}, as well as \enquote{inverse} and \enquote{direct}, may be ambiguous when applied without context.

  We prefer the first pair of terms because it is less confusing: direct limits correspond to colimits, which can be considered inverse to general categorical limits.

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{rem:direct_and_inverse_limit_terminology/inverse_system} A diagram \( D: \cat{I} \to \cat{C} \), where \( \cat{I} \) is thin, is often called either a \enquote{direct system} or \enquote{inverse system}, depending on whether we are interested in \( \cat{C} \) or \( \cat{C}^{\oppos} \). The categorical colimits of a direct system are called \enquote{direct limits}, while the categorical limits of an inverse system are called \enquote{inverse limits}.

    This terminology is usually used in literature that avoids general categorical constructions. \Textcite[315]{Willard2004GeneralTopology} writes
    \begin{displayquote}
      Inverse limits (29.9 and 29C) were introduced in topology by Alexandroff (\textit{Untersuchungen iiber Gestalt und Lage abgeschlossenes Mengen}). They are useful in the theory of compact topological groups. See, for example, Weil (\textit{L’intégration dans les Groupes Topologiques et ses Applications}) or Pontrjagin (\textit{Topological Groups}). In all these sources and other references from earlier periods, they are called projective limits (of inverse systems).
    \end{displayquote}

    His definition of inverse limits is based on \enquote{inverse limit sequences}, corresponding to our \hyperref[def:projective_sequence]{projective sequences}. \enquote{Inverse sequences} are also defined for topological spaces in \cite[98]{Engelking1989GeneralTopology}, who however also considers more general \enquote{inverse systems} indexed by preordered sets.

    This latter definition, based on preordered sets, is given by Bourbaki for sets in \cite[\S III.7]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets}, and then adapted to algebraic and topological structures in their other books, all without explicit mentions of categories. Bourbaki also define direct systems and limits.

    Inverse and direct limits are defined in a categorical language in \cite[ch. B-7]{Rotman2015AdvancedModernAlgebraPart1} and \cite[\S 2.5]{Jacobson1985BasicAlgebraI}, without mentioning (co)cones and general (co)limits.

    \thmitem{rem:direct_and_inverse_limit_terminology/sequences} We define inverse and direct sequences in \cref{def:projective_sequence} and \cref{def:inductive_sequence}, correspondingly, based on the general definition from \cite[158; 163]{Perrone2024StartingCategoryTheory}. As already mentioned earlier, we prefer calling them \enquote{projective} and \enquote{inductive} sequences to avoid possible confusion.

    \thmitem{rem:direct_and_inverse_limit_terminology/limits} In a general categorical context, \textcite[55; 68]{MacLane1998CategoryTheory} and \textcite[def. 7.1.2; def. 8.1.2]{Schubert1972Categories} introduce both \enquote{inverse limit} and \enquote{projective limit} as dated synonyms for general categorical limits, and \enquote{direct limit} and \enquote{inductive limit} as dated synonyms for colimits.

    \Textcite[def. 3.1.21]{Riehl2016CategoryTheory} defines an \enquote{inverse limit} as a limit indexed by \( \omega^\oppos \) --- the opposite of the \hyperref[thm:smallest_inductive_set_existence]{smallest inductive set} \( \omega \) considered as a thin category as per \fullref{thm:order_category_isomorphism}. This is equivalent to a limit of a projective sequence.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{remark}
