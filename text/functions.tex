\section{Functions}\label{sec:functions}

\paragraph{Set-valued functions}

\begin{concept}\label{con:variable_dependence}
  Consider the following \hyperref[def:fol_formula]{first-order formula}:
  \begin{equation*}
    \qforall \synx \qexists \syny \synp(\synx, \syny).
  \end{equation*}

  Both \hyperref[con:variable]{variables} here are \hyperref[con:variable_binding]{bound}, but semantically they play distinct roles --- for the formula to be satisfied, the values which \( \synx \) can take are unrestricted, while the values of \( \syny \) depend on both \( \synx \) and \( \synp(\synx, \syny) \). This is the essence of \fullref{alg:fol_skolemization}.

  We refer to this dependency as a \term[ru=соотвествие (\cite[\S 1.2.5]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ}), en=correspondence (\cite[30]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory})]{correspondence} or a \term[en=map (\cite[30]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory})]{map}/\term[ru=отображение (\cite[\S 1.2.4]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ}), en=mapping (\cite[30]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory})]{mapping}. We call \( \synx \) the \term[bg=независима променлива (\cite[127]{Тагамлицки1971ДиференциалноСмятане}), ru=независимая (переменная) (\cite[39]{ИльинСадовничийСендов1985АнализТом1}), en=independent variable (\cite[74]{Kleene2002Logic})]{independent variable} of the correspondence, and to \( \syny \) as the \term[bg=зависима променлива (\cite[127]{Тагамлицки1971ДиференциалноСмятане}), ru=зависимая переменная (\cite[39]{ИльинСадовничийСендов1985АнализТом1}), en=dependent variable (\cite[74]{Kleene2002Logic})]{dependent variable}.

  If a given \hyperref[con:logical_model]{model}, we can express \( \syny \) as a \hyperref[def:function]{set-valued function} of \( \synx \); we call it an \term[bg=неявна функция (\cite[377]{Тагамлицки1971ДиференциалноСмятане}), ru=неявная функция (\cite[314]{Фихтенгольц1968ОсновыАнализаТом2}), en=implicitly-defined function (\cite[29]{DontchevRockafellar2014SolutionMappings})]{implicit function}.
\end{concept}
\begin{comments}
  \item Formal definitions of the term \enquote{correspondence} are given in the literature for several related concepts --- see \cref{rem:function_definition}.

  \item Formally, correspondences manifest themselves as single-valued and set-valued functions, which we discuss in this section, as well as the purely syntactic \( \muplambda \)-term abstractions discussed in \fullref{ch:lambda_calculus}.

  As per \cref{rem:cartesian_product_definition} (and \cref{rem:function_definition}), we have defined indexed families in \cref{sec:relations} as more primitive precursors of single-valued functions.
\end{comments}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:function_definition}
  In the literature, functions are often conflated with their graphs, which precludes a formal treatment of function images and related concepts like surjectivity. Additionally, set-valued functions, which we discuss in \cref{def:set_valued_function}, are not an established concept and their treatment is often delegated to a more general discussion of relations.

  To address both, we proceed as follows:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item We have already introduced indexed families in \cref{def:indexed_family}. These act as \hyperref[con:variable_dependence]{correspondences} that, unlike functions, are determined solely by their graphs.

    Based on indexed families, we have defined Cartesian products and relations in \cref{sec:relations}.

    \item In \cref{def:set_valued_function}, we introduce set-valued functions from \( A \) to \( B \) as \hyperref[def:binary_relation]{binary relations} \( F \subseteq A \times B \) with a fixed \enquote{type signature} \( A \to B \) (encoded \( (R, A, B) \)).

    \item Finally, we introduce the usual single-valued functions in \cref{def:function} as a special case of set-valued functions.

    Functions are thus determined both by their graphs and by their signatures. Even though this makes functions conceptually different from indexed families, we often use them interchangeably. For example, when stating formal axioms like the axiom of choice in \cref{def:zfc/choice} or the axiom of universes in \cref{def:axiom_of_universes}, we use indexed families in the object theory and treat them as functions in the metatheory.
  \end{itemize}

  As mentioned in the beginning, this approach differs from what is established in the literature. We list approaches used in different sources.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Indexed families are not usually defined as standalone entities but are introduced as special cases of functions. As explained in \cref{rem:cartesian_product_definition}, we make a formal distinction between an indexed family and a function for the purpose of streamlining our chain of definitions.

    A thorough discussion of indexed families can be found in \cite[\S 9]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory}. Halmos defines them as functions, but treats them like we do.

    \item \Textcite[def. II.3.2]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets} define \enquote{correspondences} (\enquote{\textfr{correspondance}} in French) as relations (\enquote{graphs}) with type signatures, exactly how we define set-valued functions.

    Some authors like \textcite[17]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ} follow this definition. Without type signatures (i.e. conflated with their graphs), set-valued functions are introduced under different names (mostly in non-set-theoretic literature):
    \begin{itemize}
      \item The term \enquote{correspondence} (\enquote{\textru{соответствие}} in Russian) is used in this sense in
      \cite[77]{Lang2002Algebra},
      \cite[253]{ПоловинкинБалашов2007ВыпуклыйАнализ} and
      \cite[49]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика}.

      On the other hand, the term is used for single-valued functions in
      \cite[12]{Jech2006SetTheory},
      \cite[30]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory} and
      \cite[12]{Курош1973ОбщаяАлгебра}.

      \item The phrase \enquote{set-valued map} is used for set-valued functions in \cite[34]{AubinFrankowska1990SetValuedAnalysis} and, with only an informal definition, in \cite[2]{DontchevRockafellar2014SolutionMappings}.

      This is not to be confused with \enquote{set-valued functors}, as used in \cite[\S 8.1]{Awodey2010CategoryTheory}, which are simply \hyperref[def:functor]{functors} into the \hyperref[def:category_of_small_sets]{category of small sets}.

      Aubin and Frankowska list alternatives such as \enquote{correspondence} (discussed above), \enquote{multifunction} and \enquote{multivalued function}, all of which can be found in the literature.

      \item For a for a function into a power set, the phrase \enquote{multivalued function} (\enquote{\textru{многозначное изображение}}) and its variants are introduced in
      \cite[278]{Deimling1985NonlinearFA},
      \cite[3]{GondranMinoux1984GraphsAndAlgorithms},
      \cite[42]{ИоффеТихомиров1974ЭкстремальныеЗадачи} and
      \cite[147]{ФедорчукФилиппов2006ОбщаяТопология},
      and with only an informal definition in
      \cite[113]{Арутюнов2014ВыпуклыйАнализ}.
    \end{itemize}

    \item Single-valued functions, as one of the fundamental notions in all of mathematics, are extensively discussed in all areas.

    Still, the definition of a function \( f: A \to B \) as the relation \( f \subset A \times B \) is prevalent, used in set theory literature like
    \cite[def. I.6.3]{Kunen2013SetTheory},
    \cite[11]{Jech2006SetTheory},
    \cite[43]{Enderton1977SetTheory},
    \cite[30]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory},
    \cite[def. 6.2.8]{Hinman2005Logic},
    \cite[xxi]{Mendelson2015Logic},
    \cite[143]{КолмогоровДрагалин2006ЛогикаДополнительныеГлавы},
    \cite[127]{Герасимов2014Вычислимость} and
    \cite[\S 1.2.4]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ} (despite later defining \enquote{\textru{соответствия}} (\enquote{correspondences}) as relations with signatures).

    \Textcite[26]{Kelley1975GeneralTopology} explicitly states
    \begin{displayquote}
      We do not distinguish between a function and its graph.
    \end{displayquote}

    The most notable exception to this convention is \cite[def. II.3.4]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets}, who call the above a \enquote{functional graph} (\enquote{\textfr{graphe fonctionne}} in French), and proceed to define a function \( f: A \to B \) as a triple \( (\gr f, A, B) \).

    This is followed by authors in other areas, for example
    \cite[2]{Harzheim2005OrderedSets} and
    \cite[14]{AdámekHerrlichStrecker2004JoyOfCats}.
  \end{itemize}
\end{remark}
\begin{comments}
  \item A consequence of treating functions as triples is that the concept of a \enquote{small function} (with respect to a \hyperref[def:grothendieck_universe]{Grothendieck universe}) changes --- see \cref{thm:small_set_closure/function}.
\end{comments}

\begin{definition}\label{def:set_valued_function}\mimprovised
  A \term[ru=многозначное отображение (\cite[253]{ПоловинкинБалашов2007ВыпуклыйАнализ}), en=set-valued map (\cite[def. 1.3.1]{AubinFrankowska1990SetValuedAnalysis})]{set-valued map} from \( A \) to \( B \) is an ordered triple \( F = (R, A, B) \), where \( R \subseteq A \times B \) is a \hyperref[def:binary_relation]{binary relation}.

  We call \( (A, B) \) the \term[en=type signature (\cite{HaskellWiki:type_signature})]{type signature} of \( F \) and denote it by \( A \multto B \), resembling \hyperref[con:type_annotation]{type annotations} for \hyperref[def:arrow_type]{arrow types}\fnote{For \hyperref[def:function]{single-valued functions}, the notation \( f: A \to B \) coincides with that for \hyperref[def:type_theory_function_term]{function terms} (even though formally these are very distinct objects).}. We denote the function itself (as a triple) by \( F: A \multto B \).

  We introduce several important notions:
  \begin{thmenum}[series=def:set_valued_function]
    \thmitem{def:set_valued_function/graph}\mcite[def. 1.3.1]{AubinFrankowska1990SetValuedAnalysis} We call the relation \( R \) the \term{graph}\fnote{The term \enquote{graph} in this context is unrelated to the different types of graphs defined in \fullref{sec:graphs}.} of \( F \) and denote it by \( \gr(F) \).

    \thmitem{def:set_valued_function/value}\mcite[def. 1.3.1]{AubinFrankowska1990SetValuedAnalysis} We define the \term{value} of \( F \) at \( x \) as
    \begin{equation*}
      F(x) \coloneqq \set{ y \in B \given (x, y) \in \gr(F) }.
    \end{equation*}

    We also call \( F(x) \) the \term{image} or \term[en=action (\cite[9]{Aluffi2009Algebra})]{action} of \( F \) at \( x \).

    The notation \( F(x) \) refers to a member of \( B \) if \( x \) is a \hyperref[con:variable_binding]{bound variable} and to the function \( F \) itself if \( x \) is a \hyperref[con:variable_binding]{free variable}.

    \thmitem{def:set_valued_function/value_cardinality}\mcite[3]{DontchevRockafellar2014SolutionMappings} We set apart the following cases for the value \( F(x) \):
    \begin{thmenum}
      \thmitem{def:set_valued_function/value_cardinality/empty} If \( F(x) \) is the empty set, we say that \( F \) is \term{empty-valued} at \( x \).

      \thmitem{def:set_valued_function/value_cardinality/single} If \( F(x) \) is a singleton set, we say that \( F \) is \term{single-valued} at \( x \).

      \thmitem{def:set_valued_function/value_cardinality/multi} Otherwise, we say that \( F(x) \) is a is \term{multi-valued} at \( x \).
    \end{thmenum}

    \thmitem{def:set_valued_function/domain}\mcite[def. 1.3.1]{AubinFrankowska1990SetValuedAnalysis} We define the \term[ru=область определения (\cite[\S 1.2.4]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ})]{domain} of \( F \) as the subset of \( A \) for which \( F \) is non-empty-valued:
    \begin{equation*}
      \dom(F) \coloneqq \set{ x \in A \given F(x) \neq \varnothing }.
    \end{equation*}

    \thmitem{def:set_valued_function/image}\mcite[def. II.3.3]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets} We define the \term[ru=образ (\cite[\S 1.2.5]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ})]{image} of a subset \( X \subseteq A \) under \( F \) as
    \begin{equation*}
      F[X] \coloneqq \bigcup \set{ F(x) \given x \in X }.
    \end{equation*}

    We can also denote the image by \( F(X) \), although this may be ambiguous --- see \cref{rem:notation_for_function_image}.

    If \( X = A \), we call the set \( F[A] \) the \term[ru=область значений (\cite[\S 1.2.5]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ})]{image} of \( F \) and denote it by \( \img(F) \).

    \thmitem{def:set_valued_function/codomain} We define the \term{codomain} \( \cod(F) \) of \( F \) as the entire set \( B \).

    The reason for the inconsistency with the term \enquote{domain} is due to the terminology coming from \hyperref[def:function]{single-valued functions}, where the domain is required to be the entirety of \( A \).
  \end{thmenum}

  The following terminology is consistent with relations:
  \begin{thmenum}[resume=def:set_valued_function]
    \thmitem{def:set_valued_function/empty} We say that \( F: A \multto B \) is \term{empty} if \( \gr(F) = \varnothing \), i.e. if the graph is empty in the sense of \cref{def:relation/empty}.

    \thmitem{def:set_valued_function/identity}\mcite[82]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets} On every set \( A \), we define the \term[ru=тождественная функция (\cite[33]{ШеньВерещагин2020НачалаТеорииМножеств}), en=identity mapping (\cite[82]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets})]{identity function} \( \id_A \) using the diagonal relation \( \Delta_A \) from \cref{def:binary_relation/diagonal}.

    It is vacuously a single-valued function.

    \thmitem{def:set_valued_function/inverse}\mcite[def. II.2.5]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets} We define the \term[ru=обратное (соответствие) (\cite[\S 1.2.6]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ})]{inverse} \( F^{-1}: B \multto A \) of a set-valued map \( F: A \multto B \) as the function whose graph is the inverse of \( \gr(F) \) in the sense of \cref{def:binary_relation/inverse}.

    If \( F \) is single-valued and \hyperref[def:functor_invertibility/bijective]{bijective}, so is its inverse \( F^{-1} \).

    For each subset \( Y \subseteq B \), we call \( F^{-1}(Y) \) the \term[ru=прообраз (\cite[33]{ШеньВерещагин2020НачалаТеорииМножеств}),en=preimage (\cite[def. 1.16]{Rudin1991FunctionalAnalysis})]{preimage} of \( Y \).

    \thmitem{def:set_valued_function/composition}\mcite[def. II.2.6]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets} We define the \term{composition} of two set-valued functions \( F: A \multto B \) and \( G: B \multto C \) by composing their graphs as per \cref{def:binary_relation/composition}\fnote{The square brackets around \( G \bincirc F \) are not a special notation, but rather another pair of delimiters that looks different from parentheses for the sake of reducing visual clutter.}:
    \begin{equation*}
      \begin{aligned}
        &G \bincirc F: A \to C \\
        &[G \bincirc F](x) \coloneqq G(F(x)).
      \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}

    As shown in \cref{thm:def:function/composition}, the composition of single-valued functions is again single-valued.

    \thmitem{def:set_valued_function/restriction}\mcite[58]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика} Set-valued functions have a general notion of \term[en=restriction (\cite[34]{AubinFrankowska1990SetValuedAnalysis}), ru=ограничение]{restriction} inherited from \hyperref[def:relation/restriction]{that of relations}.

    Namely, given a function \( F: A \multto B \) and subsets \( X \subseteq A \) and \( Y \subseteq B \), we can define the function \( F\restr_{X,Y}: X \to Y \) with graph \( \gr F \cap (X \times Y) \).

    If \( F \) is a single-valued function, in order for \( F_\restr{X,Y} \) to also be single-valued, \( Y \) must be a superset of the image.

    We usually only restrict the domain, in which case we write \( F\restr_X: X \to B \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item This definition is an amalgamation of notions defined by different authors at different levels of generality. See \cref{rem:function_definition}.

  The precise definition of \( F: A \multto B \) is used in \cite[def. II.3.2]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets}, where it is called a \enquote{correspondence}, however the terminology and notation more closely resembles \cite[def. 1.3.1]{AubinFrankowska1990SetValuedAnalysis} and \cite[1]{DontchevRockafellar2014SolutionMappings}.

  \item Since set-valued functions are often conflated with their graphs, many of the above definitions can be stated for binary relations, as it is done in \cite[8]{Kelley1975GeneralTopology}.
\end{comments}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:notation_for_function_image}
  In \cref{def:set_valued_function/image}, we have denoted the image of a function \( F: A \to B \) at \( X \subseteq A \) by \( F[X] \). For single-valued functions, the notation is used by \textcite[11]{Kelley1975GeneralTopology}, while the related notation \( F\Bracks{X} \) is used by \textcite[44]{Enderton1977SetTheory}.

  An alternative is to use \( F(X) \), which is unfortunately ambiguous if \( X \) is a \hyperref[def:transitive_set]{transitive set}: if \( A \) contains both \( x \) and \( \set{ x } \) (e.g. if \( A \) is an \hyperref[def:ordinal]{ordinal}), then
  \begin{equation*}
    F[\set{ x }] = \set{ F(x) } \T{may not equal} F(\set{ x }).
  \end{equation*}

  Outside set theory, \( F(X) \) is the dominant notation, introduced for single-valued functions in
  \cite[31]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory},
  \cite[1]{Engelking1989GeneralTopology},
  \cite[4]{Willard2004GeneralTopology},
  \cite[def. 2.2]{Rudin1976AnalysisPrinciples},
  \cite[3]{Folland1999RealAnalysis},
  \cite[12]{WheedenZygmund2015MeasureAndIntegral},
  \cite[\S 1.2.5]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ},
  \cite[46]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика} and
  \cite[33]{ШеньВерещагин2020НачалаТеорииМножеств}.

  \Textcite[def. II.2.3]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets} use \( F(X) \) and \( F\braket{ X } \).

  Yet another alternative is to denote the value \( F(x) \) by \( F'x \) and the image \( F[X] \) by \( F^\dprime X \). For single-valued functions, this can be found in set-theoretic literature:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \Textcite[21]{Jech2006SetTheory} and \textcite[def. I.6.12]{Kunen2013SetTheory} uses \( F(X) \) and \( F^\dprime X \).
    \item \Textcite[def. 6.2.10]{Hinman2005Logic} uses \( F^* X \).
    \item \Textcite[245]{Mendelson2015Logic} uses \( F^\dprime X \).
  \end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:def:set_valued_function}
  \hyperref[def:set_valued_function]{Set-valued functions} have the following basic properties:

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:def:set_valued_function/associative} \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/composition]{Composition} is associative: for any three functions \( F: A \to B \), \( G: B \to C \) and \( H: C \to D \) we have
    \begin{equation*}
      H \bincirc [G \bincirc F] = [H \bincirc G] \bincirc F.
    \end{equation*}

    We will henceforth simply write \( H \bincirc G \bincirc F \).

    \thmitem{thm:def:set_valued_function/identity} \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/identity]{Identities} act as \hyperref[def:semigroup_neutral_element]{neutral elements}: for every function \( F: A \to B \), we have
    \begin{equation*}
      F \bincirc \id_A = \id_B \bincirc F = F.
    \end{equation*}

    \thmitem{thm:def:set_valued_function/composition_inverse} Inverses are contravariant: for any two functions \( F: A \to B \) and \( G: B \to C \), we have
    \begin{equation*}
      [G \bincirc F]^{-1} = F^{-1} \bincirc G^{-1}.
    \end{equation*}
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:set_valued_function/associative} Fix some \( a \in A \) and \( d \in D \).

  In order for \( d \) to belong to \( [[H \bincirc G] \bincirc F](a) \), there must exist values \( b \in B \) and \( c \in C \) such that \( b \in F(a) \) and \( c \in G(b) \) and \( d \in H(c) \).

  Clearly this is also the condition for \( d \) to belong to \( [H \bincirc [G \bincirc F]](a) \).

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:set_valued_function/identity} Trivial.

  \SubProofOf{thm:def:set_valued_function/composition_inverse} Since \( G \bincirc F \) has signature \( A \to C \), clearly \( [G \bincirc F]^{-1} \) has signature \( C \to A \). Let \( c \in C \).

  \begin{itemize}
    \item If \( [G \bincirc F]^{-1}(c) \) is empty, \( c \notin \img(G \bincirc F) \), hence either \( G^{-1}(c) \) is empty or is nonempty, but disjoint from \( \img(F) \). Hence, \( [F^{-1} \bincirc G^{-1}](c) \) is also empty.

    \item Suppose that \( [G \bincirc F]^{-1}(c) \) is not empty and let \( a \in [G \bincirc F]^{-1}(c) \).

    By definition, there exists \( b \in B \) and such that \( b \in F(a) \) and \( c \in G(b) \). Hence, \( b \in G^{-1}(c) \) and \( a \in F^{-1}(b) \), which implies that the image of \( c \) under the composition \( F^{-1} \bincirc G^{-1} \) also contains \( a \).
  \end{itemize}

  In both cases, for every \( c \in C \) we have
  \begin{equation*}
    [G \bincirc F]^{-1}(c) = [F^{-1} \bincirc G^{-1}](c).
  \end{equation*}

  Hence, the two set-valued maps are equal.
\end{proof}

\begin{concept}\label{con:function_arguments}
  As mentioned in \cref{def:set_valued_function/value}, given a map \( F: A \multto B \) and a free variable \( x \), we sometimes use the notation \( F(x) \) rather than \( F \).

  If \( A = A_1 \times \cdots \times A_n \) is a \hyperref[def:cartesian_product]{finite Cartesian product}, we can instead use the notation \( F(x_1, \ldots, x_n) \) and regard \( x_1, \ldots, x_n \) as free variables that have no assigned value.

  We call these variables \term[ru=аргументы (\( n \)-местной операции) (\cite[def. 2.1]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика}), en=arguments (of an \( n \)-ary operation) (\cite[xxii]{Mendelson2015Logic})]{arguments}. This notions is well-defined in some cases like \hyperref[def:operation_on_set]{\( n \)-ary operatons}, but may be ambiguous in general since \( A \) can often be represented as a Cartesian product in different ways and with different arities. For example, if \( A = B \times C \) and \( C = D \times E \), we can write both \( f(b, c) \) and \( f(b, d, e) \). In practice, the number of arguments is usually clear from the context. We sometimes use \( \vect{a} \) to highlight that we regard \( a \) as a tuple.

  If the number of arguments is well-defined, we call it the \term{arity} of the function and use prefixes from \cref{def:operation_arity_terminology} like \enquote{unary}, \enquote{binary} and so forth.
\end{concept}

\paragraph{Single-valued functions}

\begin{definition}\label{def:function}\mcite[def. II.3.9]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets}
  If a \hyperref[def:set_valued_function]{set-valued function} \( F: A \multto B \) is \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/value_cardinality/single]{single-valued} at every point of \( A \), we say that it is a \term[ru=однозначная функция (\cite[12]{Курош1973ОбщаяАлгебра}), en=single-valuedness (\cite[5]{Jacobson1985BasicAlgebraI})]{single-valued function}, a \term[en=total function (\cite[def. 2.3.13]{Rosen2019DiscreteMathematics})]{total function} or simply a \term[ru=функция (\cite[\S 1.2.4]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ})]{function}.

  We write write \( F: A \to B \) rather than \( F: A \multto B \).

  All concepts from \cref{def:set_valued_function} apply to single-valued functions, however we treat the value \( F(x) \) as an element of \( B \) rather than as a singleton subset of \( B \).
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item We can simplify \cref{def:set_valued_function/image} to
  \begin{equation*}
    f[A]
    =
    \bigcup \set[\big]{ \set{ f(x) } \given x \in A }
    =
    \set{ f(x) \given x \in A }.
  \end{equation*}

  \item By convention, when both single-valued and set-valued maps are involved, the former are denoted using lowercase letters and the latter using uppercase letters.
\end{comments}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:set_valued_map_as_single_valued}
  As discussed in \cref{rem:function_definition}, rather than defining \hyperref[def:function]{single-valued functions} as a special case of \hyperref[def:set_valued_function]{set-valued functions}, some authors define set-valued functions from \( A \) to \( B \) as single-valued functions from \( A \) to the power set \( \pow(B) \).

  The downside of the latter approach is that notions such as the \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/image]{image}, \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/codomain]{codomain} and \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/inverse]{inverse} of the set-valued map have a different and less useful for us meaning.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}\label{def:partial_function}\mcite[def. 2.3.13]{Rosen2019DiscreteMathematics}
  If a \hyperref[def:set_valued_function]{set-valued function} \( F: A \multto B \) is either \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/value_cardinality/empty]{empty-valued} or \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/value_cardinality/single]{single-valued} at every point of \( A \), we say that it is a \term[ru=частичное отображение (\cite[35]{Мальцев1970АлгебраическиеСистемы})]{partial function}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:red_herring_principle}\mcite{nLab:red_herring_principle}
  The \term{mathematical red herring principle} states that a \enquote{red herring} need not, in general, be red or a herring.
\end{remark}
\begin{comments}
  \item This principle is barely related to the eponymous informal logical fallacy, stated in \cite[\S 3.2.8]{Hurley2023LogicIntroduction} as follows:
  \begin{displayquote}
    The red herring fallacy is committed when the arguer diverts the attention of the reader or listener by changing the subject to a different but sometimes subtly related one. He or she then finishes by either drawing a conclusion about this different issue or by merely presuming that some conclusion has been established. By so doing, the arguer purports to have won the argument. The fallacy gets its name from a procedure used to train hunting dogs to follow a scent. A red herring (or bag of them) is dragged across the trail with the aim of leading the dogs astray. Since red herrings have an especially potent scent (caused in part by the smoking process used to preserve them), only the best dogs will follow the original scent.
  \end{displayquote}
\end{comments}

\begin{example}\label{ex:rem:red_herring_principle}
  We list examples of the \hyperref[rem:red_herring_principle]{red herring principle}:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{ex:rem:red_herring_principle/function} The principle mostly applies to generalizations, like \hyperref[def:set_valued_function]{set-valued functions} and \hyperref[def:partial_function]{partial functions} generalize \hyperref[def:function]{functions}.

    \thmitem{ex:rem:red_herring_principle/contravariant_functor} More interestingly, a \hyperref[def:contravariant_functor]{contravariant functor} from \( \cat{C} \) to \( \cat{D} \) is a \hyperref[def:functor]{functor} from \( \cat{C}^{\oppos} \) to \( \cat{D} \).
  \end{thmenum}
\end{example}

\begin{concept}\label{con:undefinedness}\mimprovised
  Consider the (real-valued) \hyperref[def:derived_trigonometric_functions/tan]{tangent function} \( \tan(x) \). We can regard it as \hyperref[def:function]{single-valued function} on the set \( \BbbR \setminus \set{ (2k + 1) \pi / 2 \given k \in \BbbZ } \), or as a \hyperref[def:partial_function]{partial} function on the entire real line \( \BbbR \).

  The second approach is often more convenient. Yet, it requires a new notion --- called \term[ru=неопределённость (\cite[62]{ШеньВерещагин2020НачалаТеорииМножеств}), en=undefinedness (\cite[284]{Farmer2008STTVirtues})]{undefinedness} --- used to express the fact that \( \tan(\pi / 2) \) becomes a \hyperref[con:expression]{syntactic expression} without meaningful \hyperref[con:syntax_semantics_duality]{semantics}. We say that \( \tan(\pi / 2) \) is undefined.

  Having such a notion available in the \hyperref[con:metalanguage]{metalanguage} can simplify case-wise definitions. For example, consider the characterization of the \hyperref[def:polynomial_degree]{degree} of a univariate polynomials over the real numbers from \cref{rem:zero_polynomial_degree/minus_one}:
  \begin{equation*}
    \deg(f) = \begin{cases}
      \min\set{ k = 0, 1, \ldots \given D^{k+1} f = 0 }, &f \neq 0, \\
      \T{undefined},                                     &\T{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
  \end{equation*}
\end{concept}
\begin{comments}
  \item Some authors use the \hyperref[def:propositional_alphabet/constants/falsum]{falsum symbol} \( \bot \) for denoting undefined values. For example, \textcite[99]{Savage2008ModelsOfComputation} when discussing \hyperref[def:turing_machine]{Turing machines}, or \textcite[62]{ШеньВерещагин2020НачалаТеорииМножеств} when discussing set theory.

  \textcite[\S 3.2]{Farmer2008STTVirtues} suggests a dedicated constant \( \bot_\tau \) as an undefined value of type \( \tau \) in his presentation of \hyperref[def:higher_order_logic]{higher-order logic}. This is done specifically to provide well-behaved semantics for the \hyperref[con:description_operator/iota]{definite description operator}.

  \item \Textcite[475]{Farmer2004UndefinednessInCalculus} describes undefinedness as follows:
  \begin{displayquote}
    A mathematical term is \textit{undeﬁned} if it has no prescribed meaning or if it denotes a value that does not exist.
  \end{displayquote}

  \item Ill-formed syntactic expressions are necessarily undefined (i.e. have no meaningful semantics), but not vice versa. The examples above demonstrate that allowing undefinedness can be less daunting than restricting the syntax of the metalanguage to expressions that we consider meaningful.

  \item Some programming languages handle undefinedness using the Maybe monad --- see \cref{ex:def:monad/maybe}.

  \item \hyperref[con:primitive_notion]{Primitive notions} are also sometimes called \enquote{undefined} --- see \cref{rem:undefined_and_primitive_terms}.
\end{comments}

\begin{definition}\label{def:set_valued_selection}\mcite[52]{DontchevRockafellar2014SolutionMappings}
  If \( f: A \to B \) is a single-valued function, \( F: A \multto B \) is a set-valued map and \( \gr(f) \subseteq \gr(F) \), we say that \( f \) is a \term{selection} of \( F \).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:def:function}
  \hyperref[def:function]{Single-valued functions} have the following properties when regarded as \hyperref[def:function]{set-valued maps}:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:def:function/total} Single-valued functions are \hyperref[def:partial_function]{total} as set-valued functions.

    \thmitem{thm:def:function/selection_total} If a set-valued function \( F: A \multto B \) has a \hyperref[def:set_valued_selection]{selection}, then \( F \) itself is \hyperref[def:partial_function]{total}.

    \thmitem{thm:def:function/composition} The \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/composition]{composition} of single-valued functions is a single-valued function.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:function/total} Trivial.
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:function/selection_total} Trivial.
  \SubProofOf{thm:def:function/composition} Trivial.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:constant_function}\mcite{Social:empty_constant_function}
  If the \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/image]{image} of a \hyperref[def:function]{single-valued function} \( f: A \to B \) is some \hyperref[def:singleton_set]{singleton set} \( \set{ b } \), we call it a \term[bg=тъждествено преобразование (\cite[151]{Станилов1974АналитичнаГеометрия}), ru=тождественное отображение (\cite[46]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика})]{constant function} with value \( b \).
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:constant_function}
  The definition for constant function we give in \cref{def:constant_function} avoids a common pitfall.

  Consider the following definition, based on Walter Rudin's one in \cite[def. 4.3]{Rudin1976AnalysisPrinciples}:
  \begin{displayquote}
    If a \hyperref[def:function]{single-valued function} \( f: A \to B \) satisfies \( f(a) = b \) for every \( a \in A \), we call it a \term{constant function} with value \( b \).
  \end{displayquote}

  Under the latter definition, any function \( f: \varnothing \to B \) from an empty set is vacuously a constant function taking every possible value in \( B \). This is discussed in \cite{Social:empty_constant_function}.

  On the other hand, under the former definition, constant functions are precisely those that that \hyperref[def:factors_through]{factor through} \enquote{the} \hyperref[thm:universal_sets]{terminal set} \( \set{ \varnothing } \).

  Hence, our definition also gives a good categorical characterization.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Function invertibility}

\begin{definition}\label{def:function_invertibility}\mcite[def. II.3.10]{Bourbaki2004TheoryOfSets}
  A \hyperref[def:function]{single-valued function} \( f: A \to B \) has a well-defined \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/inverse]{set-valued inverse} \( f^{-1}: B \multto A \). We are interested in how conditions on \( f \) affect \( f^{-1} \).
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:function_invertibility/injective} We say that \( f \) is \term[bg=инективно (изображение) (\cite[150]{Станилов1974АналитичнаГеометрия}), ru=инъективное (отображение) (\cite[41]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика})]{injective} if \( f(x) = f(y) \) implies \( x = y \).

    Equivalently, \( f^{-1} \) must either be empty-valued or single-valued at every point of \( B \).

    \thmitem{def:function_invertibility/surjective} We say that \( f \) is \term[bg=сюрективно (изображение) (\cite[150]{Станилов1974АналитичнаГеометрия}), ru=сюръективное (отображение) (\cite[42]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика})]{surjective} if the image \( f[A] \) coincides with the codomain \( B \).

    Equivalently, \( f^{-1} \) must either be single-valued or multi-valued at every point of \( B \).

    \thmitem{def:function_invertibility/bijective} Finally, we say that \( f \) is \term[bg=биективно (изображение) (\cite[150]{Станилов1974АналитичнаГеометрия}), ru=биективное (отображение) (\cite[42]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика})]{bijective} if it is both injective and surjective.

    Equivalently, \( f^{-1} \) must be a total single-valued function.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item \Cref{thm:function_invertibility_categorical} characterizes these notions via \hyperref[def:morphism_cancellation]{morphism cancellation} and \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility]{morphism invertibility} in the category \hyperref[def:category_of_small_sets]{category of small sets}.

  \item There is also another popular terminology, that can be found, for example, in \cite[31; 32]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory} --- if \( f: A \to B \) is injective, it is said to be \enquote{one-to-one}, and if it is surjective, it is said to be \enquote{onto \( B \)}. In Russian, \textcite[15]{Мальцев1970АлгебраическиеСистемы} translates the latter as \enquote{на \( B \)}.
\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:function_composition_invertibility}
  The composition of injective (resp. surjective or bijective) functions is injective (resp. surjective or bijective).
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item A similar result holds for \hyperref[def:category/morphisms]{categorical morphisms} --- see \cref{thm:def:morphism_invertibility/composition}.

  \item A related but more limited result holds for \hyperref[def:function_superposition]{function superposition} --- see \cref{thm:function_superposition_invertibility}.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  Let \( f: A \to B \) and \( g: B \to C \) be arbitrary functions and let \( h \coloneqq g \circ f \).

  \SubProofOf[def:function_invertibility/injective]{injectivity} Suppose that \( f \) and \( g \) are injective.

  Suppose that \( h(x_1) = h(x_2) \), that is, \( g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) \). Then \( f(x_1) = f(x_2) \) since \( g \) is injective, and \( x_1 = x_2 \) since \( f \) is injective.

  Since \( x_1 \) and \( x_2 \) were arbitrary, we conclude that \( h \) is also injective.

  \SubProofOf[def:function_invertibility/surjective]{surjectivity} Suppose that \( f \) and \( g \) are surjective.

  Fix some \( z \) from \( C \). There exists some \( y \) in \( B \) such that \( g(y) = z \) and similarly there exists some \( x \) in \( B \) such that \( f(x) = y \). Thus, \( h(x) = g(f(x)) = z \).

  Since \( z \) was arbitrary, we conclude that \( h \) is also surjective.

  \SubProofOf[def:function_invertibility/bijective]{bijectivity} We have shown that if \( f \) and \( g \) are both injective or surjective, so is \( h \). Hence, if \( f \) and \( g \) are bijective, so is \( h \).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{thm:diagonal_product_injectivity}
  Let \( \seq{ f_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \) be an indexed family of functions, where \( f_k: A \to B_k \) for every index \( k \) in \( \mscrK \). If at least one of the functions is injective, the \hyperref[def:topological_product]{diagonal product}
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
      &f: A \to \prod B_k \\
      &f(x) \coloneqq \seq{ f_k(x) }_{k \in \mscrK}
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}
  is also injective.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Suppose that \( f_{k_0} \) is injective. Let \( f(x_1) = f(x_2) \). Then \( f_{k_0}(x_1) = f_{k_0}(x_2) \), so we can conclude that \( x_1 = x_2 \). Since \( x_1 \) and \( x_2 \) were arbitrary, we conclude that \( f \) itself is injective.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:function_superposition}\mcite[395]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика}
  We define the \term[ru=суперпозиция, en=superposition (\cite[168]{Lorentz1966ApproximationOfFunctions})]{superposition} of the family of \hyperref[def:set_valued_function]{set-valued functions} \( G: B_1 \times \cdots \times B_n \multto C \) and \( F_k: A \multto B_k \), \( k = 1, \ldots, n \), as
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
      &H: A \to C, \\
      &H(x) \coloneqq G(F_1(x), \ldots, F_n(x)).
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Applied to single-valued functions, this term can mostly be found in Russophone literature, for example
  \cite[395]{БелоусовТкачёв2004ДискретнаяМатематика},
  \cite[279]{Герасимов2014Вычислимость} and
  \cite[\S 25]{Фихтенгольц1968ОсновыАнализаТом2}.

  It is also used for ordinary composition, for example in
  \cite[\S 1.2.6]{КусраевКутателадзе2005БулевозначныйАнализ} and
  \cite[37]{Кострикин2020АлгебраЧасть1}.

  Mention of superposition in Anglophone literature is scarce. It can be found, for example, in \cite[168]{Lorentz1966ApproximationOfFunctions}, and, in the context of \hyperref[def:primitive_recursion]{primitive recursion}, as \enquote{generalized composition} in \cite[291]{Andrews2002Logic}.
\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:function_superposition_invertibility}
  Any superposition of injective functions is injective.
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Compare this result to the more powerful statement on ordinary composition --- \cref{thm:function_composition_invertibility}.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  Suppose that we are given injective functions \( f_k: A \multto B_k \) for \( k = 1, \ldots, n \) and \( g: B_1 \times \cdots \times B_n \multto C \). From \cref{thm:diagonal_product_injectivity} it follows that the diagonal product
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
      &d: A \to B_1 \times \cdots \times B_n, \\
      &d(x) \coloneqq (f_1(x), \ldots, f_n(x))
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}
  is injective.

  Then from \cref{thm:function_composition_invertibility} it follows that the desired superposition
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
      &h: A \to C, \\
      &h(x) \coloneqq \underbrace{g(f_1(x), \ldots, f_n(x))}_{g(d(x))}
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}
  is injective.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:function_image_preimage_composition}
  Composing any function \( f: A \to B \) with its (set-valued) inverse \( f^{-1}: B \multto A \) gives the following inequalities:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:function_image_preimage_composition/preimage_of_image} If \( X \subseteq A \), then \( X \subseteq f^{-1}[f[X]] \). Equality holds if \( f \) is injective.

    \thmitem{thm:function_image_preimage_composition/image_of_preimage} If \( Y \subseteq B \), then \( f[f^{-1}[Y]] \subseteq Y \). Equality holds if \( f \) is surjective.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:function_image_preimage_composition/preimage_of_image} Every \( x \) in \( X \) clearly belongs to \( f^{-1}(f(x)) \). Thus,
  \begin{equation*}
    X \subseteq f^{-1}[f[X]].
  \end{equation*}

  Now suppose that \( f \) is injective and fix some \( x \) from \( f^{-1}[f[X]] \). There exists some \( y \) in \( f[X] \) such that \( x \) in \( f^{-1}(y) \) and some \( z \) in \( X \) such that \( y = f(z) \). Since \( f \) is injective and \( f(x) = y = f(z) \), it follows that \( x = z \) and thus \( x \) belongs to \( X \). Since \( x \) was chosen arbitrarily from \( f^{-1}[f[X]] \), we conclude that
  \begin{equation*}
    f^{-1}[f[X]] \subseteq X.
  \end{equation*}

  \SubProofOf{thm:function_image_preimage_composition/image_of_preimage} If \( y \) belongs to \( f[f^{-1}[Y]] \), there exists some \( x \) in \( f^{-1}[Y] \) such that \( f(x) = y \). Furthermore, there also exists some \( t \) in \( Y \) such that \( x \) is in the preimage \( f^{-1}(t) \). Hence, \( y = f(x) = t \) and \( y \) belongs to \( Y \). Therefore,
  \begin{equation*}
    f[f^{-1}[Y]] \subseteq Y.
  \end{equation*}

  Now suppose that \( f \) is surjective and fix some \( y \) from \( Y \). Then from surjectivity if follows that there exists some \( x \) in \( X \) such that \( f(x) = y \). Hence, \( x \) is in \( f^{-1}(y) \) and \( y = f(x) \) is in \( f[f^{-1}(y)] \). Since \( y \) was chosen arbitrarily from \( Y \), we conclude that
  \begin{equation*}
    Y \subseteq f[f^{-1}[Y]].
  \end{equation*}
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:function_image_properties}
  \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/image]{Images of sets} under \( f: A \to B \) have the following basic properties:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:function_image_properties/monotonicity} If \( X_1 \subseteq X_2 \), then \( f[X_1] \subseteq f[X_2] \).

    \thmitem{thm:function_image_properties/union} For any \hyperref[def:indexed_family]{indexed family} \( \seq{ X_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \subseteq A \) of subsets of \( A \), we have the equality
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:function_image_properties/union}
      f\bracks*{ \bigcup_{k \in \mscrK} X_k } = \bigcup_{k \in \mscrK} f[X_k].
    \end{equation}

    \thmitem{thm:function_image_properties/intersection} For any indexed family \( \seq{ X_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \) of subsets of \( A \), we have the inclusion
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:function_image_properties/intersection}
      f\bracks*{ \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} X_k } \subseteq \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} f[X_k].
    \end{equation}

    Equality holds if \( f \) is \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/injective]{injective}. Otherwise, if both \( A \) and \( B \) are nonempty and \( f[X_1] = f[X_2] = B \), then
    \begin{equation*}
      f[X_1 \cap X_2] = f[\varnothing] = \varnothing \subsetneq f[X_1] \cap f[X_2] = B.
    \end{equation*}

    \thmitem{thm:function_image_properties/difference} For any two subsets \( X_1 \) and \( X_2 \) of \( A \), we have the inclusion
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:function_image_properties/difference}
      f[X_1] \setminus f[X_2] \subseteq f[X_1 \setminus X_2].
    \end{equation}

    Equality holds if \( f \) is injective. Otherwise, if \( X_1 \subsetneq X_2 \) but \( f[X_1] = f[X_2] \), we have
    \begin{equation*}
      f[X_1] \setminus f[X_2] = \varnothing \subsetneq f[X_1 \setminus X_2].
    \end{equation*}
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Compare this result to the more well-behaved properties of \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/inverse]{preimages} described in \cref{thm:function_preimage_properties}.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:function_image_properties/monotonicity} If \( x \in X_1 \), then \( x \in X_2 \) and hence \( f(x) \in f[X_2] \). Therefore, \( f[X_1] \subseteq f[X_2] \).

  \SubProofOf{thm:function_image_properties/union} If \( x_0 \in X_{k_0} \) for some \( k_0 \in \mscrK \), clearly
  \begin{equation*}
    f(x_0) \in f[X_{k_0}] \subseteq \bigcup_{k \in \mscrK} f[X_k].
  \end{equation*}

  Therefore,
  \begin{equation*}
    f\bracks*{ \bigcup_{k \in \mscrK} X_k } \subseteq \bigcup_{k \in \mscrK} f[X_k].
  \end{equation*}

  Conversely, if \( y_0 \in f[X_{k_0}] \) for some \( k_0 \in \mscrK \), by \cref{thm:function_image_properties/monotonicity} obviously
  \begin{equation*}
    y_0 \in f\bracks*{ \bigcup_{k \in \mscrK} X_k }.
  \end{equation*}

  Therefore,
  \begin{equation*}
    f\bracks*{ \bigcup_{k \in \mscrK} X_k } \supseteq \bigcup_{k \in \mscrK} f[X_k].
  \end{equation*}

  Hence, \eqref{eq:thm:function_image_properties/union} holds.

  \SubProofOf{thm:function_image_properties/intersection} If \( x_0 \) belongs to \( \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} X_k \), then \( x_0 \) belongs to \( X_k \) for all \( k \in \mscrK \). It follows that \( f(x_0) \) belongs to \( f[X_k] \) for all \( k \in \mscrK \) and hence to their intersection. Therefore, the inclusion \eqref{eq:thm:function_image_properties/intersection} holds.

  Now suppose that \( f \) is injective. Let \( y_0 \) be a point in the intersection \( \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} f[X_k] \). We thus have \( y_0 \in f[X_k] \) for all \( k \in \mscrK \). Since \( f \) is injective, for each \( k \in \mscrK \) there exists a unique \( x_k \in X_k \) such that \( f(x_k) = y_0 \). Again because of injectivity of \( f \), all these elements are equal because \( f(x_k) = f(x_m) = y_0 \) for \( k, m \in \mscrK \). Hence,
  \begin{equation*}
    y_0 \in f\bracks*{ \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} X_k }.
  \end{equation*}

  Therefore, the reverse inclusion in \eqref{eq:thm:function_image_properties/intersection} holds if \( f \) is injective.

  \SubProofOf{thm:function_image_properties/difference} If \( f[X_1] \setminus f[X_2] \) is empty, \eqref{eq:thm:function_image_properties/difference} obviously holds. Suppose that it is nonempty and let \( y_0 \in f[X_1] \setminus f[X_2] \).

  Then there exists a point \( x_0 \in X_1 \) such that \( f(x_0) = y_0 \). It cannot be that \( x_0 \in X_2 \) because otherwise \( y_0 = f(x_0) \in f[X_2] \), which would contradict our choice of \( y_0 \). Hence, \( x_0 \in X_1 \setminus X_2 \) and \( y_0 \in f(X_1 \setminus X_2) \).

  Since \( y_0 \) was chosen arbitrarily, we conclude that the inclusion \eqref{eq:thm:function_image_properties/difference} holds.

  For the converse, suppose that \( f \) is injective. If \( f(X_1 \setminus X_2) \) is empty, by \eqref{eq:thm:function_image_properties/difference} the set \( f[X_1] \setminus f[X_2] \) is also empty and the converse holds.

  Now suppose that it is nonempty and let \( y_0 \in f(X_1 \setminus X_2) \). Then there exists a point \( x_0 \in X_1 \setminus X_2 \) such that \( f(x_0) = y_0 \). Furthermore, since \( f \) is injective, \( x_0 \) is the only preimage of \( y_0 \) and hence \( f(x_0) \in f[X_1] \setminus f[X_2] \), which proves the reverse inclusion in \eqref{eq:thm:function_image_properties/difference}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:function_preimage_properties}
  \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/inverse]{Preimages of sets} under \( f: A \to B \) have the following basic properties:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:function_preimage_properties/monotonicity} If \( Y_1 \subseteq Y_2 \), then \( f^{-1}[Y_1] \subseteq f^{-1}[Y_2] \).

    \thmitem{thm:function_preimage_properties/union} For any \hyperref[def:indexed_family]{indexed family} \( \seq{ Y_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \subseteq B \) of subsets of \( B \), we have the equality
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:function_preimage_properties/union}
      f^{-1}\bracks*{ \bigcup_{k \in \mscrK} Y_k } = \bigcup_{k \in \mscrK} f^{-1}[Y_k].
    \end{equation}

    \thmitem{thm:function_preimage_properties/intersection} For any indexed family \( \seq{ Y_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \) of subsets of \( B \), we have the equality
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:function_preimage_properties/intersection}
      f^{-1}\bracks*{ \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} Y_k } = \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} f^{-1}[Y_k].
    \end{equation}

    \thmitem{thm:function_preimage_properties/difference} For any two subsets \( Y_1 \) and \( Y_2 \) of \( B \), we have the equality
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:function_preimage_properties/difference}
      f^{-1}[Y_1] \setminus f^{-1}[Y_2] = f^{-1}[Y_1 \setminus Y_2].
    \end{equation}
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Compare this result to the less well-behaved properties of images described in \cref{thm:function_image_properties}.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  \SubProofOf{thm:function_image_properties/monotonicity} Analogous to \cref{thm:function_image_properties/monotonicity}.

  \SubProofOf{thm:function_image_properties/union} Analogous to \cref{thm:function_image_properties/union}.

  \SubProofOf{thm:function_image_properties/intersection} If \( y_0 \) belongs to \( \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} Y_k \), then \( y_0 \) belongs to \( Y_k \) for all \( k \in \mscrK \). It follows that \( f(y_0) \subseteq f^{-1}[Y_k] \) for all \( k \in \mscrK \) and hence it is also a subset of their intersection. Therefore,
  \begin{equation*}
    f^{-1} \bracks*{ \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} Y_k } \subseteq \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} f^{-1}[Y_k].
  \end{equation*}

  Conversely, if \( x_0 \in \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} f^{-1}[Y_k] \), it belongs to \( f^{-1}[Y_k] \) for all \( k \in \mscrK \). Clearly then \( f(x_0) \in Y_k \) for all \( k \in \mscrK \) and thus \( f(x_0) \in \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} Y_k \). Hence, by \cref{thm:function_preimage_properties/monotonicity} we have
  \begin{equation*}
    f^{-1}(f(x_0))
    \subseteq
    f^{-1}\bracks*{ \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} Y_k }.
  \end{equation*}

  Since \( x_0 \in f^{-1}(f(x_0)) \),
  \begin{equation*}
    x_0 \in f^{-1}\bracks*{ \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} Y_k }.
  \end{equation*}

  Since \( x_0 \) was chosen arbitrarily from \( \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} f^{-1}[Y_k] \), we can conclude that
  \begin{equation*}
    \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} f^{-1}[Y_k] \in f^{-1}\bracks*{ \bigcap_{k \in \mscrK} Y_k }.
  \end{equation*}

  Hence, \eqref{eq:thm:function_preimage_properties/intersection} holds.

  \SubProofOf{thm:function_preimage_properties/difference} If \( y_0 \in Y_1 \setminus Y_2 \), there exists a point \( x_1 \in Y_1 \) such that \( f(x_1) = y_0 \). Aiming at a contradiction, suppose that there exists a point \( x_2 \in f^{-1}[Y_2] \) such that \( f(x_2) = y_0 \). Then \( y_0 = f(x_1) = f(x_2) \) implies that \( f^{-1}(y_0) \subseteq f^{-1}[Y_1] \cap f^{-1}[Y_2] \). \Cref{thm:function_preimage_properties/intersection} then in turn implies that \( f^{-1}(y_0) \subseteq f^{-1}[Y_1 \cap Y_2] \) and hence by \cref{thm:function_image_properties/monotonicity}
  \begin{equation*}
    y_0 = f(f^{-1}(y_0)) \in f[f^{-1}[Y_1 \cap Y_2]] = Y_1 \cap Y_2,
  \end{equation*}
  which contradicts our choice of \( y_0 \). Since the choice of \( y_0 \in Y_1 \setminus Y_2 \), \( x_1 \in f^{-1}(y_0) \cap Y_1 \) and \( x_2 \in f^{-1}(y_0) \cap Y_2 \) was arbitrary, the obtained contradiction shows that
  \begin{equation*}
    f^{-1}(Y_1 \setminus Y_2) \subseteq f^{-1}[Y_1] \setminus f^{-1}[Y_2].
  \end{equation*}

  Conversely, we have
  \begin{equation*}
    f(f^{-1}[Y_1] \setminus f^{-1}[Y_2])
    \reloset {\eqref{eq:thm:function_image_properties/difference}} \subseteq
    f(f^{-1}(Y_1 \setminus Y_2))
    \reloset {\ref{thm:function_image_preimage_composition/image_of_preimage}} \subseteq
    Y_1 \setminus Y_2.
  \end{equation*}

  Hence,
  \begin{equation*}
    f^{-1}[Y_1] \setminus f^{-1}[Y_2]
    \reloset {\ref{thm:function_image_preimage_composition/preimage_of_image}} \subseteq
    f^{-1}\parens[\big]{ f\parens[\big]{ f^{-1}[Y_1] \setminus f^{-1}[Y_2] } }
    \reloset {\eqref{thm:function_preimage_properties/monotonicity}} \subseteq
    f^{-1}(Y_1 \setminus Y_2).
  \end{equation*}
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{thm:empty_function_invertibility}
  An \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/empty]{empty} (single-valued) \hyperref[def:function]{function} is always \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/injective]{injective} and \hyperref[def:morphism_cancellation/left]{left cancellable}.

  Its codomain is empty if and only if it is \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/surjective]{surjective}, \hyperref[def:morphism_cancellation/right]{right cancellable}, \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/left]{left invertible} or \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility/right]{right invertible}.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Fix an empty function \( g: B \to C \). Clearly \( B = \varnothing \).

  \SubProofOf[def:function_invertibility/injective]{injectivity} Vacuous.

  \SubProofOf[def:function_invertibility/surjective]{surjectivity}

  \SufficiencySubProof* If \( C \) is empty, then \( g \) is vacuously surjective.

  \NecessitySubProof* If \( g \) is surjective, the preimage \( g^{-1}(c) \) of every \( c \) in \( C \) belongs to the empty set \( B \), hence \( C \) must also be empty.

  \SubProofOf[def:morphism_cancellation/left]{left cancellation} Any function \( f: A \to B \) into \( B \) is empty because \( B \) is. Thus, all functions that can be composed with \( g \) on the right are empty, meaning that \( g \) is left cancellative.

  \SubProofOf[def:morphism_cancellation/left]{right cancellation}

  \SufficiencySubProof* If \( C \) is empty, then any function \( h: C \to D \) that can be composed with \( g \) on the left must also be empty, hence \( g \) is right cancellative.

  \NecessitySubProof* Conversely, suppose that \( g \) is right cancellative.

  Consider the functions \( h_1, h_2: C \to \set{ 1, 2 } \) sending all elements to \( 1 \) and \( 2 \), respectively. Then \( h_1 \bincirc g = h_2 \bincirc g \) since both compositions are empty, and, since \( g \) is right cancellable, we have \( h_1 = h_2 \).

  If \( C \) is nonempty, and \( c \) is any element of \( C \), then \( 1 = h_1(c) = h_2(c) = 2 \). The obtained contradiction shows that \( C \) must be empty.

  \SubProofOf[def:morphism_invertibility/left]{left invertibility}

  \SufficiencySubProof* If \( C \) is empty, then \( g: \varnothing \to \varnothing \) is its own inverse.

  \NecessitySubProof* If \( f: C \to B \) is a left inverse of \( g: B \to C \), it must be empty because \( B \) is. In this case \( C \) must also be empty.

  \SubProofOf[def:morphism_invertibility/left]{right invertibility} Analogous.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:function_cancellation_categorical}
  In relation to \hyperref[def:morphism_cancellation]{morphism cancellation} in the \hyperref[def:category_of_small_sets]{category of small sets}, we have the following:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:function_cancellation_categorical/left} A function is a monomorphism (left cancellable) if and only if it is \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/injective]{injective}.

    \thmitem{thm:function_cancellation_categorical/right} A function is an epimorphism (right cancellable) if and only if it is \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/surjective]{surjective}.

    Sufficiency here depends on \fullref{thm:existence_of_single_valued_selections}, which is equivalent to the \hyperref[def:zfc/choice]{axiom of choice} in \hyperref[def:zfc]{\logic{ZF}}.

    \thmitem{thm:function_cancellation_categorical/bimorphism} A function is a bimorphism if and only if it is \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/bijective]{bijective}.

    Unlike the above, this equivalence does not depend on the axiom of choice.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item We prove this result here rather than in \fullref{sec:functions} because it requires \fullref{thm:surjective_functions_are_right_invertible}, which requires the axiom of choice that we have introduced in this section.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  Fix function \( g: B \to C \). We have already handled the cases where \( g \) is empty in \cref{thm:empty_function_invertibility}, so suppose that it is nonempty.

  \SubProofOf{thm:function_cancellation_categorical/left}

  \SufficiencySubProof* Suppose that \( g \) is a left cancellable and let \( g(y_1) = g(y_2) \).

  Let \( f: B \to B \) be \( \id_B: B \to B \) modified as to exchange \( y_1 \) and \( y_2 \). Then
  \begin{equation*}
    g(f(y_2)) = g(\id_B(y_1)) = g(\id_B(y_2)) = g(f(y_1))
  \end{equation*}
  and \( g(f(y)) = g(\id_B(y)) \) for all other \( y \) in \( B \).

  Since \( g \) is left cancellable, it follows that \( f = \id_B \). In particular, \( y_1 = y_2 \), which demonstrates that \( g \) is injective.

  \NecessitySubProof* Conversely, suppose that \( g \) is injective and let \( g \bincirc f_1 = g \bincirc f_2 \) for some \( f_1, f_2: A \to B \).

  From \( g(f_1(x)) = g(f_2(x)) \) it follows that \( f_1(x) = f_2(x) \), so, generalizing on \( x \), we conclude that \( f_1 = f_2 \). Thus, \( g \) is left invertible.

  \SubProofOf{thm:function_cancellation_categorical/right}

  \SufficiencySubProof* Suppose that \( g \) is right cancellable. Aiming at a contradiction, suppose that it is not surjective.

  Let \( \widetilde h: g[B] \to B \) be a \hyperref[def:set_valued_selection]{single-valued selection} of the (restriction of) \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/inverse]{inverse} \( g^{-1}\restr_{g[B]} \). Such a selection exists by \fullref{thm:existence_of_single_valued_selections}.

  Since \( g \) is nonempty, its image is nonempty. Fix elements \( z_1 \) from \( g[B] \) and \( z_2 \) from \( C \setminus g[B] \). Let \( f_1: C \to B \) and \( f_2: C \to B \) be extensions of \( \widetilde h \) that send all elements outside \( g[B] \) to \( z_1 \) and \( z_2 \), correspondingly.

  Clearly \( f_1 \bincirc g = f_2 \bincirc g \). Since \( g \) is right cancellable, this implies that \( f_1 = f_2 \), and in particular \( z_1 = z_2 \). But we have picked \( z_1 \) and \( z_2 \) from disjoint sets.

  The obtained contradiction shows that \( g \) must be surjective.

  \NecessitySubProof* Conversely, suppose that \( g \) is surjective and let \( h_1 \bincirc g = h_2 \bincirc g \) for some functions \( h_1: C \to D \) and \( h_2: C \to D \).

  For any \( z \) in \( C \), there exists \( y \) be an element of \( B \) such that \( g(y) = z \). Then
  \begin{equation*}
    h_1(z) = h_1(g(y)) = h_2(g(y)) = h_2(z).
  \end{equation*}

  We conclude that \( h_1 = h_2 \), hence \( g \) is right cancellative.

  \SubProofOf{thm:function_cancellation_categorical/bimorphism}

  \SufficiencySubProof* Suppose that \( g \) is both left and right cancellable.

  Then \cref{thm:function_cancellation_categorical/left} implies that \( g \) is injective, and the set-valued inverse \( g^{-1}: C \multto B \) becomes a single-valued function when restricted to \( g[B] \). This gives us the single-valued selection that we need in \cref{thm:function_cancellation_categorical/right}, so using \fullref{thm:existence_of_single_valued_selections} becomes unnecessary.

  Therefore, \( g \) is both injective and surjective irrespective of the axiom of choice.

  \NecessitySubProof* If \( g \) is bijective, \cref{thm:function_cancellation_categorical/left} and \cref{thm:function_cancellation_categorical/right} imply that \( g \) is left and right cancellable, i.e. a bimorphism.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:function_invertibility_categorical}
  In relation to \hyperref[def:morphism_invertibility]{morphism invertibility} in the \hyperref[def:category_of_small_sets]{category of small sets}, we have the following:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:function_invertibility_categorical/left} A \emph{\hyperref[def:set_valued_function/empty]{nonempty}} function is left invertible if and only if it is \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/injective]{injective}.

    An \emph{empty} function is left invertible if and only if it is both injective and has an empty codomain.

    \thmitem{thm:function_invertibility_categorical/right} A function is right invertible if and only if it is \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/surjective]{surjective}.

    Necessity here depends on \fullref{thm:surjective_functions_are_right_invertible}, which is equivalent to the \hyperref[def:zfc/choice]{axiom of choice} in \hyperref[def:zfc]{\logic{ZF}}.

    \thmitem{thm:function_invertibility_categorical/isomorphism} A function is fully invertible (i.e. an isomorphism) if and only if it is \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/bijective]{bijective}.

    Unlike the above, this equivalence does not depend on the axiom of choice.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Fix function \( f: A \to B \). We have already handled the cases where \( f \) is empty in \cref{thm:empty_function_invertibility}, so suppose that it is nonempty.

  \SubProofOf{thm:function_invertibility_categorical/left}

  \SufficiencySubProof* Let \( g: B \to A \) be a left inverse of \( f: A \to B \).

  If \( f(x_1) = f(x_2) \), then \( x_1 = g(f(x_1)) = g(f(x_2)) = x_2 \), implying that \( f \) is injective.

  \NecessitySubProof* Conversely, suppose that \( f \) is injective.

  Its set-valued inverse \( f^{-1}: B \multto A \) is a partial function. Fix any value \( b_0 \) from \( B \) and define
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
      &g: B \to A \\
      &g(y) \coloneqq \begin{cases}
        f^{-1}(y), &y \in f[A], \\
        b_0,       &\T{otherwise.}
      \end{cases}
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}

  By construction, \( g \) is a left inverse of \( f \).

  \SubProofOf{thm:function_invertibility_categorical/right}

  \SufficiencySubProof* Let \( g: B \to A \) be a right inverse of \( f: A \to B \).

  For any \( y \) in \( B \), \( f(g(y)) = y \), so \( g(y) \) is a preimage of \( y \) under \( f \).

  Therefore, \( f \) is surjective.

  \NecessitySubProof* Follows from \fullref{thm:surjective_functions_are_right_invertible}.

  \SubProofOf{thm:function_invertibility_categorical/isomorphism}

  \SufficiencySubProof* If \( f \) is an isomorphism, \cref{thm:function_invertibility_categorical/left} and \cref{thm:function_invertibility_categorical/right} imply that \( f \) is both injective and surjective, i.e. bijective.

  \NecessitySubProof* Suppose that \( f \) is bijective. Then \( f^{-1} \) is trivially verified to be both a left and right inverse.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{thm:category_of_sets_is_balanced}
  The \hyperref[def:category_of_small_sets]{category of small sets} is \hyperref[def:balanced_category]{balanced}.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  \Cref{thm:function_cancellation_categorical/bimorphism} implies that bimorphisms are bijective, and \cref{thm:function_invertibility_categorical/isomorphism} implies that bijective functions are isomorphisms.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:cartesian_product_projection}\mcite[11]{Архангельский1988КанторовскаяТеорияМножеств}
  Consider the \hyperref[def:cartesian_product]{Cartesian product} \( \prod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \). For each index \( m \) in \( \mscrK \), we define the \term[ru=оператор проектирования, en=projection (\cite[36]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory})]{projection map}
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:def:cartesian_product_projection}
    \begin{aligned}
      &\pi_m: \prod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \to A_m, \\
      &\pi_m\parens[\big]{ \seq{ a_k }_{k \in \mscrK} } \coloneqq a_m.
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item In the \hyperref[def:basis_decomposition]{coordinate space} \( R^n \), we refer to \( \pi_m \) as a \enquote{coordinate projection}.
\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:cartesian_product_projection_surjective}
  In a nonempty \hyperref[def:cartesian_product]{Cartesian product}, the \hyperref[def:cartesian_product_projection]{projection maps} are \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/surjective]{surjective}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Consider the product \( \prod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \). We have assumed that it is nonempty. Let \( \seq{ a_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \) be a member.

  Then, for every \( k \) in \( \mscrK \) and every \( b \) in \( A_k \), we can replace \( a_k \) with \( b \) in \( \seq{ a_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \) to obtain a member of the product whose \( k \)-th coordinate is \( b \). Thus, \( \pi_k \) is surjective.

  Generalizing on \( k \), we conclude that all projection maps are surjective.
\end{proof}

\begin{comments}
  \item We show in \cref{rem:definition_via_product_universal_property} how the universal property of products allows recovering a morphism from its projections.

\begin{comments}
  \item Since, for every \( a \) in \( A \), we have

    f(a) = \seq{ f_k(a) }_{k \in \mscrK},
  \end{equation*}
  we can conflate the function \( f \) with the tuple \( \seq{ f_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \).
\end{comments}

\begin{definition}\label{def:set_enumeration}\mimprovised
  An \term[en=enumeration (\cite[190]{Kleene2002Logic})]{enumeration} of the set \( A \) is an \hyperref[def:ordered_tuple]{ordered tuple} or \hyperref[def:sequence]{infinite sequence} (possibly \hyperref[def:transfinite_sequence]{transfinite}) over \( A \) that is bijective as a function.
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item We generalize enumerations to multisets in \cref{def:multiset_enumeration}.
\end{comments}

\paragraph{Choice functions}

\begin{definition}\label{def:choice_function}\mcite[def. 6.4.1]{Hinman2005Logic}
  Let \( \mscrA \) be a (possibly empty) family of nonempty sets. A \term[ru=функция выбора (\cite[136]{Герасимов2014Вычислимость})]{choice function} on \( \mscrA \) is a \hyperref[def:function]{function} \( c: \mscrA \to \bigcup \mscrA \) such that, for every set \( A \) in \( \mscrA \), \( f(A) \) belongs to \( A \).
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item A choice function on \( \mscrA \) \enquote{chooses} an element out of each member of \( \mscrA \). We sometimes have a canonical choice function. For example, as shown in \cref{thm:truncated_polynomial_algebra/isomorphism}, in a suitable setting the polynomial remainder \( \rem(f, g) \) is a \hyperref[def:quotient_polynomial_algebra_as_truncated]{principal representative} in every coset \( [f] \) of the quotient algebra \( R[X] / \braket{ g(X) } \).

  In general, however, the existence of a choice function is not by any means obvious.

  \item Furthermore, even the existence of a choice function for a concrete family may be difficult to prove. We guarantee its existence via the \hyperref[def:zfc/choice]{axiom of choice} --- the \logic{C} in \logic{ZFC}.

  \item Unlike Cartesian products, which are commonly defined for indexed families of sets, choice functions are commonly defined for non-indexed families. This is the definition we will find convenient in practice, and the one used by
  \textcite[def. 6.4.1]{Hinman2005Logic},
  \textcite[59; 60]{Halmos1960NaiveSetTheory},
  \textcite[thm. 6M(3)]{Enderton1977SetTheory},
  \textcite[def. 0.3.4]{Harzheim2005OrderedSets} and
  \textcite[136]{Герасимов2014Вычислимость},
  among others.
\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:cartesian_product_via_choice_functions}
  Fix an \hyperref[def:indexed_family]{indexed family} \( \seq{ A_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \) of sets, and consider its corresponding non-indexed family
  \begin{equation*}
    \mscrA \coloneqq \set{ A_k \given k \in \mscrK}.
  \end{equation*}

  Then the set
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:cartesian_product_via_choice_functions}
    \bigsqcap_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \coloneqq \set{ f \given f \T{is a \hyperref[def:choice_function]{choice function} for} \mscrA },
  \end{equation}
  with projection maps
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:cartesian_product_via_choice_functions/projection}
    \begin{aligned}
      &\pi'_m: \bigsqcap_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \to A_m, \\
      &\pi'_m(f) \coloneqq f(A_m).
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  satisfies \fullref{thm:cartesian_product_universal_property} --- it is a \hyperref[def:categorical_product]{categorical product} in \( \hyperref[def:category_of_small_sets]{\cat{Set}} \).
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item The only formal distinction between \( \bigsqcap_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \) as defined here and \( \prod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \) as defined in \cref{def:cartesian_product} is that choice functions have attached information about their domain and codomain. Thus, taking the \hyperref[def:set_valued_function/graph]{graph} of the choice functions instead, we obtain \( \prod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \).

  Following the practice from \cref{rem:categorical_limit_uniqueness} of not distinguishing between different instances of the same categorical limit (as long as it is used consistently), we will regard elements of Cartesian products as choice functions in the cases where we will find that convenient.

  In particular, we only use the notation \( \bigsqcap \) in this particular proposition, and use the established \( \prod \) symbol generally.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  As per our comment above, taking the function graphs of the choice functions, we obtain the product \( \prod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \), for which we have already shown \fullref{thm:cartesian_product_universal_property}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Set of all functions}

\begin{definition}\label{def:set_of_all_functions}\mimprovised
  We will use several notations for the set of all \hyperref[def:function]{single-valued functions} from \( A \) to \( B \), similarly to how we, depending on the situation, use both \( \exp(z) \) and \( e^z \) for the \hyperref[def:exponential_function]{exponential function}.

  Generally, in analogy with \hyperref[def:category/morphisms]{categorical \( \hom \)-sets}, we will denote the set of functions by \( \fun(A, B) \).

  Occasionally, like when dealing with \hyperref[def:coordinate_space]{coordinate spaces} like \( R^n \) or state assignment in \hyperref[def:cellular_automaton]{cellular automata} like \( E^{D_0} \), we will find it more convenient to use the succinct notation \( B^A \). The notation is justified by \fullref{thm:cardinality_exponentiation_rule}. This usage mostly coincides with the cases where we treat functions like \hyperref[def:indexed_family]{indexed families}.

  Finally, in some situations like \hyperref[con:free_construction/evaluation]{evaluation homomorphisms}, we find it clearest to combine the two notations into \( \fun(M^\mscrX, M) \) rather than \( \fun(\fun(\mscrX, M), M) \) or \( M^{M^\mscrX} \).
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item Usage of notation in the literature is discussed in \cref{rem:set_of_all_functions_notation}.

  \item As shown in \cref{thm:set_of_all_functions_via_cartesian_product}, \( B^A \) can be viewed as a degenerate case of the \hyperref[def:cartesian_product]{Cartesian product}.
\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:set_of_all_functions_via_cartesian_product}
  There is a bijective correspondence between the \hyperref[def:set_of_all_functions]{set of all functions} \( B^A \) and the degenerate \hyperref[def:cartesian_product]{Cartesian product} \( \prod_{a \in A} B \).
\end{proposition}
\begin{comments}
  \item A similar result holds for function types and dependent product types; see \cref{con:dependent_type}.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  Every function \( f: A \to B \) defines a family \( \seq{ f(a) }_{a \in A} \) of elements of \( B \). This family is obviously injective, and it is surjective because the family \( \seq{ b_a }_{a \in A} \) defines a function \( f(a) \coloneqq b_a \).
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Disjoint unions}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:disjoint_unions}
  It is often the case that we wish to have several \enquote{copies} of the same set --- for example in the construction of abstract syntax trees of \hyperref[def:propositional_formula_ast]{propositional formulas} or \hyperref[def:lambda_term_ast]{\( \muplambda \)-terms}, or for defining \hyperref[def:cardinal_arithmetic/addition]{cardinal addition}.

  Given a \enquote{prototype} set \( A \), there are distinct ways to construct two disjoint copies \( B \) and \( C \) that are equivalent to \( A \) --- in the sense that they are in a bijective correspondence\fnote{In the terminology of \fullref{sec:cardinals}, \( A \), \( B \) and \( C \) are \hyperref[def:equinumerosity]{equinumerous}}. The union \( B \cup C \) then consists of two \enquote{copies} of \( A \).

  More generally, given an \hyperref[def:indexed_family]{indexed family} \( \seq{ A_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \) of sets (which may even all be equal), we are interested in a family \( \seq{ B_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \) in which the sets are disjoint and there is a \enquote{canonical} bijective inclusion function \( \iota_k: A_k \to B_k \) for every index \( k \) in \( \mscrK \).

  We do not usually care about the particular construction. In accordance with \cref{rem:universal_property_as_definition}, we will provide a \enquote{canonical} disjoint union definition in \cref{def:disjoint_union}, and, after proving \fullref{thm:disjoint_union_universal_property}, only rely on the canonical inclusion maps.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}\label{def:disjoint_union}\mcite[19]{Хелемский2014ФункциональныйАнализ}
  We define the \term[ru=дизъюнктное произведение, en=disjoint union (\cite[8]{Aluffi2009Algebra})]{disjoint union} of the \hyperref[def:indexed_family]{indexed family} \( \seq{ A_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \) of nonempty sets as
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:def:disjoint_union}
    \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \coloneqq \set[\big]{ (a, k) \given k \in \mscrK \T{and} a \in A_k }.
  \end{equation}

  We associate with each \( k \in \mscrK \) the inclusion
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
      &\iota_k: A_k \to \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k, \\
      &\iota_k(a) \coloneqq (a, k).
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}
\end{definition}
\begin{comments}
  \item As discussed in \cref{rem:disjoint_unions}, outside this definition and \fullref{thm:disjoint_union_universal_property}, we prefer working with the inclusion \( \iota_k(x) \) rather than with the concrete representation \( (a, k) \).
\end{comments}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:disjoint_product_inclusions_injective}
  \hyperref[def:disjoint_union]{Disjoint union inclusion maps} are \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/injective]{injective}.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Consider the union \( \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \).

  For a fixed index \( k \) in \( \mscrK \), if \( \iota_k(a) = \iota_k(b) \), then \( (a, k) = (b, k) \) and \cref{thm:indexed_family_extensionality} implies that \( a = b \). Therefore, \( \iota_k \) is injective.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:ordered_tuple_via_disjoint_union}
  The \hyperref[def:disjoint_union]{disjoint union} \( \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \) satisfies a variant of \cref{thm:indexed_family_extensionality}:
  \begin{equation*}
    \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k = \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} B_k \T{if and only if} A_k = B_k \T{for every} k \T{in} \mscrK.
  \end{equation*}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  \SufficiencySubProof Suppose that \( \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k = \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} B_k \).

  An element \( a_k \) of \( A_k \) embeds into \( \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \) as the pair \( (k, a_k) \). This element also belongs to \( \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} B_k \). \Cref{thm:indexed_family_extensionality} implies that \( (i, x) = (j, y) \) if and only if \( i = j \) and \( x = y \). With \( i = k \) and \( x = a_k \), we can conclude that \( (k, a_k) \) is an embedding of \( a_k \) from \( B_k \), i.e. \( a_k \) belongs to \( B_k \).

  This demonstrates that \( A_k \subseteq B_k \). We can similarly show that \( B_k \subseteq A_k \). Generalizing on \( k \), we conclude that \( A_k = B_k \) for every \( k \) in \( \mscrK \).

  \NecessitySubProof Suppose that \( A_k = B_k \) for every \( k \) in \( \mscrK \).

  Every element of \( \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \) is by construction either \( (k, a_k) \) for some \( a_k \) in \( A_k = B_k \), and hence belongs to \( \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} B_k \). The converse is similarly obvious.

  We thus conclude that \( \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k = \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} B_k \).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{thm:cartesian_product_via_disjoint_union}
  Fix an \hyperref[def:indexed_family]{indexed family} \( \seq{ \mscrA_k }_{k \in \mscrK} \) of sets, and consider the set
  \begin{equation*}
    \mscrA \coloneqq \set*{ \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k \given A_k \in \mscrK }.
  \end{equation*}

  With the projection maps
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
      &\pi'_m: \mscrA \to \mscrA_m, \\
      &\pi'_m\parens*{ \coprod_{k \in \mscrK} A_k } \coloneqq A_m.
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}
  satisfies \fullref{thm:cartesian_product_universal_property}.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  As noted in \fullref{thm:cartesian_product_universal_property}, the proof only depends on \cref{thm:indexed_family_extensionality}, which we emulate in \cref{thm:ordered_tuple_via_disjoint_union}.
\end{proof}

    l_X(\iota_k(a_m)) = \beta_k(a_m).

\paragraph{Factorization}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:function_induces_equivalence}
  For any \hyperref[def:function]{function} \( f: A \to B \), the \hyperref[def:relation]{relation}
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:thm:function_induces_equivalence}
    a \sim b \T{if and only if} f(a) = f(b).
  \end{equation}
  is an \hyperref[def:equivalence_relation]{equivalence} on \( A \).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Trivial.
\end{proof}

  Then there exists a unique \hyperref[def:function_invertibility/bijective]{bijective function} \( \widetilde{f}: A / {\cong} \to A[X] \) such that the following diagram commutes:
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
      \includegraphics[page=1]{output/thm__function_factorization}
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation*}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  For any \( a \) in \( A \), the function \( \widetilde{f} \) must satisfy
  \begin{equation*}
    \widetilde{f}([a]) = f(a).
  \end{equation*}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:categorical_image_and_coimage}
  We take \fullref{thm:function_factorization} from \cite[thm. I.2.7]{Aluffi2009Algebra}, where Aluffi introduces no special name for \( A / {\sim} \). One candidate is \enquote{coimage}, based on a similar notion for module homomorphisms.

  Most generally, based on categorical \hyperref[def:categorical_kernels]{kernels and cokernels}, \textcite[def. IX.1.15]{Aluffi2009Algebra} defines the \term{image} of a morphism \( f: A \to B \) as \( \img f \coloneqq \ker \co\ker f \) and the \term{coimage} \( \co\img f \coloneqq \co\ker \ker f \).

  These definitions are stated in Abelian categories (such as the \hyperref[def:module/category]{categories of modules}), where kernels and cokernels are required to exist for every morphism. With the additional restrictions placed on Abelian categories, the categorical image of \( f \) coincides with the set-theoretic image \( f[A] \) --- see \cref{thm:module_categorical_kernels}. More generally, however, this may fail --- for example, in a category of small groups, \cref{thm:kernel_is_normal_subgroup} shows that kernels are normal subgroups, so \( \ker \co\ker f \) must be normal. Yet, \( f[A] \) is in general not normal --- see \cref{ex:image_of_normal_subgroup} --- and so \( \ker \co\ker f \) is the \hyperref[def:normal_closure]{normal closure} of \( f[A] \).

  For these reasons, we avoid defining coimages more generally.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{\( \omega \) recursion}

\begin{theorem}[Recursion theorem]\label{thm:omega_recursion}\mcite[73]{Enderton1977SetTheory}
  Let \( A \) be a nonempty set. Suppose that we are given some member \( a_0 \) of \( A \) and some transformation \( T: A \to A \). Then there exists a \hyperref[def:sequence]{sequence} \( f: \omega \to A \) such that
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \( f(n) = a_0 \).
    \item For every \( n \in \omega \), we have \( f(\op{sc}(n)) = T(f(n)) \).
  \end{itemize}
\end{theorem}
\begin{comments}
  \item Note that we do not yet use the notation \( n + 1 \) because we will use this theorem to define addition.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  Let \( G \subseteq \pow(\omega \times A) \) be the set of all \hyperref[def:partial_function]{partial single-valued functions} \( g: \omega \to A \) such that
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If \( g \) is defined at \( \varnothing \), then \( g(\varnothing) = a_0 \).
    \item For every \( n \in \omega \), if \( g \) is defined at \( \op{sc}(n) \), then \( g \) is also defined at \( n \) and
    \begin{equation*}
      g(\op{sc}(n)) = T(f(n)).
    \end{equation*}
  \end{itemize}

  Clearly \( G \) is nonempty because the function \( \set{ (\varnothing, a_0) } \) belongs to \( G \).

  The conditions imposed on the functions in \( G \) ensure that every function is defined in some \hyperref[def:order_interval/unbounded]{initial segment} of the natural numbers. A more obvious approach is to require \( g \) to be defined at \( \op{sc}(n) \) if it is defined at \( n \), however that would hinder our induction schema.

  Define \( f \coloneqq \bigcup G \). At this point \( f \) is simply some \hyperref[def:function]{set-valued map}. We must now show that \( f \) has all the properties that we want.

  \SubProofOf[def:partial_function]{totality} First, we will use \fullref{thm:omega_induction} to show that \( f \) is total. Clearly \( \varnothing \in \dom f \).

  Now fix \( n \in \dom f \). Then there exists a function \( g \in G \) defined at \( n \).

  \begin{itemize}
    \item If \( g \) is also defined at \( \op{sc}(n) \), this directly proves that \( \op{sc}(n) \in \dom f \).
    \item If \( g \) is not defined at \( \op{sc}(n) \), consider
    \begin{equation*}
      \widehat g \coloneqq g \cup \set{ (\op{sc}(n), T(g(n)) }.
    \end{equation*}

    The function \( \widehat g \) is again a single-valued partial function, and thus it belongs to \( G \), hence \( \op{sc}(n) \in \dom f \).
  \end{itemize}

  Therefore, \fullref{thm:omega_induction} allows us to conclude \( f: \omega \multto A \) is a total set-valued map.

  \SubProofOf[def:function]{single-valuedness} Now that we know that \( f \) is total, we will prove that it is single-valued and thus is a function in the usual sense of the term.

  Clearly \( f \) is single-valued at \( \varnothing \).

  Now suppose that \( f \) is single-valued at \( n \). Since \( f \) is total, there exist at least one partial function in \( G \) that is defined at \( \op{sc}(n) \), from which it follows that it is also defined at \( n \).  Let \( g \) and \( h \) both be such (single-valued partial) functions.

  Then
  \begin{equation*}
    g(\op{sc}(n)) = T(g(n)) = T(f(n)) = T(h(n)) = h(\op{sc}(n)),
  \end{equation*}
  hence \( g \) and \( h \) coincide at \( \op{sc}(n) \), which in turn implies that \( f \) is single-valued at \( \op{sc}(n) \).

  Therefore, \fullref{thm:omega_induction} allows us to conclude that \( f \) is a single-valued total function.

  \SubProofOf[def:function]{uniqueness} Now that it is clear that \( f \) satisfies the theorem, we must verify that it is unique.

  Suppose that \( f_1 \) and \( f_2 \) both satisfy the theorem. Clearly \( \varnothing \in H \). Fix some \( n \neq \varnothing \) and suppose that \( f_1(n) = f_2(n) \). Then
  \begin{equation*}
    f_2(\op{sc}(n)) = T(f_1(n)) = T(f_2(n)) = f_2(\op{sc}(n)).
  \end{equation*}

  Therefore, \fullref{thm:omega_induction} allows us to conclude that \( f_1 = f_2 \). So there is at most one function that satisfies the theorem, and we have already shown that \( f \) is such a function.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}\label{def:omega_operations}\mimprovised
  We will use \fullref{thm:omega_recursion} for defining arithmetic operations for natural numbers. These constructions will be more elaborate than the basic recursive sequences like those defined in \fullref{thm:banach_fixed_point_theorem}.

  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{def:omega_operations/addition} We will represent the addition operation \( \oplus \) as follows:

    \begin{itemize}
      \item Fix the first summand \( n \). We will now construct a function \( \oplus_n: \omega \to \omega \) such that \( \oplus_n(m) = n \oplus m \). This is a particular instance of \hyperref[con:currying]{currying}.

      In the context of \fullref{thm:omega_recursion}, let \( a_0^{(n)} = n \) and
      \begin{equation*}
        \begin{aligned}
          &T^{(n)}: \omega \to \omega, \\
          &T^{(n)}(k) \coloneqq \op{sc}(k).
        \end{aligned}
      \end{equation*}

      Recursion then gives us a function \( \oplus_n: \omega \to \omega \).

      \item We can now define the full binary addition function \( \oplus: \omega \times \omega \to \omega \) via its graph
      \begin{equation*}
        \set[\big]{ \parens[\big]{ (n, m), \oplus_n(m) } \given* n, m \in \omega }.
      \end{equation*}
    \end{itemize}

    \thmitem{def:omega_operations/multiplication} We define natural number multiplication analogously. For each \( n \in \omega \), recursively define \( \odot_n \) via
    \begin{equation*}
      \begin{aligned}
        &T^{(n)}: \omega \to \omega, \\
        &T^{(n)}(k) \coloneqq k \oplus n.
      \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}
    and \( a_0^{(n)} = 0 \) and then define \( \odot: \omega \times \omega \to \omega \) via its graph
    \begin{equation*}
      \set[\big]{ \parens[\big]{ (n, m), \odot_n(m) } \given* n, m \in \omega }.
    \end{equation*}
  \end{thmenum}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[\( \omega \) is a model of \( \logic{PA}_2 \)]\label{thm:omega_is_model_of_pa}
  The \hyperref[def:smallest_inductive_set]{smallest inductive set} \( \omega \) is a model of \hyperref[def:peano_arithmetic]{Peano arithmetic} with the following interpretation:
  \begin{thmenum}
    \thmitem{thm:omega_is_model_of_pa/zero} \hyperref[def:peano_arithmetic/signature/zero]{Zero} is interpreted as \( \varnothing \).

    \thmitem{thm:omega_is_model_of_pa/succ} The \hyperref[def:peano_arithmetic/signature/succ]{successor} operation \( s \) is interpreted as \( \op{sc} \).

    \thmitem{thm:omega_is_model_of_pa/plus} \hyperref[def:peano_arithmetic/signature/plus]{Addition} is interpreted by the \( \oplus \) function given in \cref{def:omega_operations/addition}.

    \thmitem{thm:omega_is_model_of_pa/mult} Similarly, \hyperref[def:peano_arithmetic/signature/times]{multiplication} is interpreted by \( \odot \) from \cref{def:omega_operations/multiplication}.
  \end{thmenum}
\end{theorem}
\begin{comments}
  \item This is an extension of \cref{thm:omega_is_model_of_pa_without_operations}.
\end{comments}
\begin{proof}
  We have already shown in \cref{thm:omega_is_model_of_pa_without_operations} that \( \omega \) satisfies the axioms \eqref{eq:def:peano_arithmetic/theory/PA1}-\eqref{eq:def:peano_arithmetic/theory/PA3}. It remains to show that is satisfies \eqref{eq:def:peano_arithmetic/theory/PA4}-\eqref{eq:def:peano_arithmetic/theory/PA7}.

  \SubProofOf{eq:def:peano_arithmetic/theory/PA4} For each \( n \in \omega \) the starting condition (i.e. \( m = \varnothing \)) in \cref{def:omega_operations/addition} implies that \( n \oplus \varnothing = n \).

  \SubProofOf{eq:def:peano_arithmetic/theory/PA5} For each \( n \in \omega \) the transformation \( T_n \) in \cref{def:omega_operations/addition} is defined so that \( \oplus_n(m) = k \) implies
  \begin{equation*}
    \oplus_n(\op{sc}(m)) = \op{sc}(k).
  \end{equation*}

  It follows that, for all \( n, m \in \omega \),
  \begin{equation*}
    n \oplus \op{sc}(m) = \op{sc}(n \oplus m).
  \end{equation*}

  \SubProofOf{eq:def:peano_arithmetic/theory/PA6} For each \( n \in \omega \), the starting condition in \cref{def:omega_operations/multiplication} implies that \( n \odot \varnothing = \varnothing \).

  \SubProofOf{eq:def:peano_arithmetic/theory/PA7} For each \( n \in \omega \), the transformation \( T_n \) in \cref{def:omega_operations/multiplication} is defined so that \( \odot_n(m) = k \) implies
  \begin{equation*}
    \odot_n(\op{sc}(m)) = k \oplus n
  \end{equation*}

  It follows that, for all \( n, m \in \omega \),
  \begin{equation*}
    n \odot \op{sc}(m) = \op{sc}(n \oplus m).
  \end{equation*}
\end{proof}
